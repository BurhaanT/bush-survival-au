# Writing the guide in plain language

**Status:** Book-wide writing rule, requested by the founder on 6 September 2026.  
**Applies to:** all book text, quick-reference cards, instructions, captions, diagrams, tables, labels and index entries. Also applies to any draft intended for reader testing.

This rule applies to existing material as well as new writing. Review old sections when editing them and before moving them into the book. Simplify project explanations and research summaries where it helps, but preserve exact source titles, quotations, scientific names, official labels and technical details needed to check the evidence. Never change a quoted source silently. Archived decisions remain a record of what was decided at the time.

## The reader

Write for a person with no survival training. They may be tired, frightened, injured, cold, distracted or reading in poor light. They should be able to find the next action and understand it on the first reading.

Use respectful adult language. Explain concepts without talking down to the reader. Never assume that a familiar word, a picture or a short sentence makes an instruction understood by everyone. Check understanding with people who have not seen the draft.

## Words and sentences

- Use everyday words wherever they carry the full meaning.
- Keep sentences short enough to follow easily. Split a sentence that contains several decisions or actions.
- Name the action and the object: what should the reader do, and to what?
- Address the reader as “you”. When helping someone else, use “the person”.
- Use active verbs: “check”, “cover”, “call”, “hold”, “look” and “wait”. Avoid phrases such as “undertake an assessment” or “implement a strategy”.
- Explain a necessary technical term where it first appears. A reader opening the book halfway through must not need to search for its meaning.
- Keep official warning names and equipment labels accurate so the reader can match them to what they see.
- Spell out an abbreviation before using it. Repeat its meaning on a separate emergency card when needed.
- Use the same name for the same item throughout. Do not alternate between “container”, “reservoir” and “vessel” merely for variety.
- Prefer “do not” in critical warnings. Avoid double negatives and wording that relies on a reader noticing a small exception.
- Replace vague words such as “appropriate”, “adequate”, “assess”, “monitor” or “safe” with observable checks where the evidence allows. If the check cannot be explained reliably, keep the action out of the approved guide until that gap is resolved.

## Order of information

Begin with the reader's immediate problem. Use headings the reader might look for, such as “You can see smoke” or “Your phone will not connect”, once the linked guidance is approved.

For a practical technique, use the following order where relevant:

1. **When to use this:** the situation and conditions that must be met.
2. **Do not use this if:** the important limits, beside the action they affect.
3. **What you need:** the specific items and quantities supported by the evidence.
4. **What to do:** numbered steps in the order they must happen, usually one action per step.
5. **How to check it:** what the reader can see, feel or otherwise check without creating another hazard.
6. **If it does not work:** the next supported action and when to stop.

Give a brief reason when it helps the reader make the decision or avoid a mistake. Longer explanations follow the urgent steps. Do not make someone read a history lesson or technical explanation before reaching the action.

A condition must appear before or beside the action it controls. Do not put an essential exception in a later paragraph, footnote or another chapter. Where two people must act at the same time, say who does what; do not force a simultaneous task into a misleading single-person sequence.

## Preserve the safety meaning

Making a sentence simpler must preserve its conditions, timing, quantity, uncertainty and exceptions. Never change “may reduce harm” to “will keep you safe”. A shorter instruction fails if it invites the wrong action.

Each rewrite keeps the same content ID and supporting sources. If a rewrite changes the action or its meaning, return it to the relevant reviewer. Changes after page layout need the same check, because a heading, crop or page break can change what a reader understands.

Explain uncertainty in terms of the decision it affects. Avoid a general paragraph of caution that leaves the reader guessing what to do. Research notes may retain specialist terms for precise source comparison, but that wording must be translated before it becomes book text.

## Warnings and evidence confidence

Before uncertain survival-critical advice, put a visible warning box that names the outstanding review or exact uncertainty, explains the evidence confidence and states the practical limitation. Repeat it at a subsection when the limitation is more specific than the chapter's opening warning. Do not put a controlling exception after the action.

Use **High**, **Moderate**, **Low** or **Insufficient** with an explanation, as defined in the book's opening chapter. These labels describe agreement with evidence and remaining application limits, not a survival probability or independent approval. For consequential material awaiting authority review, also follow the founder's request for an **editorial percentage estimate of wording accuracy** when the source comparison supports one. Call it subjective and uncalibrated. Never express it as the chance of survival, safety, correct plant identification, ignition, treatment success or another real-world outcome. If an outcome cannot be estimated, say so. A warning does not justify including an unsupported dangerous procedure.

## First aid with and without a kit

Use the headings **With a first-aid kit** and **Without a first-aid kit**. Name the actual item needed; “comprehensive kit” does not mean that every reader has it.

Keep the same shared priorities above both paths. Describe what can be done when supplies are missing and what cannot be replaced. Both versions must be checked by the medical reviewer. Plain language must not hide a medicine's name, dose, age limit, device difference or reason not to use it.

## Numbers, pictures and page links

- Use consistent metric units and make the measured item clear.
- Give a number only when supported and useful; do not add precise-looking estimates for reassurance.
- Label picture parts with the same words used in the steps.
- State the viewing direction if left/right or front/back could be confused.
- Explain what arrows and symbols mean. Do not rely on colour alone.
- Use captions to explain what to notice or do.
- Use descriptive page links, such as “See Getting help”, with page numbers added during PDF production. Avoid “see above” or a reference that becomes meaningless after a page break.
- Keep full research detail in the source notes while preserving traceable citations. Instructions must make sense without opening a source link during an emergency.

## Examples of editing style

These examples illustrate language only. They do not approve any survival action.

| Research-style wording | Reader wording |
|---|---|
| Select guidance according to the incident category rather than the preceding activity. | Find the section for the problem you face now. |
| Record the provenance and temporal currency of the warning. | Write down who issued the warning and when it was issued. |
| Product nomenclature must remain consistent across text and illustrations. | Use the same item name in the words and the picture. |
| In the absence of the specified equipment, consult the alternative pathway. | If you do not have the listed item, read “Without a first-aid kit”. |

## Check each page

The editor checks that the next action is easy to find, unfamiliar terms are explained and warnings remain beside the relevant step. A reader then explains the page in their own words and, where safe, shows what they would do with harmless props.

Ask the reader to identify when to use the technique, the first action, what to do next, when to stop and what to do if an item is missing. Record misunderstandings and any help the tester had to give. Revise and retest when an error could cause harm.

A spelling check or readability score can flag difficult wording. Neither proves that a person can use the page correctly. Do not describe a draft as tested until an actual reader test has been recorded in [VALIDATION_LOG.md](VALIDATION_LOG.md).
