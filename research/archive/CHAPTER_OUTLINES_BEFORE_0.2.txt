# Victorian Bush Survival Field Guide

## Personal edition

**Geographic scope:** Victoria, Australia  
**Edition:** Working source - not yet field-ready  
**Last source review:** Not yet completed

> **Do not use this draft in an emergency.** These chapters are outlines and research plans, not finished instructions. Advice will enter the finished guide only after it passes the required evidence, safety and wording checks.

## Purpose

This guide is intended to help someone without survival training who is lost, injured or stranded in the Victorian bush. It will explain what to do first and why. Immediate danger, getting help, body temperature, injuries and water come before less urgent bushcraft or food gathering. The order will depend on the emergency.

Find advice by the problem you face, whatever brought you into the bush. First-aid pages will explain what to do with a first-aid kit and without one. Each page will name the supplies it needs.

## Evidence standard

Every fact, instruction and picture must pass the project's [content checks](../../CONTENT_VALIDATION_GATE.md). For advice that could cause serious harm if wrong, we compare independent sources, look for conflicting advice, check that it applies in Victoria and obtain specialist review.

## Emergency limitation

The finished guide will help you prepare and make decisions. It cannot guarantee your safety or make a phone or radio connect. It does not replace training, emergency services or medical care.

# First aid and getting medical help

**Chapter status:** Proposed - no medical guidance approved.

## Reader job

Recognise urgent threats, give first aid within your training and ability, and work out when and how to get medical help.

Every practical condition page will use the same paired presentation:

- **With a first-aid kit** — name the supplies needed for this injury or illness.
- **Without a first-aid kit** — also use this section if your kit is missing those supplies.

Put the shared first actions, when to call for help and what to keep checking before these two sections.

## Planned sections

- Checking the emergency and knowing when to call for help
- CPR (chest compressions and rescue breaths) and using an automated external defibrillator (AED)
- Severe bleeding and shock
- Snakebite
- Head and spinal injury
- Fracture, dislocation, sprain and inability to walk
- Hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature) and heat illness
- Burns from heat, smoke or hot gases, electricity and chemicals
- Asthma, stroke, seizure, heart attack and diabetic emergency
- Bites, stings and allergic reactions
- Wounds and infection risk
- Eye injury and poisoning
- Checking for changes, keeping notes and telling the people taking over care
- Moving to medical care versus waiting for rescue

## Gate requirements

- Every treatment, symptom threshold and evacuation rule is Level 3.
- Use current Australian clinical guidance, not overseas or historical survival texts as the primary authority.
- Record guideline version and review date.
- Define the comprehensive first-aid kit in chapter 13 and cross-check every named item, quantity, expiry and prerequisite.
- The no-kit path must identify when no safe substitute exists and when lack of equipment changes evacuation urgency.
- Do not include invasive improvisation, unverified natural remedies or substitutes that introduce infection, bleeding, airway, circulation or poisoning risk.
- A qualified Australian clinician must review the exact final words and diagrams.
- Test page navigation under time pressure without simulating unsafe treatment.
- Traditional remedies, if discussed at all, must be separated from clinically established treatment and pass both medical and cultural gates.

# Water

**Chapter status:** Proposed - no source or treatment method approved.

## Reader job

Weigh the need for water against the dangers of looking for it. These include injury, becoming more lost and drinking water that contains germs or harmful chemicals. Explain what each treatment can and cannot remove.

## Planned sections

- When water becomes a priority
- Whether to move in search of water
- Victorian landscape and seasonal indicators
- Rain, surface water and carried sources
- Collection methods
- Treatment methods and their limitations
- Cloudy water, disease-causing germs, blue-green algae, salt and harmful chemicals
- Containers, storage and keeping treated water from becoming contaminated again
- Methods that do not make unsafe water safe

## Gate requirements

- All safety, treatment-performance and drinking claims are Level 3.
- Use current Australian/Victorian public-health and water authority sources plus independent technical confirmation.
- Record organism/contaminant, treatment conditions, time, temperature and equipment limits.
- Seek failure evidence and conditions under which a method is ineffective.
- Do not infer water safety from appearance, movement, animal use or a single landscape sign.
- A field test can show whether someone can use the equipment. It cannot prove the water is free of harmful germs; that needs suitable scientific testing.

# Plants and natural materials

**Chapter status:** Proposed - no species or use approved.

## Reader job

Identify a small number of common Victorian plants and natural materials for useful emergency tasks. Explain when uncertainty means you must not use them.

## Planned entry structure

- Common and accepted scientific names
- Traditional or local names only with the necessary permission and correct local context
- Whole-plant shape, how it grows and its size
- Features that distinguish it: leaves or leaf-like structures, bark, stems, flowers, fruit and seeds
- Habitat and Victorian distribution
- Season and life stage
- The exact part that can be used and what it could help with in an emergency
- Preparation, limits and when not to use it
- Dangerous lookalikes
- Poisoning risks and clear reasons not to use it
- Identification confidence, reviewers and source IDs

## Gate requirements

- Every species use/identity entry follows the Level 3 edible-plant/natural-food gate where consumption is involved.
- Evidence of a plant's name or where it grows is not evidence that it is edible or that a preparation method is safe.
- At least three independent checks, including an authoritative Victorian botanical source, are required for consequential claims.
- A Victorian botanist must review the exact final entry and image panel.
- Pictures must show the features needed to distinguish the plant, its season and size. Do not use AI-generated pictures for identification.
- If a novice cannot reliably distinguish a dangerous lookalike from the printed panel, reject the consumption entry.
- Aboriginal knowledge must also pass the Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property (ICIP) checks. Finding information in a public source does not by itself give permission to reuse it.

## Source method

The final chapter will explain how we check advice. Advice that could cause more harm needs stronger evidence. Several websites repeating one original source do not count as independent checks. We also look for conflicting advice and explain or leave out claims that remain uncertain.

## Specialist reviewers

The final edition will list, with permission, each reviewer's relevant role, the material reviewed, version and date. Review of one topic does not imply endorsement of the whole book.

## Cultural authority and contributors

Any Aboriginal knowledge, language, artwork or cultural mapping will record the authorising person or organisation, Country, agreed wording, attribution, permitted edition/distribution and any limits. Material without an appropriate authority will be excluded.

## Known limitations

The final edition will state unresolved limitations, excluded topics, geographic boundaries, data dates and circumstances in which current official advice must override the book.

## Corrections

Edit the original chapter and its records, not the combined Markdown or PDF. Check the change for wording, evidence and safety. Then rebuild the files and check that the pages, pictures and links still work.
