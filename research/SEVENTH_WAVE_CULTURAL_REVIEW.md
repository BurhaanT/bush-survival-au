# Seventh-wave cultural-source review

**Date:** 8 September 2026  
**Purpose:** Keep the new plant material with its named people, place, source and permission boundary.

**Evidence record:** S-424, S-428, S-430, S-433, S-435 and S-438; synthesis and decisions R-431–R-447. These are public-source checks, not consultation, consent or endorsement.

## Governing rule

Traditional Owner knowledge is not a decorative “heritage” layer and is not automatically reusable because it is visible online. Cultural authority is strongest for the knowledge itself. Botanical identity, toxicology, nutrition and individual medical safety are separate questions that still need their own qualified review.

For this private edition:

- exact public names and brief statements may be recorded with their Country and source;
- accounts from different Nations are not merged;
- a government, museum, council or academic host is described honestly rather than called direct community publication;
- a research permit, Elder participation or permission for one project is not treated as permission for this guide;
- detailed harvesting, medicine, artwork, maps and seasonal designs are not copied; and
- any shared or published edition must reopen consent, attribution, benefit and editorial-control decisions with the relevant Traditional Owner organisation.

This approach follows the Victorian Traditional Owner Native Foods and Botanicals Strategy and consent example in S-350, the Victorian Aboriginal Heritage Council and IP Australia governance guidance in S-384, and the private-copy/wider-use boundary in S-421.

## White elderberry

Museums Victoria's Bunjilaka plant page records **Burne-burne** in a Djabwurrung context and **Garawed** in a Gunaikurnai context for *Sambucus gaudichaudiana*. It says the cream-coloured berries can be eaten raw. The guide itself warns that people without proper knowledge should not try to eat the plants.

The page was prepared within Bunjilaka and is valuable public museum material. It is not presented as a direct current statement from either corporation, and it does not authorise this project's food instruction. The two names remain separate. The book paraphrases only a short food-history point and adds no method.

## Rounded noon-flower

The City of Melbourne's floral-emblem page labels two distinct sections:

- a **Bunurong** account that describes the leaves eaten raw or cooked and ripe fruit enjoyed in summer; and
- a **Wurundjeri** section which expressly says no ancestral Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung record is known, then attributes contemporary information to Ngarigo Elder Rodney Mason of south-eastern New South Wales.

The book does not turn the Ngarigo material into Wurundjeri history or Victorian ancestral evidence. It retains only the narrow Bunurong leaf statement for the conditional page. The fruit and medicine statements are not turned into actions because botanical and medical questions remain unresolved. The website's copyright and licence terms are recorded; no artwork or long passage is copied.

## Old-man saltbush

No direct current Victorian Traditional Owner-controlled source was located that safely joins *Atriplex nummularia*, a named Country and a preparation suitable for this guide. Beth Gott's University of Melbourne essay points to *Victorian Koorie Plants*, prepared with the Yangennanock Women's Group, but the accessible material does not justify assigning this exact plant claim to a particular Nation.

The absence of a reused attribution is not a claim that knowledge was absent. It means the project has not located a source and permission path strong enough for the intended statement. No generic “Aboriginal use” is inserted.

## Seaberry saltbush

The Warrnambool nutrition study says its selected foods remained important to a Martang Djab Wurrung community and records Elder assistance, collection permission and a blessing for that research. The paper is evidence of a real, named relationship. It is not authority for this private project to claim that every result is community-owned approval for a survival method.

The Martang Registered Aboriginal Party registration was revoked with effect from **1 August 2019**. Eastern Maar Aboriginal Corporation now has Registered Aboriginal Party responsibilities over part of the relevant area. This later change is included only to prevent the book from presenting old governance as current. It does not allow the project to relabel the original study as Eastern Maar knowledge.

The private page therefore says only that the study records Martang Djab Wurrung Elder participation and direct ripe-berry use. It does not assign the title Elder to other people named separately in the acknowledgements. A shared edition would need advice from the people connected with the original work and the current relevant Traditional Owner authority.

## Chocolate lily

The City of Melbourne page keeps **Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung** and **Bunurong** accounts separate. It records different details about tubers, flowers and seasonal practice. The Bunurong section describes roasted tubers as sweet and nutty; the Wurundjeri section says roasting was required because raw tubers were poisonous.

A separate Taungurung-partnered Mansfield garden page places **Djimba Wendarri** on the lands of the Yowung-illam-balluk clan of the Taungurung Nation and says tubers were eaten raw or cooked. The project records this conflict rather than deciding that one source cancels another. It gives no preparation method.

The Bunurong account also describes a careful tending and replanting practice. This is not stripped into a generic “survival harvest” instruction. It demonstrates stewardship and skill, which is why the page tells the reader not to dig wild plants.

## Pale vanilla-lily

The 2020 *Smart Gardens for a Dry Climate* booklet says its Traditional Knowledge section was written by Dja Dja Wurrung man Harley Douglas, then working with Dja Dja Wurrung Enterprises trading as Djandak. It records **Gitjawil matom** for chocolate lily and vanilla lily.

The same plant-guide text calls underground structures “tuberous fruits”, which conflicts with botanical morphology. The guide preserves the attribution but does not copy that error or infer a complete method. The booklet is published by Coliban Water and the City of Greater Bendigo, so DJAARA's website terms are not claimed as its legal licence. The Dja Dja Wurrung joint-management plan, DJAARA material and this project's ICIP rule still establish the need to keep the knowledge with Djandak and seek DJAARA's direction before wider publication or adaptation.

The name appears here in a short private-copy attribution. Remove or re-clear it before any wider distribution.

## What is deliberately absent

This pass does not include:

- sacred or restricted knowledge;
- exact cultural harvesting sites;
- copied seasonal-calendar artwork;
- medicinal recipes or claimed treatments;
- detailed tending or collection sequences detached from Country;
- a claim that all Aboriginal Victorians used one plant in one way; or
- a claim that any Traditional Owner organisation endorses this guide.

## Future review path

Before sharing these pages, ask the relevant organisation whether it wants the material removed, corrected, attributed differently, licensed, co-written or left private. The question must include the exact proposed words, images, audience and purpose. Silence is not consent.
