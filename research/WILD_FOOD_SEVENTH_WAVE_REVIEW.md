# Wild-food seventh-wave review

**Date:** 8 September 2026  
**Scope:** White elderberry, rounded noon-flower, old-man saltbush, seaberry saltbush, chocolate lily and pale vanilla-lily.  
**Review status:** Internal source, wording, rights and visual review for a private working edition. No botanist, toxicologist, food-safety specialist, dietitian, conservation or legal specialist, Traditional Owner organisation, or untrained reader has approved these pages.

**Evidence record:** S-424–S-439 and R-431–R-447. Source count is not independent-confirmation count; limitations are recorded claim by claim in the registers.

## Result

This pass adds six useful plant pages without treating all six as emergency food.

All six pages are non-actionable after the final adversarial pass. They teach recognition limits, food history, nutrition limits, cultural context or reasons to leave the plant alone. Rounded noon-flower had briefly been drafted as a conditional reminder, but the missing safe-serving, personal-tolerance and complete plant/site gates made that too permissive for an emergency book.

## Shared gate applied to every candidate

Each candidate was checked separately for:

1. current accepted Victorian taxonomy and distribution;
2. diagnostic features and meaningful lookalikes;
3. exact food part and life stage;
4. preparation evidence rather than a copied folk shortcut;
5. human safety, including uncertainty about dose and other plant parts;
6. fresh-food energy evidence and whether it could change survival decisions;
7. legal, conservation and contaminated-site limits;
8. Country, knowledge holder, source type and reuse boundary for cultural material;
9. image provenance, licence and what the picture does not show; and
10. whether the instruction could create a dangerous false positive.

A strong result in one column did not cancel a gap in another. A documented food tradition does not by itself identify a specimen, define a safe modern serving or give this project cultural authority. Likewise, a correct botanical name does not prove food safety.

## White elderberry

### Evidence that agrees

- VicFlora accepts *Sambucus gaudichaudiana* as a native Victorian species and describes a low shrub or subshrub arising each year from a perennial tuberous rootstock. Its compound leaves, branched white-flower clusters and pale drupes provide a useful feature set.
- The Australian National Botanic Gardens records white berries eaten raw and describes the fruit as juicy, pleasantly flavoured and a little sour. Its page cites earlier published sources rather than a modern feeding study.
- Museums Victoria's Bunjilaka page separately records the plant and cream-coloured berries eaten raw. It places **Burne-burne** in a Djabwurrung context and **Garawed** in a Gunaikurnai context.
- A Royal Botanic Gardens NSW publication describes a pale yellow or whitish edible drupe around 5 mm across and gives a mean fruit mass of 86 mg in its study material.

### Why the page remains non-actionable

The species-specific chemical evidence is too thin. An older screening paper reported no cyanogenic reaction for the tested *S. gaudichaudiana* material. That is useful but does not establish every part, maturity stage, place, serving or person. A modern review of elder species shows why advice from European or North American elderberries cannot be transferred automatically: cyanogenic glycosides and other constituents differ by species, plant part and treatment.

The two photographs show a Victorian flowering plant and a source-labelled fruit cluster. They do not show a measured flower, mature versus immature fruit series, rootstock, old stem, seed section or matched elder comparison. The fruit photograph appears pale green to translucent; its title and source identify the species, but the picture alone does not establish full ripeness.

**Decision:** no eating sequence. The page may report the mature pale-fruit food history and expected juicy, mildly sour character as cultural and educational context. Green fruit and all flowers, leaves, stems, bark, roots and separated seeds remain excluded. No calorie number is supplied.

## Rounded noon-flower

### Botanical and food evidence

VicFlora and PlantNET support *Disphyma crassifolium* subsp. *clavellatum* as a creeping succulent with narrow, fleshy leaves, pink-purple flowers and a dry capsule. It occurs in coastal saline habitats and also in some inland north-western saline or heavy-soil settings. Victorian Resources Online gives a useful second Victorian description.

The City of Melbourne's rounded noon-flower page contains separately labelled Traditional Owner material. Its Bunurong section records the leaves eaten raw or cooked and the ripe fruit enjoyed seasonally. The same page says there is no known record of ancestral Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung use; it then presents contemporary knowledge shared by Ngarigo Elder Rodney Mason from south-eastern New South Wales. Those accounts must not be merged or called one Melbourne tradition.

The Bunurong leaf statement is the narrowest useful food claim that also agrees with the broader public food record. However, the same page's ripe-fruit description cannot yet be reconciled safely with VicFlora's dry, opening capsule description. The word “fruit” may be used differently across sources, but this project will not guess. Fruit, flowers, roots and topical/medicinal uses are therefore withheld.

### Nutrition and field value

A peer-reviewed study of cultivated *D. crassifolium* leaves from Portugal reported about **34 kJ or 8.1 kcal per 100 g fresh material**. This is an overseas, species-level sample rather than the Victorian subspecies, a wild-site average or a safe serving. It is retained only to show scale: even if Victorian leaves were similar, this would be a very low-energy, watery, salty vegetable.

The plant cannot replace drinking water. Salty succulent tissue may increase thirst, and neither moisture content nor appearance proves that a site is free of metals, sewage, pesticides or other contaminants. Washing removes surface dirt; it cannot establish what the plant absorbed.

### Action boundary

Conditional leaf use is retained only when the reader learned the exact plant before the emergency **and** already knew the exact site's access, treatment and contamination history. All measured and reproductive features must agree, drinking water must already be adequate, and no stop condition may apply. The page is not a first-identification key. White or milky sap, a name-only match such as “pigface” or “karkalla”, an incomplete feature set, an unverified site, a protected saltmarsh, or any need to uproot or strip the patch ends the decision.

## Old-man saltbush

### Identity and conservation

VicFlora recognises *Atriplex nummularia* in Victoria with subsp. *nummularia* and subsp. *omissa*. Its account says the subspecies are not always readily distinguishable. The current Victorian conservation assessment lists subsp. *omissa* as Endangered. A broad grey-green saltbush appearance is therefore not enough for a food instruction.

Useful features for an expert include a shrub up to about 3 m, separate male and female plants, variable broad grey-green leaves, and thick mealy fruiting bracteoles around the seed. The acquired habit and leaf photographs have no confirmed locality or subspecies. The small USDA image shows dry seed-containing units with a 5 mm scale; it is not a clean photograph of a naked seed and is not Victorian.

### Food evidence and chemistry

FSANZ's Record of Views supports a history of use for old-man saltbush as food. Beth Gott's University of Melbourne essay describes a Victorian plant-use publication produced with the Yangennanock Women's Group, but the accessible passage does not attach an old-man-saltbush claim to one Nation and Country. No more specific attribution is invented.

One Australian commercial-leaf laboratory sample reported **111 kJ, about 26.5 kcal, per 100 g fresh leaf**, with 3.6 g protein, 0.3 g fat and 0.1 g available carbohydrate. It was a single refrigerated commercial sample supplied in 2011; provenance, Victorian subspecies and wild-site conditions were not established. It is a scale example, not a field value.

Agricultural sources and field studies show why chemistry matters: *Atriplex* can accumulate salt, oxalate and nitrate, and concentrations respond to growing conditions. Livestock limits cannot be converted into a safe human serving. Nor does culinary sale prove that a wild roadside, saltflat or restoration planting is fit to eat.

**Decision:** education only. The page explains the likely salty taste and low energy of the one commercial sample but gives no wild serving, harvest or processing method.

## Seaberry saltbush

### Name and recognition

VicFlora uses *Rhagodia candolleana* subsp. *candolleana*. The Australian Plant Census also retains that accepted Australian usage; Kew currently places the taxon under *Chenopodium candolleanum*. The book leads with the Victorian name and records the international synonym rather than presenting the disagreement as two species.

VicFlora describes a sprawling shrub to about 4 m, leaves that may be opposite or alternate, shiny green upper surfaces and grey-scaly undersides, and small crimson mature fruit clasped by the reddish flower covering. It is mainly coastal in Victoria, with some inland brackish or salt-lake occurrences.

The Victorian field photograph shows glossy leaves and crimson fruit; the source does not establish ripeness. Two University of Melbourne herbarium sheets provide scaled, place- and date-labelled pressed material from Tidal River and Brighton Beach. They show dry form, not fresh colour. None clearly shows a living whole shrub, both leaf surfaces at scale, flower development, cut fruit or seed.

### Food, taste and cultural scope

The 2020 Warrnambool study records direct consumption of ripe *R. candolleana* berries and analysed permitted, botanist-assisted collections made with Martang Djab Wurrung Elder involvement. Agriculture Victoria separately describes the mature fruit as bitter. The study is strong evidence for its exact material and research relationship; the Agriculture Victoria page supports the taste description. Neither source is this project's permission, a serving-size study or a current Traditional Owner organisation's approval.

The Martang Registered Aboriginal Party was later revoked. Eastern Maar Aboriginal Corporation now holds Registered Aboriginal Party responsibilities over part of the relevant area. This governance change does not erase the study or allow the project to reassign its knowledge. Any future shared edition needs current direction from the relevant Traditional Owner authority and the people connected with the original work.

The study reports dry-basis composition and variable mineral results, not a validated fresh edible-flesh energy value. Tiny fruit and an unknown safe serving mean calories cannot be budgeted.

African boxthorn is a useful warning comparison because it also carries red-orange berries in coastal and disturbed places. It is a very thorny shrub with smooth clustered leaves and round, many-seeded berries around 1 cm across. Agriculture Victoria says its fruit may be toxic to people. These differences reject some candidates; their absence does not positively identify seaberry.

**Decision:** education only. No tasting, berry serving or leaf use.

## Chocolate lily

### Identity and pictures

VicFlora describes *Arthropodium strictum* as a tufted perennial up to about 1.2 m, with narrow basal leaves, small tubers some distance from the rootstock, usually branched flowering stems and solitary deep pink or mauve, chocolate-scented flowers. It is widespread through Victorian grassy plains and woodlands but rare in the Mallee and absent from the Alps and far east beyond the Bairnsdale area.

The three acquired photographs show flowering plants in western Victoria and South Australia, including a useful flower close-up. Despite its filename, `chocolate-lily-whole.jpg` shows only flowers, buds and part of a stem. No image shows the rootstock, measured tubers, complete basal leaves, capsule, seed or matched relatives. VicFlora also records the unresolved *Arthropodium* sp. 3 as resembling and being largely coextensive with *A. strictum*. Its relationship and food status cannot be inferred. The plate cannot support digging.

### Food conflict and stewardship

The City of Melbourne page contains separately labelled Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung and Bunurong accounts. The Wurundjeri section says tubers were roasted because they are poisonous raw. The Bunurong section describes roasted tubers with a sweet, nutty flavour and a careful seasonal tending practice. A Taungurung-partnered Mansfield garden page, by contrast, says **Djimba Wendarri** tubers were eaten raw or cooked.

The disagreement is recorded, not averaged. It could reflect different practice, place, plant material or source wording. Without direct review by the relevant knowledge holders and a food-safety specialist, this guide gives neither raw nor cooked instructions. It also does not reproduce the Bunurong harvesting design as a generic survival technique.

The Warrnambool laboratory study measured moisture and dry-basis nutrients in tubers, but did not publish a validated fresh energy figure or safe cooking endpoint for field use. An editor-created calorie number would give false precision.

**Decision:** cultural and stewardship page only. Do not dig or eat wild chocolate lily. The Bunurong roasted-tuber taste description remains clearly attributed; smell and taste never identify the plant.

## Pale vanilla-lily

### Identity and lookalikes

VicFlora describes *Arthropodium milleflorum* as a tufted perennial to about 1.3 m, with tubers 2–3 cm long, long flat or spreading basal leaves, and clusters of two to nine pale flowers at each node. It occurs chiefly in montane and subalpine country along the Great Divide, with scattered cool lowland records. Flowering is mainly November to February.

Victoria also has an Endangered unnamed entity, *Arthropodium* sp. 1, and an unresolved *Arthropodium* sp. 2. Small flower and anther details are part of the separation. The present three images show pale flowers, stems and some basal leaves, but no underground organs, seed or complete matched comparison.

### Food, energy and cultural boundary

Australian National Botanic Gardens material records vanilla-lily roots among south-eastern Australian plant foods. Its later horticultural page says tubers were eaten raw or roasted and flowers eaten, but it is a compiled account, not Victorian species-specific toxicology or current cultural permission.

A Coliban Water booklet includes a Traditional Knowledge section written by Dja Dja Wurrung man Harley Douglas and records the Dja Dja Wurrung name **Gitjawil matom** for chocolate lily and vanilla lily. Its vanilla-lily plant text contains the botanically inaccurate phrase “tuberous fruits”. That wording is not repeated as morphology, and the source is not converted into a field recipe. The booklet is a third-party publication, so DJAARA's website terms are not described as its legal licence. Under this project's ICIP rule, wider use of the attributed knowledge should nevertheless be cleared with DJAARA.

An older composition table reports **124 kJ, about 30 kcal, per 100 g raw root** under “Vanilla Lily”. The source reproduces 1993 tables, but gives too little specimen, Country, season, sample, taxonomic and preparation detail for field transfer. Even taken at face value, this is low energy relative to the effort and damage of digging.

No reliable taste description suitable for a rejection check was found. “Vanilla” refers to flower scent, not proof that the root tastes like vanilla.

**Decision:** cultural and stewardship page only. No wild digging, tasting, cooking or calorie budgeting.

## Image decision

Sixteen files were decoded and visually inspected. All are intact. Every caption states whether the image is Victorian, interstate, possibly cultivated, historical, small, or missing a decisive structure. No AI botanical illustration is used.

Important gaps remain:

- no complete flower-to-ripe-fruit plate for white elderberry;
- no verified Victorian rounded-noon-flower close-up showing flower, leaf cross-section and dry capsule together;
- no confirmed-subspecific old-man-saltbush set;
- no fresh seaberry underside, cut-fruit and seed sequence;
- no lawful cultivated underground-organ sequence for either lily; and
- no botanist-approved matched panels for each consequential lookalike.

The two University of Melbourne herbarium files are CC BY-NC 3.0 AU and remain limited to this personal, non-commercial edition. All other licence and integrity details are in `IMAGE_REGISTER.md` and `book/assets/manifest.json`.

## Release decision

Keep all six pages in the private reader because they add meaningful identification limits, nutrition honesty and place-specific cultural context. None provides an eating or harvesting sequence. Do not call any page a first-time field identification system.

`FIELD_READY_BUILD=NO` remains controlling. No PDF is approved from this review.
