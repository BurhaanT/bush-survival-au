# Victorian fish action evidence packet

**Evidence cut-off and access date:** 28 September 2026  
**Geographic scope:** Victoria  
**Project status:** staged research decision; not field-ready instruction  
**Controlling release state:** `FIELD_READY_BUILD=NO`

## Question answered

This packet tests whether Chapter 12 can safely progress from a generic fishing section to a bounded sequence that helps a novice:

1. decide whether fishing is still a sensible use of time;
2. identify a small number of realistic Victorian catches;
3. apply the correct legal disposition;
4. handle, cook and eat a retained catch safely; and
5. manage an accidental embedded fishhook.

It does **not** approve fishing as an early survival priority. Rescue communication, immediate danger, first aid, exposure control, shelter and water remain ahead of food in most short survival events. It also does not approve any fish image, dispatch method, cleaning cut or medical procedure for publication or field reliance.

## Bottom line

**MODIFY / staged GO.** A three-species recognition and legal-disposition atlas may proceed for:

- European carp (*Cyprinus carpio*);
- redfin or English perch (*Perca fluviatilis*); and
- black bream (*Acanthopagrus butcheri*), with a yellowfin-bream comparison and hybrid warning.

The atlas must be a positive-identification aid, not a complete Victorian fish key. It must send uncertain identity to a stop branch and must require the current Victorian Recreational Fishing Guide, exact-water restrictions, signs and health warnings to be checked.

The following remain withheld:

- species-specific dispatch instructions;
- brain-location, spiking and bleeding diagrams;
- anatomy-dependent gutting or filleting steps;
- a claim that one fish cooking temperature is the settled Australian rule; and
- any self-removal fishhook procedure.

A conservative, non-removal fishhook-stabilisation card and a clearly labelled cooking-temperature synthesis may advance only as review drafts. They still need the reviewers identified below. Nothing in this packet changes `FIELD_READY_BUILD=NO`.

## 1. Sequence boundary

The proposed action sequence is:

1. **Do not fish yet** if rescue, immediate danger, first aid, exposure, shelter or water needs attention.
2. Use only lawful equipment already available. Do not improvise traps, unattended lines or destructive methods.
3. Check the current licence or exemption, exact water, method, equipment, bait, closures, seasons, local notices and health warnings. The VFA states that lawful equipment can still be prohibited in a particular water or time [S-459].
4. Decide before casting whether every likely catch can be identified and lawfully dealt with. There is no safe universal action for an unidentified fish: a protected, out-of-range or excess fish normally needs prompt release, while a positively identified European carp must not be returned alive [S-459–S-462].
5. Identify and measure quickly. Measure from the tip of the closed-mouth snout to the end of the tail [S-459].
6. Apply the species- and place-specific legal disposition below.
7. Retain only a positively identified, lawful fish from water not rejected by current health advice. Identity, legal take and food safety are separate gates [S-459, S-465].
8. Do not continue into dispatch, cleaning or eating unless the later reviewed route is complete.

This is a project synthesis. No located Victorian authority publishes this exact survival-priority sequence.

## 2. Three-species atlas

### European carp — *Cyprinus carpio*

**Why it belongs:** Carp are widespread and common in much of south-eastern Victoria and the Murray–Darling system, occur in other western and south-western waters, and are subject to a legal disposition that a reader must not confuse with ordinary catch-and-release [S-460]. They are absent east of the Snowy River according to the VFA species account, so “widespread” must not be printed as “statewide” [S-460].

**Minimum positive features for the plate:**

- stocky, moderately deep body;
- small eye, blunt snout and protrusible mouth with thick lips;
- ordinarily four barbels: one longer barbel at each mouth corner and one shorter barbel on each end of the upper lip;
- one long-based dorsal fin; and
- normally large, thick scales [S-460, S-463].

**Mandatory variation panel:** VFA records leather carp with few or no scales, mirror carp with a few very large scattered scales, and carp–goldfish hybrids that may have weak or no barbels [S-460]. A no-barbel fish is therefore not automatically a goldfish, and the main carp picture cannot carry the legal decision alone. A goldfish comparison should show the absence of barbels, but hybrids keep uncertainty as a stop condition [S-463].

**Current legal disposition:** No minimum legal size and no bag limit. A positively identified carp must not be returned alive. Kill it immediately; do not leave it on the bank. The VFA permits the dead carp to be taken away or the carcass to be cut so that it sinks before being returned to the water [S-460]. Possessing, transporting or releasing live carp, or using live carp as bait, is prohibited [S-460].

**Limit that must appear beside the rule:** This is a narrow, species-specific noxious-fish rule. It must not be transferred to an unknown fish, goldfish, another introduced species or general fish waste. The VFA's ordinary fish-waste advice is to use a bin or take waste home; the dead-carp disposal statement is a specific exception [S-460, S-465].

### Redfin / English perch — *Perca fluviatilis*

**Why it belongs:** Redfin are widespread and common in many Victorian lakes and slower waters and can be locally abundant [S-461]. They are not uniform across Victoria; the VFA records only a few Gippsland localities and notes their absence from fast water and usual absence from high-altitude areas [S-461].

**Minimum positive features for the plate:**

- deep, laterally compressed body and large head;
- large mouth reaching to about the eye;
- two separate dorsal fins, the first spiny;
- five or more broad dark vertical bands;
- a distinct dark blotch near the rear of the first dorsal fin; and
- red or orange pelvic and anal fins and outer/lower tail area [S-461, S-463].

The plate must mark the spiny first dorsal and strong opercular spine as hand hazards. Colour alone is not enough.

**Current legal disposition:** No minimum legal size and no bag limit. The VFA encourages anglers not to return redfin because of their effects on native fish, but the current species page does not create the same must-kill rule as carp. Transporting live fish without VFA approval is illegal [S-461].

**Limit that must appear beside the rule:** “Encouraged not to return” must not be rewritten as “illegal to release.” A retained redfin still has to pass water-quality, positive food-identity, dispatch, cold-chain and cooking gates.

### Black bream — *Acanthopagrus butcheri*

**Why it belongs:** Black bream are common in Victorian coastal drainages and occupy estuaries, coastal lakes and lower river reaches [S-462]. It gives the atlas an estuarine species and exposes an important regional rule and lookalike problem.

**Minimum positive features for the plate:**

- robust, deep body;
- sharply rounded snout and one continuous dorsal fin;
- large scales and prominent lateral line;
- gold-brown to bronze or olive upper body, pale belly and greenish reflections when fresh; and
- usually dusky brown pelvic, anal and tail fins [S-462, S-464].

**Mandatory comparison panel:** Yellowfin bream (*Acanthopagrus australis*) usually have yellow pelvic and anal fins, but black bream juveniles may have yellowish fins. The two species can be difficult to distinguish and hybridise in parts of their range [S-464]. Colour is therefore a comparison clue, not a sole identification rule. “Black bream” is also used for unrelated fishes; scientific name must accompany every working asset.

**Current legal disposition:** The minimum legal size for all bream species, including tarwhine, is 28 cm. The combined general bag limit is 10. Under the Fisheries (Gippsland Lakes Black Bream) Notice 2026, black bream in the Gippsland Lakes and tributaries, excluding Lake Tyers, have a 38 cm maximum and a bag limit of 7 [S-462]. That notice took effect on 27 August 2026 and is drafted to expire 12 months later unless revoked sooner, so the exact current notice must be checked before use [S-462]. Regulation 130 expressly prohibits filleting **black bream** in or on Victorian waters and permits it at a cleaning table at a boat ramp [S-462]. A scale-fish carcass may have the gut, gills or scales removed but must not otherwise be cut or mutilated [S-459].

**Publication conflict:** The current VFA web page and regulation 130 name black bream, while the downloadable 2026 recreational guide says more broadly that “Bream can't be filleted” [S-462]. The statutory exact scope located is black bream. The project's whole-or-legal-carcass treatment for an uncertain bream is a conservative fail-closed rule, not a claim that the regulation names every bream species.

**Conservative uncertainty rule proposed for testing:** If a bream cannot confidently be separated from black bream in the Gippsland Lakes system, the draft may use the stricter 28–38 cm, maximum-seven, whole-or-carcass treatment. This is a project fail-closed rule, not a statement that every bream everywhere has those limits. Positive food identity is still required.

### Species deferred

Trout, salmonids, native freshwater species and additional estuarine fish are deferred. Annual closures, water-specific rules, protected-species confusion and a larger lookalike burden would turn a small positive-identification atlas into a false promise of broad coverage. A future species must independently pass distribution, identity, legal-disposition, food-safety, image and human-review gates.

## 3. Dispatch and cleaning evidence

### What authorities support

- VFA advises immediately killing a retained fish with a quick blow to the head, then placing the dead fish on ice or in an ice slurry [S-465].
- RSPCA Australia describes a forceful, accurate blow just above the eyes, checking whether the stun worked, repeating the blow if the fish is not unconscious, and bleeding only after an effective stun [S-466].
- NSW DPIRD describes percussive stunning and species-specific iki-jime; it explicitly says brain location is species-specific and links to the Iki Jime site [S-467].
- The Iki Jime site has separate anatomical overlays for European carp and European perch/redfin [S-467]. Its yellowfin-bream page says related bream have similar brain locations, but no located page supplies a verified *Acanthopagrus butcheri* overlay. A search for “black bream” also reaches unrelated *Spondyliosoma cantharus* [S-467].

### Why the sequence stays withheld

The sources do not provide a complete novice-ready sequence for all three chosen species. Remaining gaps include:

- exact restraint that keeps hands clear of dorsal, opercular and hook hazards;
- a species-validated target and tool for every fish size and morph;
- observable, novice-usable signs of unconsciousness and death;
- what to do after an ineffective first strike without prolonging suffering;
- species-correct bleeding location and how to avoid gut, spine and knife injury;
- whether the reader can perform the sequence under stress, cold or darkness; and
- image-reuse permission for anatomical overlays.

Accordingly, **species-specific dispatch, spiking, bleeding and anatomy-dependent cleaning remain NO-GO** until an aquatic-welfare reviewer and experienced Victorian fish preparer approve the exact words and original or licensed visuals, followed by controlled demonstration on lawfully obtained dead fish. No autonomous live-animal trial is authorised.

The VFA carcass definition establishes what may legally be removed from a black bream at the water; it does not teach where or how to cut [S-459, S-462]. None of the authoritative sources checked for this packet provides a complete novice-safe gutting or filleting sequence for these exact three species. This is a bounded search result, not proof that no such authoritative sequence exists.

### Ice-slurry contradiction

RSPCA Australia says chilling fish in ice and water is not a suitable killing method because it does not produce rapid humane death [S-466]. Current NSW DPIRD advice permits an ice slurry as a dispatch method for a bounded class of small- to medium-bodied warm-water fish [S-467]. VFA's own order is different: quick blow, then place the **dead** fish in ice slurry [S-465].

The project must not resolve these different contexts by inventing a universal rule. For this Victorian guide, ice may be described only as post-dispatch chilling after death has been confirmed. It is not an approved primary killing method.

## 4. Cooking endpoint and thermometer placement

Australian public authorities publish different numbers:

| Source and context | Published guidance | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| NSW Food Authority consumer cooking table | Fish 63°C | Fish-specific number; no hold time is stated on the page [S-468] |
| healthdirect consumer guide | Fish 69°C; opaque and separates easily with a fork | No hold time or placement detail [S-468] |
| FSANZ general consumer basics | 70°C in the centre generally helps make food safe | General food statement, not a fish-specific endpoint [S-468] |
| FSANZ advice for older or otherwise vulnerable people | Generally 75°C; check the thickest part with a clean thermometer | Deliberately more conservative population context [S-468] |
| Victorian consumer sources already recorded in S-393–S-394 | General 75°C advice; fish thoroughly cooked and flakes easily | No fish-specific hold time |

These values must not be averaged or presented as if regulators agree on one universal fish temperature. If the personal guide retains **75°C in the thickest flesh** as a conservative working target, it must say that this is the project's synthesis, chosen from conflicting Australian guidance, not a settled fish-specific legal minimum. No hold time may be invented.

**Thermometer technique supported by FSANZ:** use a digital probe rather than an infrared surface reading; clean and sanitise the probe before and after use; place it in the thickest part; wait until the reading stabilises [S-468]. Checking more than one thick area where thickness varies is a reasonable project precaution, but it needs food-safety review before becoming instruction.

If no thermometer is available, opaque flesh that separates easily with a fork is an authority-backed sensory fallback [S-468], but it is lower-assurance than measuring the centre. Neither temperature nor appearance corrects wrong species identity, spoilage, algal or other toxins, histamine, chemical contamination or unsafe handling.

## 5. Embedded fishhook first aid

### Source conflict

ANZCOR's current embedded-object guidance says not to remove an embedded object that is causing bleeding; apply pressure and padding around it [S-469]. St John Australia's current eye guidance says never remove an object embedded in or penetrating the eye, do not press the eyeball, prevent rubbing, cover lightly without pressure and seek medical aid [S-469].

St John Australia's fifth-edition manual, still sold as its authorised digital reference, contains a narrower fishhook exception: when medical aid is not readily available and the hook is just under the skin, make one removal attempt, then manage it as an embedded object if unsuccessful; never attempt eye removal [S-470]. The formerly public PDF URL returned 404 during the final link check, so the exact passage was available only through the indexed edition text and the current publisher's product record, not a fresh full-PDF inspection.

Queensland Health's current Primary Clinical Care Manual contains removal methods for clinicians managing a **small** hook. It directs consultation for an eye injury, a large hook or involvement of tendons, joints, nerves or vessels and notes the possible need for anaesthetic and antibiotics [S-471]. Its string-yank, needle-cover and push-through techniques are clinical procedures, not lay field-first-aid permission.

### Preferred project route pending clinician review

The guide should use a **non-removal route for every embedded fishhook** until an Australian remote/emergency clinician reviews the exception. This is deliberately more conservative than the St John superficial-hook exception.

**With a comprehensive first-aid kit — proposed review draft:**

1. Stop fishing, make the scene safe and control life-threatening bleeding first.
2. Do not pull, twist, push through or cut out the embedded hook.
3. Put on gloves. Apply pressure around, not on, the hook if it is bleeding.
4. Build sterile padding around both sides of the hook and secure it without pushing the point, shank or lure deeper.
5. For an eye, eyelid or immediately adjacent injury: do not remove, press or rub; place only a light no-pressure dressing, minimise eye movement and arrange emergency medical help. The draft also withholds irrigation of a penetrating-eye injury as a conservative project control pending ophthalmic review; this particular “no irrigation” wording is not directly supplied by the cited St John page.
6. Monitor circulation, sensation, bleeding and the person's general condition while arranging medical care or retrieval.

**Without a kit — proposed review draft:** use the cleanest folded cloth available as surrounding padding, keep direct pressure off the hook and lightly secure it only if that can be done without movement. Do not improvise extraction with a knife, needle, pliers or fishing line. This clean-cloth substitution is a project inference from ANZCOR's padding principle and needs clinical review.

**Never attempt field removal in this draft:** eye, eyelid or near-eye injuries; a large or deep hook; a hook at or across a joint; suspected tendon, nerve or blood-vessel involvement; severe bleeding; altered sensation, colour, warmth or circulation; or any uncertain anatomy [S-469, S-471]. Because the preferred route is non-removal, this list is a minimum escalation list, not permission to remove hooks elsewhere.

After a hook is no longer embedded, ordinary minor-wound guidance supports rinsing with clean water or saline and covering the wound [S-471, S-472]. Water-related wound infections may progress quickly, especially when deeper tissue is involved [S-471]. A puncture or retained-foreign-body wound is tetanus-prone; the Australian Immunisation Handbook calls for clinical assessment and recommends a booster for people aged 10 or older with a tetanus-prone wound when the last dose was more than five years ago, with different management for incomplete or uncertain vaccination history [S-472]. This is not a field vaccination instruction.

## 7. Contradictions and unresolved questions

| Issue | Evidence conflict or gap | Current project treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Unidentified catch | Ordinary protected/out-of-range catch needs release; confirmed carp must not be returned alive | No universal unidentified-fish action. Do not fish where likely catches cannot be identified and lawfully dealt with |
| Carp waste | Ordinary fish waste goes to a bin or home; dead carp may be cut and returned so it sinks | Keep the carp exception species-specific; never generalise it |
| Redfin disposition | VFA encourages non-return but does not print a carp-like must-kill rule | Do not rewrite encouragement as law |
| Bream identity | Black and yellowfin bream can be difficult to separate; hybrids occur; common names overlap | Scientific names, comparison panel and a fail-closed Gippsland treatment; no colour-only identity |
| Bream filleting scope | Regulation 130 and the current VFA web page name black bream; the downloadable 2026 guide says “Bream can't be filleted” | State the statutory exact scope as black bream; treat an uncertain bream whole or as a legal carcass only as a conservative project rule |
