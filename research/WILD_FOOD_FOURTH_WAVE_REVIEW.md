# Wild-food fourth-wave review

**Date:** 7 September 2026  
**Scope:** Common apple-berry, kangaroo grass, coast beard-heath and common purslane for the private Victorian bush-survival guide.  
**Status:** Research and editorial gate only. None of these plants is approved as a field-eating instruction in the current book.

## Decision summary

| Species | Current gate | What may be said now | What must not be instructed now |
|---|---|---|---|
| Common apple-berry — *Billardiera mutabilis* | **Education only** | Explain its Victorian identity, habitat, food history and the conservation problem created by overlap with *B. scandens* | Do not tell a novice to identify it from fruit alone, pick it or eat it |
| Kangaroo grass — *Themeda triandra* | **Education only / non-harvest** | Explain its importance as a Victorian grass, its documented grain history and current First Nations-led food and restoration work | Do not give an emergency grain-harvesting, raw-snacking or field-processing recipe |
| Coast beard-heath — *Leucopogon parviflorus* | **Education only / non-harvest** | Explain the plant, its small edible-fruit record and its coastal habitat, with exact regional attribution | Do not give general wild-harvest advice or treat any white heath fruit as edible |
| Common purslane — *Portulaca oleracea* | **Withhold actionable eating advice** | It may be used as a warning example: a plant widely described as edible can still be a poor and unsafe emergency-food recommendation | Do not advise eating, tasting, making tea, choosing a portion or relying on cooking as a detoxification step |

These are book-development decisions, not claims that every traditional or contemporary food use is unsafe. A conservative gate is necessary because the intended reader may be stressed, hungry, inexperienced and working without expert help.

## Why this review does not use numerical confidence percentages

This project rejects invented confidence percentages. There is no validated calculation that can turn a number of sources, photographs or reviewer opinions into a reliable percentage chance that:

- a novice has identified a wild specimen correctly;
- the encountered plant has the same chemistry as a published sample;
- a particular amount is safe for that person; or
- eating the plant will improve the outcome of an emergency.

A value such as “90% confidence” would look precise without being measured. It could encourage a reader to accept a risk that the evidence does not justify. The book should instead give a plain gate—**actionable**, **education only / non-harvest**, or **withheld**—and state exactly what evidence is present, what is missing and which stop rules apply. If a warning box needs confidence wording, it should say that identification or safety has **not been validated for reader action**, not invent a percentage.

## Evidence rules used in this note

- **Evidence** is a claim directly supported by the cited taxonomic, legal, scientific or public cultural source.
- **Inference** is a practical conclusion drawn from evidence but not directly tested. It is labelled as inference.
- **Unknown** means the necessary evidence was not located or has not been produced.
- **Editorial precaution** is a conservative rule adopted because a mistake could cause harm. It must not be rewritten as a proven toxicological fact.
- “Edible” in a flora, garden page or historical account does not by itself establish an identification method, safe amount, preparation, nutritional value or suitability in an emergency.
- Taste, smell, animal feeding and a trial bite are not identification or safety tests.

## Shared legal, site and cultural boundaries

1. Regulation 41 of the [Victorian National Parks Regulations 2024](https://www.legislation.vic.gov.au/in-force/statutory-rules/national-parks-regulations-2024) generally prohibits cutting, picking, removing, taking, damaging or destroying vegetation in a park. See also the [authorised regulations PDF](https://content.legislation.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/24-76sra001-authorised.pdf).
2. State-forest controls can treat fruit, seed, leaves and other plant material as forest produce requiring authority. See [Victorian forest produce licences](https://www.vic.gov.au/forest-produce-licence).
3. Private land requires the landholder's permission. The guide should not assume that an emergency creates a general harvesting exemption; it should help the reader prepare before travel and avoid foreseeable dependence on wild food.
4. Reject plants from places with uncertain herbicide, pesticide, sewage, animal-waste, roadside, industrial, mine, floodwater or other contamination. Washing or cooking does not prove chemical contaminants have been removed.
5. Publicly documented Aboriginal knowledge must remain attached to the named people, Country, organisation and source. A public source is not permission to detach knowledge from Country, describe it as generic “Aboriginal knowledge” or reproduce more cultural detail than the source and relevant knowledge holders allow.

## Common apple-berry — *Billardiera mutabilis* Salisb.

### Current gate

**Education only.** It is a promising candidate for a later, tightly conditional ripe-fruit entry, but it is not ready for field action. The immediate obstacle is not a demonstrated poisonous lookalike. It is the real chance of confusing it with the threatened *Billardiera scandens* or an intermediate plant, plus the lack of a modern species-specific food-safety and nutrition review.

### Identity, Victorian range and season — evidence

- VicFlora accepts *Billardiera mutabilis* and describes it as a native slender climber widespread and common in Victorian heath, woodland and forest from sea level to subalpine areas. Flowers are recorded mainly from September to January. Source: [VicFlora — *Billardiera mutabilis*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/169cd940-d54d-4f2c-9160-f723f9551d5c), updated 3 February 2026.
- The reinstatement of *B. mutabilis* as a species is supported by the taxonomic revision by Cayzer and Crisp. DOI: [10.1071/SB03028](https://doi.org/10.1071/SB03028).
- PlantNET records green, glabrous fruit about 16–20 mm long and suggests fruiting probably in late summer in New South Wales. That timing is regional evidence, not a guaranteed Victorian calendar. Source: [PlantNET — *Billardiera mutabilis*](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Billardiera~mutabilis&page=nswfl).

### Features that matter — evidence

VicFlora describes:

- a slender climbing habit;
- young stems with hairs, becoming almost hairless;
- thin, mostly sessile leaves about 18–80 mm long and 3–18 mm wide;
- leaf margins that are plain or wavy and hardly rolled under;
- solitary hanging flowers on slender stalks that lengthen in fruit;
- a bell-shaped yellow-green corolla that may become blue-tinged with age; and
- an oblong berry about 10–20 mm long, usually green or yellowish and generally hairless, containing many seeds.

These are source characters, not a novice-ready field key. A final page would need matched, botanist-approved images of the whole climber, both leaf surfaces, young stem, flower, fruit stalk, unripe and fully ripe fruit, cut fruit and the local comparison species.

### Food, preparation, taste and energy

- VicFlora describes the berry as edible. This supports a food record but does not state a safe amount or show a novice how to decide that a fruit is fully mature.
- The [City of Casey Indigenous Plant Guide](https://www.casey.vic.gov.au/indigenous-plant-guide) says the fruit is eaten when very ripe, usually when it has fallen. This is useful local council guidance but is not a toxicology study or a substitute for botanical confirmation.
- A [Yarra Ranges plant profile](https://www.yarraranges.vic.gov.au/PlantDirectory/Climbers-Creepers/Billardiera-mutabilis) also describes the berry as edible. Its short, generic “Aboriginal use” label does not identify a knowledge holder or establish permission to make a statewide cultural claim.
- Historical descriptions sometimes call apple-berry pleasant or slightly acid. Such descriptions are subjective and may concern a different region or the older broad concept of *B. scandens*. The expected taste is therefore **not established for a verified Victorian *B. mutabilis* sample**, and taste must never be used to confirm identity.
- No defensible species-specific result was located for kilojoules, calories, moisture, protein, fat, carbohydrate, safe serving or allergy frequency in fresh Victorian *B. mutabilis* fruit.

**Inference:** the fruit is small and contains many seeds, so the recoverable food and energy are likely to be modest. That is a practical reason not to spend urgent survival time searching for it, but it is not a measured calorie result and must not be printed as one.

### Lookalike and conservation problem

VicFlora says the distinction between *B. mutabilis* and *Billardiera scandens* is often unclear. It records intermediate plants around Beechworth, the Snowy River and Lake Hume. The main comparative features include:

| Feature | *B. mutabilis* | *B. scandens* |
|---|---|---|
| Habit | Usually slender climber | Often shrubbier |
| Fruit stalk | Slender, about 12–45 mm and lengthening in fruit | Stouter, about 5–12 mm |
| Fruit surface | Usually hairless, rarely sparsely to moderately hairy | Densely covered with soft hairs |
| Leaf margin | Hardly rolled under | More clearly rolled under |

Sources: [VicFlora — *B. mutabilis*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/169cd940-d54d-4f2c-9160-f723f9551d5c), [VicFlora — *B. scandens*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/6c325a2f-c22e-4773-83ae-6c5a2a810385) and [PlantNET — *B. scandens*](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Billardiera~scandens&page=nswfl).

PlantNET also calls *B. scandens* fruit edible, so the located evidence does **not** support calling this a poisoning lookalike. The consequential issue is that *B. scandens* is listed as Endangered under Victoria's Flora and Fauna Guarantee framework. Harvesting a threatened plant, or an uncertain intermediate, is unacceptable. Sources: [Victorian threatened species list](https://www.environment.vic.gov.au/conserving-threatened-species/threatened-list) and [official *B. scandens* assessment](https://bio-prd-naturekit-public-data.s3.ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/species_assessments/Billardiera_scandens_504290.pdf).

### Cultural boundary

The [Surf Coast common apple-berry page](https://scnaturesearch.com.au/plant/Common%20Apple-berry) attributes a stewed-apple taste description to information supplied by the then-named Wathaurung Aboriginal Corporation. The site's [attribution statement](https://www.scnaturesearch.com.au/contact/) confirms that source relationship. The current host is not controlled by Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation, so this should remain labelled as a supplied historical organisational account rather than silently presented as current direct authority. It supplies no statewide name, serving or collection permission.

The older [Australian National Botanic Gardens apple-berry account](https://www.anbg.gov.au/apu/plants/billscan.html) brings together material associated with Wreck Bay and Coranderrk under an older, broad *Billardiera scandens* concept. Its accounts of names, green-fruit roasting, use and taste must not be transferred automatically to modern *B. mutabilis*, to all of Victoria or from one people to another. Any Coranderrk material requires exact historical and contemporary Traditional Owner review before inclusion. The Wreck Bay material is not Victorian evidence.

### Evidence, inference and unknowns

- **Evidence:** the exact Victorian taxon and broad habitat are authoritative; an edible-fruit record exists; the taxonomic boundary with Endangered *B. scandens* can be difficult.
- **Inference:** the small, seed-filled fruit is probably a minor snack rather than useful survival energy.
- **Unknown:** a verified Victorian maturity test, fresh edible yield, energy, safe amount, allergy/toxicology profile, and whether untrained readers can reliably reject *B. scandens* and intermediate plants.
- **Editorial precaution:** no pick or eat instruction until those gaps are addressed. This does not mean the berry is proven poisonous.

### Requirements before any upgrade

1. A Victorian botanist approves a flowering-and-fruiting plate and a conservative no-pick rule for *B. scandens*, intermediates and specimens lacking decisive characters.
2. A food-safety or toxicology reviewer approves the exact edible part, maturity and preparation wording.
3. Any nutritional number is tied to exact taxon, edible portion, wet or dry basis, provenance and sample size; otherwise it remains “not established.”
4. Relevant Traditional Owner organisations determine whether any Coranderrk or other Victorian cultural account is suitable and how it is attributed.
5. A novice test with verified specimens and images produces no unacceptable false positives. No tasting is allowed during the test.

## Kangaroo grass — *Themeda triandra* Forssk.

### Current gate

**Education only / non-harvest.** Kangaroo grass deserves a strong page about ecology, food history and contemporary First Nations-led work. It is not a practical emergency-grain instruction. Small and uneven seed yield, rapid seed shedding, difficult processing, uncertain field food-safety details and the risk of removing seed from vulnerable grasslands outweigh its immediate food value.

### Identity, Victorian range and season — evidence

- VicFlora accepts *Themeda triandra*; *Themeda australis* is a synonym. It is widespread in Victorian grassland and woodland, uncommon in the subalps and Otways, and generally absent from permanently wet, very dry, saline or heavily shaded sites. Flowering is recorded through much of the year, mainly October to March. Source: [VicFlora — *Themeda triandra*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/5c4beccd-3eef-434d-9769-4d187abb91e9).
- The plant is a tufted perennial grass to about one metre, often becoming reddish. Leaves can reach about 50 cm by 5 mm. The drooping, often one-sided inflorescence is about 10–25 cm. A fertile spikelet has a silky brown-haired base and a stout purplish awn about 4–8 cm long that bends twice. Source: VicFlora and [PlantNET — *Themeda triandra*](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Themeda~triandra&page=nswfl).

These features require a proper grass plate. A reddish tussock or a long awn alone is not a safe identification method.

### Food history, preparation and practical return

- The [Australian National Botanic Gardens summary](https://www.anbg.gov.au/apu/plants/themtria.html) records seed being ground and baked. It also says only small amounts ripen together, the seed falls quickly and gathering enough is difficult.
- A 2022 review centred on Dja Dja Wurrung Country describes *T. triandra* as **Buwatji** in that Djaara context and examines its role in Djaara food sovereignty and restoration. It reports low or variable yield, uneven ripening, seed shedding and continuing seed-quality, harvest and germination challenges. DOI: [10.1080/23311932.2022.2153964](https://doi.org/10.1080/23311932.2022.2153964).
- A current [CSIRO account of work with Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation and Melbourne Water](https://www.csiro.au/en/news/All/Articles/2024/January/Wadawurrung-grasslands-nutrition) reports promising nutritional comparisons with whole wheat. The public page does not provide a complete, field-usable kilojoule and macronutrient dataset.
- A carbohydrate study describes the difficulty of collecting enough kangaroo-grass grain for analysis. DOI: [10.1016/j.jcs.2023.103670](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcs.2023.103670); [University of Adelaide record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/531fa445-a06f-4230-9805-a92b50efd711).
- A 2023 food-composition study included *T. triandra*, but used only one composite because seed quantity was limited and deliberately did not map its numerical native-grain rows back to named species in order to protect First Nations interests. The table therefore cannot be mined for a kangaroo-grass calorie figure. DOI: [10.3389/fsufs.2023.1237862](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsufs.2023.1237862); [open full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2023.1237862/full).

**Inference:** a stranded person without specialist harvest and processing knowledge is unlikely to obtain useful energy quickly. This follows from the documented harvesting constraints, but no Victorian emergency field-yield trial was located.

### Contradictions and safety limits

- A [City of Melbourne public profile](https://participate.melbourne.vic.gov.au/floral-emblem/kangaroo-grass) suggests raw snacking as well as flour use. The stronger historical account centres on collected grain being ground and baked, while the reviewed science does not validate raw field eating, a serving or a safe improvised preparation. The book must not resolve that contradiction by choosing the easier advice.
- No adequate species-specific evidence was located for human allergenicity, antinutrients, mycotoxin risk, safe field preparation, serving size or digestible energy from an improvised Victorian harvest.
- Grass seed can be damp, mouldy, immature or contaminated. The present evidence does not provide a simple field check that makes such seed safe.

### Ecological and legal boundary

Kangaroo grass is widespread, but it is also an important component of threatened Victorian native grassland communities. Removing reproductive seed can reduce regeneration even where the individual species is common. Sources: [Australian Government — threatened ecological communities in Victoria](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/environment/biodiversity/threatened/communities/vic) and [Natural Temperate Grassland of the Victorian Volcanic Plain guidance](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/grasslands-victoria.pdf).

The book should therefore use a simple rule: **observe it; do not harvest it for this guide.** This is a conservation and product-safety decision, not a claim that all traditional or cultivated use is harmful.

### Cultural boundary

- DJAARA's [DJAKITJ enterprise](https://djakitj.com.au/) publishes **Buwatj** for *Themeda triandra* on Dja Dja Wurrung Country / Djandak. The separate agronomy paper uses **Buwatji** in its Djaara context. Preserve each source's spelling; do not merge or “correct” either into a statewide word. DJAARA's [resources notice](https://djadjawurrung.com.au/resources/) says its material must not be published without prior consent.
- Wandoon Estate Aboriginal Corporation's [*Plants of Coranderrk* brochure](https://nespurban.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Coranderrk-Plant-Brochure-pdf-002.pdf) records **BUATH** for *Themeda triandra* in the Coranderrk/Woiwurrung record. The food/cultural icons provide no recipe or general permission.
- The CSIRO work cited above is a separate partnership with Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation and Melbourne Water. Its results and purpose must remain attached to that partnership.
- First Nations-led food-sovereignty and restoration research is not a pool of generic harvesting instructions. Any expansion of names, processing or cultural explanation requires agreement with the relevant organisation and a clear benefit and attribution arrangement.

### Evidence, inference and unknowns

- **Evidence:** kangaroo grass has a documented grain-food history; current First Nations-led work is investigating nutritional and production value; uneven ripening, shedding and low harvest yield are real limitations.
- **Inference:** it is a poor emergency calorie source for an untrained person with no equipment.
- **Unknown:** a safe, efficient improvised Victorian field method; fresh field yield per hour; a directly publishable species-specific kilojoule value; safe serving; and novice identification performance.
- **Editorial precaution:** no raw-snacking, harvest, threshing, grinding or cooking instruction. Keep the page educational and non-harvest.

### Requirements before any upgrade

An actionable entry would require relevant Traditional Owner leadership and consent, a botanist-approved grass key and plate, a replicated food-composition result that may lawfully be attributed to the species, a food-safety review of the exact field process, realistic yield testing, conservation review and a lawful supply source. On current evidence, this is a low-priority upgrade because it offers little immediate survival value.

## Coast beard-heath — *Leucopogon parviflorus* (Andrews) Lindl.

### Current gate

**Education only / non-harvest.** The plant has a credible regional food record and a measured Victorian fruit sample, but its tiny fruit, incomplete human safety evidence, difficult genus-level identification and protected-flora setting make generic wild-harvest advice unsuitable.

### Identity, Victorian range and season — evidence

- VicFlora accepts *Leucopogon parviflorus* and describes it as common in dunes and on sandstone or limestone slopes along nearly the whole Victorian coast. It is rarely found more than about half a kilometre inland except in the Glenelg region. Flowering is mainly September to November. Source: [VicFlora — *Leucopogon parviflorus*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/a0c8b368-801a-4fd9-bc60-1a8f39bd01a6).
- It is an erect shrub or small tree about 1–5 m tall. Narrow elliptic to oblanceolate leaves are about 15–35 mm by 3–7 mm, paler or bluish beneath, with a thickened, channelled tip. Short terminal or near-terminal spikes carry about 6–13 white flowers whose lobes are densely bearded inside. Ripe fruit is white, fleshy and spherical, only about 4.5 mm across. Source: VicFlora.
- PlantNET similarly records white to pale-green edible fruit about 4.3–5 mm across. Source: [PlantNET — *Leucopogon parviflorus*](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Leucopogon~parviflorus&page=nswfl).

A white berry and coastal location are not enough. A final educational plate should show habit, both leaf surfaces, the channelled tip, bearded flower, ripe fruit and matched local *Leucopogon* comparisons.

### Food, taste and regional cultural evidence

- South Australian government bush-food identification cards describe the ripe fruit as sweet, eaten raw without cooking, and available there approximately September to February. The same source gives **Ngoor-le** as a Bunganditj name. This is South-East South Australian/Bunganditj evidence; it is not a statewide Victorian calendar, name or permission. Source: [South Australian Aboriginal bush-food identification cards](https://cdn.environment.sa.gov.au/landscape/docs/lc/aboriginal-bush-foods-identification-cards-gen.pdf).
- A Victorian research project collected about 300 g of white berries from one mainland plant near Warrnambool in February 2017 with Martang Djab Wurrung elders involved in the broader work. Fruit was not available for repeat collection in 2019. This provides a useful measured Victorian sample and also shows why one plant in one season cannot establish reliable field availability. Journal article DOI: [10.1111/ijfs.14400](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijfs.14400); [thesis with collection and methods](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/41825/1/NJUME_Collise-thesis_nosignature.pdf).
- A [Surf Coast coast beard-heath page](https://scnaturesearch.com.au/plant/Coastal%20Beard-heath) says its edible-fruit information was supplied by the then-named Wathaurung Aboriginal Corporation. The [site attribution statement](https://www.scnaturesearch.com.au/contact/) confirms the source relationship. As with its apple-berry page, this is a supplied account on an external host, not a current direct Wadawurrung publication or blanket reuse permission.
- “Sweet” is an expected regional taste description after expert identification, not a test for deciding whether an unknown white berry is safe.

The study's public availability does not transfer ownership of cultural knowledge to the guide. Any cultural wording drawn from the Martang Djab Wurrung collaboration requires review of the original consent and publication scope and, for fuller reuse, engagement with the relevant Traditional Owner organisation.

### Nutrition and adequacy

The Victorian study reported fruit at about 72% moisture and, per 100 g dry matter, approximately 13 g fat, 2.6 g protein, 4.2 g fibre and 59.1 g total carbohydrate. These are dry-basis analytical results from a small, single-place, single-season sample. The treatment of the stone when defining the sample is not clear enough for a field edible-portion calculation.

Do **not** convert these figures into a fresh-fruit calorie promise. The study did not directly report a validated energy value for a stone-free field serving, did not show how many plants or how much time would be needed to gather that serving, and was not replicated across Victorian populations or seasons.

**Inference:** fruit about 4.5–5 mm wide is likely to provide only a small snack or morale value, not dependable emergency energy. The inference is practical but not a measured field-yield result.

### Identification, toxicology and legal limits

- Victoria has multiple *Leucopogon* species. The [VicFlora genus account and key](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/c56af135-4b41-483c-b494-b7b5449f81ea) use more than fruit colour, and species such as [*Leucopogon virgatus*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/c1d11fa5-92f8-416f-8034-807ed7a86db7) require close comparison. “White heath berry” is not an edible category.
- No adequate modern species-specific human toxicology, allergy-incidence or safe-serving study was located.
- Victoria's current [Declared Protected Flora List](https://www.environment.vic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0037/719686/Declared-Protected-Flora-List.pdf) places all native Ericaceae, the heaths, in the restricted-use protected-flora category. That designation is not by itself a complete summary of every harvesting offence, but it creates an additional legal and provenance issue that must be reviewed alongside land-tenure rules. It supports a non-harvest rule for a general field guide.

### Evidence, inference and unknowns

- **Evidence:** the exact coastal taxon is well described; ripe fruit has a regional food record; one Victorian analytical sample exists; native heaths have a protected-flora designation.
- **Inference:** its tiny fruit and irregular availability make it a poor calorie target.
- **Unknown:** a safe wild serving, human allergy/toxicology profile, stone-free fresh energy, population variation, dependable Victorian fruiting window and novice false-positive rate.
- **Editorial precaution:** no public-land or generic wild-harvest instruction. This is not a declaration that expert-confirmed fruit used lawfully in its documented cultural or culinary setting is poisonous.

### Requirements before any upgrade

The most that may later be considered is a private/cultivated-plant note: owner permission, a known lawful source, expert confirmation of the plant, a complete lookalike plate and food-safety review. Cultural material remains within its named regional context and requires the relevant organisation's decision about scope and attribution. A general public-land foraging entry should remain out of scope.

## Common purslane — *Portulaca oleracea* L.

### Current gate

**Withhold actionable eating advice.** Purslane is widely described as edible, but this does not make it a good survival-guide plant. It is very low in energy, often grows in contaminated or sprayed places, can be confused with prostrate spurges, contains variable oxalate and nitrate, and lacks a validated field portion and preparation for a dehydrated or medically vulnerable reader.

It can be included only as a short warning example that “edible” is not the same as “safe, useful and identifiable under emergency conditions.”

### Identity, Victorian range and season — evidence

- VicFlora accepts *Portulaca oleracea* but describes it as a variable complex that may include native and introduced components; its Victorian origin is uncertain and doubtfully native. It is common in gardens, paddocks and vacant land and is recorded mainly December to March. Source: [VicFlora — *Portulaca oleracea*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/cd5fc762-22ae-45a5-ab93-02f7cef0a5c0).
- It is a low, spreading annual with reddish succulent stems to about 20 cm, fleshy obovate to wedge-shaped leaves about 5–30 mm by 5–15 mm, small yellow to greenish flowers and a capsule whose top opens like a lid. Seeds are black, tuberculate and about 1 mm.

The unresolved species complex matters: a food result or cultural record for one population cannot automatically be assigned to every plant called common purslane in Victoria.

### Conventional food evidence, taste and energy

- General food and horticultural sources describe stems, leaves and buds as eaten raw or cooked, with a tart, sour or slightly salty taste and a mucilaginous texture after cooking. Sources: [FAO publication](https://openknowledge.fao.org/3/cc5044en/cc5044en.pdf), [University of Wisconsin Extension](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/common-purslane-portulaca-oleracea/) and [North Carolina State Extension](https://plants.ces.ncsu.edu/plants/portulaca-oleracea/common-name/common-purslane/).
- The USDA FoodData Central record for raw purslane reports, per 100 g, 84 kJ or 20 kcal, 92.86 g water, 2.03 g protein, 0.36 g fat and 3.39 g carbohydrate. Source: [USDA FoodData Central — raw purslane](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/169274/nutrients).

The USDA values are a food-composition proxy, not a Victorian wild sample. Even if transferable, 20 kcal per 100 g is very low: a large bulk of succulent plant would be needed for little energy. The guide must not imply purslane is a meaningful survival-calorie solution.

Taste is not an identification test. Soil salinity, water stress, age and preparation can also change sensory properties.

### Oxalate, nitrate and preparation contradictions

- Published studies agree that purslane can contain oxalate, but cooking results are not consistent enough to authorise a field detoxification method. One study found an average reduction with cooking; another found that a short boil did not reduce oxalate. Sources: DOI [10.1016/j.jfca.2009.01.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfca.2009.01.013) and the [Poeydomenge and Savage study record](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/511303/).
- Medical literature explains that high oxalate intake can contribute to kidney injury in susceptible circumstances. A review is available in [Clinical Kidney Journal](https://academic.oup.com/ckj/article/15/2/194/6349203), and [published acute kidney-injury cases involving large purslane meals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10347717/) show a plausible high-intake hazard. Case reports do not establish that a small serving is dangerous, but they also do not define a safe serving.
- Purslane nitrate content can vary with growing conditions. Source: [reviewed composition study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8839399/).

**Evidence:** oxalate and nitrate vary, and the effect of cooking depends on the method and sample.  
**Unknown:** the chemistry of a found Victorian plant, a safe amount for a dehydrated person, a validated boil time, and risk for people with kidney disease, stones or other vulnerabilities.  
**Editorial precaution:** do not offer a serving or promise that boiling makes an encountered plant safe.

### Lookalike and contamination risks

- Spotted spurge, *Euphorbia maculata*, is another low, spreading plant found in disturbed ground. It has thinner opposite leaves, often with hairs and sometimes a reddish blotch, and produces milky latex. Sources: [VicFlora — *Euphorbia maculata*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/c6a76871-1660-41e6-8009-c9de9b0eb8ee) and [Agriculture Victoria — spotted spurge](https://vro.agriculture.vic.gov.au/dpi/vro/vrosite.nsf/pages/weeds_spotted-spurge).
- Spurge ingestion can cause gastrointestinal symptoms, while latex can injure skin and eyes. Absence of visible latex is only a rejection check; it is not positive identification of purslane. The reader must not be told to break an unknown plant with bare hands to test its sap.
- Common purslane often grows on roadsides, vacant land, paddocks and garden edges. Those habitats create realistic herbicide, vehicle, animal-waste, sewage and heavy-metal concerns. A healthy appearance, rain or washing does not prove the plant is uncontaminated.

### Cultural boundary

A colonial-era compilation records purslane seed gathering and baking in Murray/Victorian Aboriginal contexts. Source: [Victorian Aboriginal Life and Customs](https://www.latrobe.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0017/1510109/Victorian-Aboriginal-Life-and-Customs.pdf). This is historical evidence, not contemporary Traditional Owner authority. The source must not be used to assign the practice to an unnamed statewide group, and the variable *P. oleracea* complex makes a direct modern taxonomic transfer uncertain. Any cultural inclusion requires identification of the exact people, Country, source limitations and a decision from the relevant Traditional Owner organisation.

### Evidence, inference and unknowns

- **Evidence:** purslane has a broad food history and a low-energy fresh-food composition record; oxalate and nitrate can vary; a harmful prostrate spurge can occur in similar disturbed habitat.
- **Inference:** the small energy return, high water content and site-screening burden make it a poor emergency food even when identity is correct.
- **Unknown:** the exact lineage and chemistry of a Victorian specimen, safe field portion, effective field preparation, contaminant history and novice lookalike performance.
- **Editorial precaution:** withhold eating, tea and portion advice. This is more defensible than turning a familiar “edible weed” claim into emergency guidance.

## Cross-species findings

| Question | Finding | Book consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Does a historical food record make a plant actionable? | No. It establishes that knowledge and practice existed in a particular setting, not that a novice can identify, prepare and dose any modern specimen safely | Preserve the history and attribution; add action only after identification and food-safety gates |
| Which candidate has the clearest later pathway? | Common apple-berry, limited to fully ripe fruit, has the best potential, but the *B. scandens* conservation overlap remains unresolved for novice use | Education only now; prioritise a botanist-led plate and false-positive test if more plants are developed |
| Which candidate offers the least practical energy? | Purslane has a measured proxy of only 84 kJ/20 kcal per 100 g, while kangaroo-grass seed is difficult to collect in useful quantity and coast beard-heath fruit is tiny | Do not allow “edible” to dominate survival priorities such as rescue, shelter, warmth and water |
| Can cooking be presented as a universal safety fix? | No. Purslane oxalate studies conflict, and no candidate has a validated improvised preparation that controls all biological and site risks | Never use “boil it” as a generic detoxification claim |
| Can cultural knowledge be pooled into a Victorian list? | No. The located evidence belongs to specific Dja Dja Wurrung/Djaara, Wadawurrung, Martang Djab Wurrung, Bunganditj, Coranderrk, Wreck Bay or historical Murray contexts | Keep every name and practice attached to its exact source and seek the appropriate organisation's decision before fuller use |
| Should confidence be printed as a percentage? | No validated probability model exists for field identification or safety | Use plain evidence gaps and gate status; do not invent numerical confidence |

## Release gate for any future actionable plant entry

1. Recheck the current VicFlora taxon, Victorian distribution, conservation listing and land-tenure rules at the edition date.
2. Have a Victorian botanist authenticate a complete local image set: whole plant, growth habit, stem or bark, both leaf surfaces, flower, immature and mature edible structure, cut structure or seed, scale and matched local lookalikes.
3. Define objective field characters and explicit stop rules. Test them with untrained readers using verified specimens or images, without tasting. An unacceptable false positive blocks release.
4. Have a food-safety or toxicology specialist review the exact edible part, maturity, preparation, amount, vulnerable-person exclusions and contamination rules. Missing evidence remains missing.
5. Tie any energy or nutrition value to the exact taxon, edible portion, fresh or dry state, preparation, provenance, sample size and source release. Do not convert dry values or related-species data into an unsupported field number.
6. Confirm lawful source and permission. Protected areas, threatened plants, protected flora, uncertain tenure and contaminated sites are stop conditions.
7. Ask the relevant Traditional Owner organisation to decide whether names, practices and explanation are included and how consent, attribution, Indigenous Cultural and Intellectual Property and benefit are handled.
8. Put the safety gate before any instruction. State evidence, inference and unknowns in plain language. Do not add an invented confidence percentage.
