# EP-007 — Poisoning, eye injury and wounds

**Created:** 4 September 2026  
**Chapter:** 06 First aid and evacuation  
**Dependencies:** 02 First minutes; 03 Emergency communication; EP-003 Critical first aid; EP-004 Trauma first aid; EP-005 Acute medical emergencies  
**Status:** Evidence packet only — not field-ready guidance

## Purpose

Check the evidence and unanswered practical questions about:

- suspected poisoning from swallowing or breathing a substance, or getting it on skin or in eyes;
- preventing the helper from being poisoned too, including by dangerous air;
- chemical eye exposure, loose surface material and suspected penetrating eye injury;
- minor wound cleaning, covering and monitoring;
- embedded objects, dirty/deep/puncture wounds and possible deeper injury;
- wound infection, tetanus risk and deterioration;
- what can be done with the listed equipment, and without it.

The packet does not create poison-specific treatment, field antidotes, eye examination, wound closure, suturing, debridement, antibiotics or tetanus vaccination instructions. Advice from 000 and the Australian Poisons Information Centre on **13 11 26** controls the actual exposure. No sequence below is field-ready until reviewed by the specified Australian specialists and tested using the exact equipment proposed.

## Evidence strengths

### Suspected poisoning

- ANZCOR, the NSW Poisons Information Centre and healthdirect agree that a rescuer first avoids contamination, separates the person from the source only when safe, treats immediate life threats and obtains substance-specific advice promptly. A person who is unresponsive or not breathing normally follows 000/BLS; other suspected exposures should not wait for symptoms before calling 13 11 26. [S-132, S-133, S-134]
- Route matters. Swallowed, inhaled, skin and eye exposures require separate branches. The product/container, label or Safety Data Sheet and the amount, route and time should be preserved or photographed without re-exposing anyone. [S-132, S-133, S-134]
- A swallowed poison is not made to come back up and is not “neutralised” with food, drink, acid, alkali or a household remedy. The person should not eat or drink unless the Poisons Information Centre or emergency service directs it. [S-132, S-133, S-134]
- For inhaled poison, movement to fresh air and ventilation are actions only when they can be done without entering or creating a toxic/flammable atmosphere. More than one affected person is a strong environmental-contamination warning; emergency/fire services may be required. [S-132, S-133]
- Skin decontamination requires avoidance of rescuer contact, careful removal of contaminated clothing and copious water/soap treatment guided by the substance and Poisons advice. Chemical compatibility makes casual bagging, neutralisation and generic glove claims unsafe. [S-132, S-133, S-134]
- If poison remains on the mouth/lips, corrosive burns are present or the agent is unknown, ANZCOR advises chest compressions without mouth-to-mouth ventilation; a trained bag-mask is the safer ventilation method. This needs joint CPR/toxicology review so it cannot be mistaken for all poisonings. [S-132]

### Eye exposure and injury

- Chemical eye exposure requires irrigation immediately. ANZCOR/Poison Centre sources specify 15 minutes; healthdirect specifies 20 minutes; Better Health gives 15–20 minutes; clinical eye guidance continues until the chemical is cleared/neutral under professional assessment. All agree that waiting for kit saline instead of starting with available clean water can worsen injury. [S-093, S-132, S-133, S-136, S-137, S-140]
- Irrigation should keep the eyelids open and prevent contaminated runoff entering the other eye or exposing another person. Contact-lens advice varies with injury severity; a badly injured surface or difficult lens should not be manipulated. [S-136, S-137, S-138]
- Penetrating/open-globe injury or an embedded object is a vision emergency. The object is not removed, the eye is not pressed or forced open, no drops/ointment are added, activity is limited and urgent ophthalmic-capable care is required. [S-136, S-138, S-139, S-140]
- Current clinical sources prefer a rigid shield resting on the bony orbit with nothing pressing the eye for suspected penetration. A soft pad or dressing may transmit pressure; St John's generic light-dressing wording must not control the penetrating branch. [S-138, S-139, S-140]
- A loose superficial speck may sometimes be flushed with clean water/saline, but persistent pain, visual change, chemical/high-velocity mechanism, blood/fluid, inability to open the eye or failed gentle irrigation requires medical assessment. Source advice about touching a visible speck with moist cloth is inconsistent and is not approved for this guide. [S-136, S-138, S-141]

### Wounds and infection

- Life-threatening bleeding is controlled before wound cleaning. Cleaning a heavily bleeding wound may disrupt clotting and delay pressure; an embedded object is left in place and pressure/padding is applied around it. [S-076, S-078, S-141, S-146]
- For a minor, controlled wound, healthdirect, Royal Children's Hospital guidance and the Wilderness Medical Society support early irrigation with clean/potable water or saline, removal of accessible contamination without extending/exploring the wound, and a clean non-stick protective dressing. [S-141, S-142, S-143]
- The WMS guideline finds potable water equivalent to sterile saline for field irrigation and recommends no additives because common antiseptics can harm tissue. Australian public/clinical pages conflict: healthdirect says antiseptic creams are unnecessary, while some tetanus/cellulitis pages suggest antiseptic and RCH permits specific diluted agents for selected contaminated wounds. A generic “pour antiseptic into every wound” instruction is therefore withheld. [S-141, S-142, S-143, S-144]
- Deep, gaping, puncture, bite, dirty-water/soil/manure-contaminated, joint/hand/face/eye/genital wounds, retained foreign bodies, altered movement/feeling/circulation or wounds over possible fracture require early professional assessment. A field guide cannot safely close, probe or debride these. [S-141, S-142, S-143, S-144]
- Tetanus risk depends on wound type and documented immunisation history. Current Australian vaccination guidance requires clinical assessment and, in some cases, vaccine and immunoglobulin. Wound cleaning does not replace tetanus assessment, and the book will not infer immunity from memory or administer a field vaccine. [S-144, S-145]
- Increasing or spreading redness, warmth, swelling, worsening pain, pus/discharge, delayed healing, fever, red streaking or systemic deterioration can indicate infection. Confusion, drowsiness, breathlessness/fast breathing, discoloured skin, severe illness or rapid decline may indicate sepsis and require 000. [S-117, S-147, S-148]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### 000 versus 13 11 26 is a severity branch, not a choice of convenience

Unresponsiveness, abnormal breathing, collapse, seizure, severe breathing difficulty, serious burns or a dangerous scene require 000 and BLS as safe. The Poisons Information Centre gives substance-specific advice and should be called promptly for suspected exposure even without symptoms, but it is not a substitute for emergency activation. A group can make both contacts in parallel; a solo flow must be tested. [S-132, S-133, S-134]

### Decontamination can poison the rescuer

“Remove them from the source” does not authorise entry into a vehicle, tent, shed, mine, industrial site, smoke plume or low area containing an unknown atmosphere. Ordinary nitrile medical gloves are not universal chemical protection, and a wet contaminated casualty or clothing can transfer the agent. If suitable PPE and a safe approach are absent, rescue/decontamination may be impossible until trained services arrive. [S-132]

### Ventilation can ignite a flammable atmosphere

ANZCOR supports opening doors/windows when safe but warns against operating electrical switches/appliances where flammable gas is suspected. The shelter chapter must separately address carbon monoxide and combustion ventilation; the poisoning page cannot imply that entering a contaminated enclosure to open it is safe. [S-132]

### Generic oral remedies are dangerous

Advice varies by exact substance; even ANZCOR's button-battery honey/jam exception has strict age, swallowing and urgency conditions. A universal “drink water/milk,” charcoal, emetic, neutraliser or bush remedy would create harmful transfer. The default is nothing by mouth until current expert direction. Any button-battery inset requires its own three-source packet and paediatric/toxicology review before inclusion. [S-132, S-133, S-134]

### Poison-contaminated CPR changes the ventilation path

ANZCOR's chest-compressions-only caution applies when material remains on the lips, there are corrosive burns or the poison is uncertain; it is not permission to omit breaths after an unrelated tablet overdose with a clean airway. A normal pocket mask may not protect against every chemical. Exact with-kit/no-kit wording requires toxicology and resuscitation sign-off. [S-132]

### Eye irrigation duration is inconsistent

Australian public sources give 15, 15–20 or 20 minutes, while clinical protocols use pH-guided continuation that a lay reader cannot reproduce reliably. The field page can safely say “start now and continue while obtaining Poisons/000 advice,” but it still needs an approved minimum and stopping rule. No duration is field-ready yet. [S-093, S-132, S-133, S-136, S-137, S-140]

### Kit saline is convenient but volume-limited

Small eyewash ampoules cannot provide sustained eye irrigation or thorough dirty-wound irrigation. Carrying them must not create false reassurance or a delay in using available potable water. Quantity, container/nozzle, contamination risk and water reservation for drinking require physical testing. [S-136, S-137, S-141, S-143]

### Chemical eye flushing and penetrating-eye protection are opposite actions

A chemical splash needs immediate copious irrigation and opened lids; a suspected open globe requires no pressure, no forced opening and no manipulation. Mechanism/red-flag recognition must occur before a reader follows an eye diagram. If both chemical and penetration are possible, emergency/ophthalmology advice must resolve the priority rather than a hybrid picture. [S-136, S-137, S-139, S-140]

### Soft eye pads may be unsafe for suspected penetration

St John recommends a light non-pressure dressing for general eye injury, while RCH and NSW clinical guidance specify a rigid shield and no pad/pressure for penetration. The final guide must separate a simple protected eye from an open-globe branch; `KIT-022` is a rigid shield candidate, not an eye pad. [S-138, S-139, S-140]

### Surface-speck removal advice conflicts

healthdirect conditionally permits removal of a visible surface object with a moist cloth; Better Health broadly says not to remove a foreign body; penetrating/high-velocity mechanisms can look deceptively small. The personal guide will prefer gentle irrigation and professional review when symptoms persist, withholding direct cloth/tweezer contact with the eye pending ophthalmology review. [S-136, S-138, S-141]

### Irrigation pressure and volume cannot be casually copied from clinical technique

WMS and RCH support substantial irrigation and describe syringe/nozzle pressures, but very high pressure can damage tissue or drive contamination deeper. A needle-based drawing is an avoidable sharps hazard. The guide needs an exact blunt, tested device and a simple endpoint; no improvised high-pressure bottle puncture is approved. [S-142, S-143]

### Antiseptic advice conflicts and concentration matters

Some Victorian pages suggest antiseptic, while WMS advises no irrigation additives and RCH limits any exception to specific diluted concentrations/selected contamination. Household-strength disinfectant, alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, tea-tree oil or other bush remedies must not be poured into an open wound. Final wording should distinguish hand/surrounding-skin hygiene from wound-bed irrigation. [S-141, S-142, S-143, S-147]

### Removing debris can become unsafe wound exploration

Loose surface material after irrigation is different from a deeply embedded object, possible glass, thorn, tendon injury or foreign body plugging bleeding. A reader must not enlarge/probe a wound or remove an object when bleeding, depth, location or mechanism makes it unsafe. [S-076, S-078, S-141, S-142, S-143]

### Closure can trap contamination and hide injury

WMS discusses field closure for trained wilderness care, but the personal book targets a stressed non-specialist. Sutures, staples, tissue glue and closure strips can seal contamination or deeper damage and complicate later care. They remain outside the initial lay guide until a remote emergency clinician defines a narrow, testable scope; a clean cover is not “closure.” [S-141, S-143]

### Water scarcity does not justify using unsafe water without a rule

Potable water is preferred for wounds. Victorian creek, dam and floodwater can add pathogens or chemicals. The guide cannot yet quantify the trade-off between wound irrigation and drinking supply or validate a field-treated-water method for an open wound. This must join the later water-treatment packet. [S-011, S-141, S-143]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These propositions are tracked in `CONTENT_REGISTER.md`; none is approved reader instruction.

### Poisoning

1. Protect the rescuer, recognise a possible contaminated environment and remove the person/source only when safe.
2. Use 000/BLS for immediate life threats and call 13 11 26 promptly for specific advice without waiting for symptoms.
3. Identify/photograph the agent, route, amount and time without handling it unsafely; preserve the label/container for responders.
4. Do not induce vomiting, neutralise or give food/drink/charcoal unless directed for the exact exposure.
5. Use separate swallowed, inhaled, skin and eye branches; decontaminate only when safe and under current product/Poisons direction.
6. Use a separately reviewed CPR contamination warning when mouth-to-mouth could expose the rescuer.

### Eye injury

1. Begin chemical irrigation immediately with available clean water, keep runoff from the other eye and obtain Poisons/000 advice; exact minimum duration remains unresolved.
2. Do not delay flushing to fetch saline or attempt chemical neutralisation.
3. For suspected penetration/embedded object, do not remove, press, force open or add drops; protect from contact using a reviewed rigid shield and arrange urgent care.
4. Gently irrigate only a clearly superficial loose speck; do not use cloth/tweezers on the eyeball, and escalate persistent or red-flag symptoms.

### Wounds and infection

1. Control serious bleeding before cleaning and do not remove an embedded object.
2. For a minor controlled wound, wash hands/use gloves, irrigate early with potable water or saline, remove only loose accessible contamination under a reviewed method, and apply a clean non-stick cover.
3. Do not pour unapproved antiseptics or household/bush remedies into the wound and do not probe, debride or close a wound in the lay guide.
4. Seek early professional advice for high-risk wounds and tetanus assessment; do not equate cleaning with immunisation.
5. Monitor for local infection and systemic deterioration, escalating rapidly for spreading infection or sepsis signs.

## Required paired treatment paths

### With a first-aid kit

Candidate capability classes, not approved generic inclusions:

- `KIT-001` gloves for ordinary body-fluid protection, while explicitly stating that medical nitrile is not universal chemical PPE;
- the exact chemical-specific PPE and Safety Data Sheet required by a known work/farm product, stored with that product rather than assumed in a generic kit;
- a large-volume, protected eye-irrigation supply/nozzle (`KIT-021`) that can begin immediately and does not create a delay when tap/potable water is closer;
- a purpose-designed rigid eye shield (`KIT-022`) and securement that never presses the globe;
- a blunt, tested wound-irrigation delivery system (`KIT-023`) with adequate potable water/saline and no sharps;
- sterile/clean non-adherent minor-wound dressings and suitable tape (`KIT-024`);
- existing trauma pads/bandages for bleeding (`KIT-003`, `KIT-004`), kept conceptually separate from routine wound covering;
- clean tweezers only if a specialist approves a narrow loose-surface-debris use away from the eye; no item is yet registered for this task;
- communication, light, notebook/time record, thermometer only if its role is validated, and secure access to current medication/immunisation information.

Activated charcoal, emetics, neutralising agents, universal antidotes, eye drops, topical antibiotics, oral antibiotics, local anaesthetic, sutures, staples and tissue glue are not generic kit inclusions.

### Without a first-aid kit

This also applies if the kit is missing the item needed for the condition.

- Poisoning: do not touch/enter when the scene is unsafe; call 000 and/or 13 11 26 through the available communication method, move to clean air only by a safe route, and use the exact available label/product advice. There is no generic oral or bush-remedy substitute.
- Chemical eye: begin with available clean running/poured water rather than waiting for kit saline. Protect the other eye and rescuer from runoff. If clean water is unavailable, do not substitute urine, plant liquid or an unknown natural water source without expert direction.
- Penetrating eye: prevent touching/rubbing and movement, do not manipulate/remove the object, and arrange urgent help. An improvised cup shield is not approved until its material, rim, fixation and crush risk have been tested.
- Minor wound: after bleeding control, use the cleanest demonstrably potable water available, clean hands/material and a non-fluffy clean cover. Do not use creek/floodwater, alcohol, peroxide, ash, sap, plant material or saliva as treatment.
- High-risk wound/infection: communication and evacuation become more important because there is no field substitute for examination, tetanus prophylaxis, imaging, surgical cleaning or antibiotics.

## Visual requirements and prohibitions

Required candidate visuals:

- poison route fork with a dominant “protect yourself” entry and 000 versus 13 11 26 branch;
- label/photo/handover checklist that does not show unsafe container handling;
- chemical-eye runoff direction and immediate-water setup;
- penetrating-eye rigid shield versus prohibited soft pressure/embedded-object removal;
- “bleeding first, cleaning second” wound fork;
- minor-wound irrigation/covering sequence and high-risk wound escalation panel;
- local infection progression and systemic red-flag panel, carefully tested across skin tones.

Prohibitions:

- no rescuer entering an unknown fumes/gas area or touching contaminated clothing bare-handed;
- no vomiting induction, drink/food/charcoal/neutraliser or generic antidote;
- no mouth-to-mouth image over contaminated/corrosive lips;
- no delay-to-fetch-saline image for chemical eyes;
- no eye rubbing, forced eyelid opening, embedded-object removal, soft pressure pad or drop/ointment in suspected penetration;
- no sharp needle, high-pressure improvised nozzle, deep tweezing/probing, wound enlargement, debridement or lay closure;
- no disinfectant, alcohol, peroxide, plant, soil, ash, urine or saliva placed in a wound;
- no visual claim that a dressing or tetanus cleaning prevents infection.

## Validation and review required

- Australian clinical toxicologist/Poison Centre-aligned reviewer resolves route-specific action, oral prohibitions, contaminated CPR and 000/13 11 26 sequencing.
- Australian emergency ophthalmologist/optometrist with ocular-trauma competence resolves chemical duration, contact lenses, superficial specks, shield method and evacuation urgency.
- Australian remote/wilderness emergency clinician and wound-care specialist resolve irrigation device/volume, debris limits, covering, infection and evacuation thresholds.
- Australian immunisation clinician/pharmacist reviews tetanus wording against the current Australian Immunisation Handbook.
- Resuscitation instructor reviews the contaminated-mouth ventilation branch.
- Physical tests use the exact water container, irrigation nozzle, shield, tape and dressings with gloves, wind, low light, one-handed operation and limited water. No chemical or penetrating injury is simulated on a person.
- Images are reconstructed with inert props, measured against approved technique and independently checked; clinical source images are not copied without permission.
- Final cross-chapter review aligns burns, poisoning, water treatment, bleeding, eye, infection and evacuation instructions.

## Decision

Evidence packet complete. Core propositions may advance only to specialist review drafts. The following remain excluded: unsafe-scene entry, generic oral poison remedies, button-battery treatment without its own packet, a fixed eye-flush duration, soft padding of a suspected penetrating eye, direct cloth/tweezer contact with the eyeball, untested high-pressure irrigation, wound-bed antiseptics, field closure, antibiotics and any plant/bush treatment.
