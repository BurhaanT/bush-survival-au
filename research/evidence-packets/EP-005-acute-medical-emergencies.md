# EP-005 — Acute medical emergencies

**Created:** 4 September 2026  
**Chapter:** 06 First aid and evacuation  
**Dependencies:** 02 First minutes; 03 Emergency communication; 04 Stay or move; 05 Temperature and shelter; EP-003 Critical first aid; EP-004 Trauma first aid  
**Status:** Evidence packet only — not field-ready guidance

## Purpose

Check the evidence and unanswered questions about these illnesses when medical help is far away:

- an asthma attack;
- suspected stroke or a transient ischaemic attack (temporary stroke-like symptoms);
- seizure;
- suspected heart attack/chest pain;
- serious illness, shock and possible sepsis;
- diabetic emergencies, especially hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar).

The aim is not to teach diagnosis. It is to help the reader recognise danger, use the person's existing medical plan and medicine correctly, avoid common harmful actions and get help early. A medicine cannot enter the general kit until we check when to use it, legal supply, storage, reasons not to use it, required training and exact instructions.

No sequence below is approved as reader-facing treatment. Every final entry must use `book/MEDICAL_ENTRY_TEMPLATE.md`, present both kit and no-kit paths, and receive review by qualified Australian clinicians with relevant specialty and remote-care competence.

## Evidence strengths

### Acute asthma

- ANZCOR, the National Asthma Council Australia and healthdirect agree that the person's current Asthma Action Plan and reliever control. Severe or rapidly worsening breathing difficulty, inability to speak comfortably, blue lips, little/no reliever response or deterioration requires immediate 000. [S-105, S-106, S-107]
- For a blue/grey reliever puffer when there is no plan, the Australian first-aid sequence is comfortable upright positioning, four separate puffs preferably through a spacer with four breaths per puff, wait four minutes, repeat, then 000 if not improving and continue while awaiting help. Severe signs trigger 000 immediately rather than after the cycle. [S-105, S-106, S-107]
- ANZCOR's short page summary says to seek medical aid when there is no improvement “within 5 minutes”, but its detailed Australian first-aid table uses a four-minute wait and four-minute repeat cycle. The National Asthma Council and healthdirect independently use four minutes. D-065 and IM-201 therefore follow the specific table and the two corroborating sources, record the mismatch, and do not present the interval as evidence-settled. [S-105, S-106, S-107]
- Not every reliever is a blue/grey metered-dose puffer. The National Asthma Council provides separate current instructions for combination and other inhalers. A single “four puffs” graphic must not be transferred to every device. [S-106]
- When asthma and anaphylaxis are both possible in a person with known asthma and a known severe allergy who has severe sudden breathing symptoms after possible contact with something they are severely allergic to, current Australian sources prioritise the person’s current adrenaline product before the asthma reliever. Skin symptoms are not required. “Autoinjector” is no longer a safe universal term because the 2026 Australian landscape includes nasal adrenaline. [S-088, S-105, S-149, S-482]

### Suspected stroke

- ANZCOR and the Stroke Foundation support FAST as a practical recognition tool: face, arms, speech, time to call 000. The call remains urgent even when symptoms are brief or resolve. [S-108, S-109, S-110]
- FAST captures common signs, not every presentation. Sudden vision loss, severe abrupt headache, balance loss/fall, numbness/weakness and swallowing difficulty can also occur. The guide must not use a negative FAST result to clear a person. [S-108, S-109, S-110]
- ANZCOR says not to give food or drink because swallowing may be impaired. A trained glucose measurement can inform an overlapping hypoglycaemia possibility, but it cannot delay 000. [S-108]

### Seizure

- ANZCOR, Epilepsy Action Australia and healthdirect converge on staying with the person, timing the event, removing hazards, protecting the head with something soft/flat, not restraining, and putting nothing in the mouth. [S-111, S-112, S-113]
- Once practical after convulsive movement stops, an unresponsive breathing person uses the recovery position with frequent airway/breathing checks. Do not give food or drink until fully awake and alert. [S-111, S-112]
- Across the three sources, ambulance triggers include a seizure over five minutes, another seizure beginning, failure to regain consciousness, first/unknown seizure, an unknown person or no plan, injury, water, pregnancy, departure from the person's plan/usual pattern, food/fluid/vomit in the mouth, breathing concern or any doubt; healthdirect additionally names diabetes. The exact lists differ and require clinical reconciliation. [S-111, S-112, S-113]
- Emergency seizure medicine is not a generic kit action. Only a trained carer following the person's current management/medication plan may use the person's prescribed medicine. [S-111, S-112]

### Suspected heart attack or serious chest pain

- A heart attack may occur without chest pain; shortness of breath, nausea, sweating, dizziness or discomfort in the chest, arm, shoulder, jaw, neck or back can be relevant. Older people, women, people with diabetes and First Nations people may report atypical or minimal symptoms. [S-114, S-115, S-116]
- Australian sources agree that this is time critical: stop activity, rest in a comfortable position, call 000 at the urgent threshold, follow operator instructions, assist only with the person's prescribed angina medicine as directed, stay with them and prepare for CPR/AED if they deteriorate. The person should not drive themselves. [S-114, S-115, S-116]
- ANZCOR suggests 300 mg aspirin for adults with non-traumatic chest pain unless there is known anaphylaxis to aspirin, but grades this weak with very-low-certainty evidence. healthdirect adds “unless a doctor has told you not to.” This is not yet a sufficient lay contraindication screen for a generic bush kit. [S-114, S-116]

### Serious illness, shock and possible sepsis

- Current ANZCOR deliberately prioritises recognising that someone is seriously unwell over identifying a precise cause. Its management threshold is direct: phone 000 if there is any concern that serious illness may be present or if the person, a parent, another carer or the first aider thinks something is wrong. The observable signs help recognition but are not prerequisites for the call. [S-117]
- ANZCOR and healthdirect treat “shock” as a consequence with multiple causes, including blood loss, heart problems, severe infection, anaphylaxis, dehydration, burns and spinal injury. The known cause should be treated under its own entry while the person is rested, temperature is protected appropriately, and breathing/consciousness is monitored. [S-117, S-118, S-119]
- Current Australian sources explicitly say not to use routine leg elevation. ANZCOR supports lying down if comfortable, otherwise bringing a chair rather than making the person walk. Recovery position/CPR follows consciousness and breathing. [S-117, S-118, S-119]

### Diabetic emergency

- When available and the user is trained, a blood glucose meter improves recognition. If a person with diabetes reports or shows possible hypoglycaemia and can follow commands and swallow safely, ANZCOR recommends swallowed glucose; 15–20 g glucose tablets are the preferred measurable option, followed by reassessment and a further dose when still indicated. [S-120]
- Diabetes Australia and healthdirect corroborate rapid treatment, a roughly 15-minute reassessment and longer-acting carbohydrate after recovery, while demonstrating that confectionery/drink quantities vary. [S-121, S-122]
- Drowsiness, inability to swallow, seizure or unconsciousness removes the oral path. Use 000, airway/recovery-position or CPR as indicated. A trained carer may use the person's prescribed glucagon according to their plan/product. [S-120, S-121, S-122]
- A first aider should not alter an insulin pump. If glucose is unknown, ANZCOR says to manage suspected low blood glucose rather than high, but oral glucose still requires safe swallowing and the stroke overlap still requires immediate 000. Hyperglycaemia and illness require the person's plan and professional assessment, not improvised insulin. [S-120]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### A personal plan outranks a generic field sequence—but must be current and present

Asthma, seizure and diabetes care may depend on an individual action plan, exact medicine and trained companion. The book can prompt the reader to locate/follow these; it cannot safely recreate every plan. Preparation pages must require an accessible paper copy, medicine/device match, expiry/storage check and companion practice. [S-105, S-106, S-111, S-112, S-120, S-121]

### Asthma devices cannot share a hybrid instruction

The familiar 4-by-4-by-4 sequence applies to the Australian blue/grey-puffer pathway. Combination relievers and other inhalers have distinct device and dosing instructions. IM-201 begins with the person's current plan and exact reliever, fences the blue/grey method from other relievers and contains no product-identification art. That boundary remains subject to respiratory, pharmacist/device, first-aid trainer and human-factors review. [S-105, S-106, S-107]

### Asthma upright positioning conflicts with anaphylaxis positioning

Asthma first aid places the person comfortably upright. Anaphylaxis guidance says never let the person stand/walk and generally keeps them flat, with ground sitting and legs outstretched when breathing is difficult. When a person with known asthma and a known severe allergy has severe sudden breathing symptoms after possible contact with something they are severely allergic to, adrenaline comes first. IM-191 supplies the recognition/immediate-action cross-reference, IM-192 owns the different anaphylaxis positions and IM-193 owns the product-neutral adrenaline-before-asthma branch; all remain unapproved pending joint respiratory/allergy review. [S-087, S-088, S-105, S-149, S-482]

### “Wait four minutes” must not delay an emergency call

The mild/moderate blue-grey pathway includes waiting four minutes, but severe signs or rapid worsening require 000 immediately. ANZCOR's summary says “within 5 minutes”, while its detailed Australian table, the National Asthma Council and healthdirect use four minutes; IM-201 uses the detailed, multiply corroborated four-minute cycle and exposes the inconsistency for expert review. In a bush setting, establishing communications may itself take time. The card branches on severity before the first timed cycle and allows a second person to call while treatment starts. [S-105, S-106, S-107]

### FAST is a trigger, not a clearance test

FAST is memorable and useful but does not capture every stroke. A person with sudden balance, vision, numbness or severe-headache symptoms may still need 000 even if the three FAST tasks appear normal. The guide must avoid an “all clear” outcome. [S-108, S-109, S-110]

### Stroke and hypoglycaemia can resemble one another

Both may produce weakness, slurred speech, confusion or altered consciousness. ANZCOR supports blood-glucose measurement when trained/available, but stroke activation must not wait. Oral glucose is prohibited when the person cannot swallow safely, and a possible stroke may itself impair swallowing. The final overlap box needs joint stroke/diabetes clinical review. [S-108, S-120]

### Seizure side positioning during versus after convulsions is easy to misread

ANZCOR says to lay the person down/turn on the side when practical and clearly uses recovery position after the seizure for an unresponsive breathing person. Epilepsy Action emphasises rolling after a tonic-clonic seizure, or earlier if food/fluid/vomit is in the mouth. healthdirect says during the seizure if safe. The guide should not instruct a novice to wrestle a convulsing person onto their side. Exact exception wording requires epilepsy/emergency review and handling testing. [S-111, S-112, S-113]

### A five-minute threshold does not mean “do nothing until five minutes”

Timing, safety, airway observation and plan retrieval begin immediately. Water, serious injury, breathing difficulty, pregnancy, first/unknown seizure or a plan-specific trigger can require an earlier call. In remote Victoria, inability to establish rapid contact may justify activating available rescue communications before the threshold, but that inference requires clinician/SAR review. [S-111, S-112, S-113]

### Heart-attack call timing is presented inconsistently

ANZCOR says call when warning signs are severe, worsen quickly or last longer than ten minutes. The Heart Foundation page says both “call immediately” for warning signs and, in its action-plan section, call if worse or not better after ten minutes. A remote guide must not create a ten-minute barrier when symptoms are severe or access is delayed. Ambulance Victoria/emergency-cardiology review should set one conservative field rule. [S-114, S-115, S-116]

### Aspirin may help, but a one-line screen may harm

ANZCOR's aspirin suggestion applies only to adults with non-traumatic chest pain and is weak/very-low-certainty. The selected public pages do not jointly provide a complete screen for bleeding, prior medical advice, medicine interactions or diagnostic mimics. Aspirin remains withheld from the generic kit and reader instructions until an Australian emergency/cardiology clinician and pharmacist approve exact inclusion, exclusion and no-kit wording. [S-114, S-116]

### “Shock position” and routine legs-up advice are rejected

ANZCOR says to assist a conscious person to lie down, or bring a chair if they are uncomfortable, unable or do not want to lie down; healthdirect and St John explicitly say not to raise the legs. The position still changes with breathing difficulty, vomiting, pregnancy, anaphylaxis, heat illness, spinal injury and other causes. There will be no universal shock silhouette. [S-087, S-097, S-117, S-118, S-119]

### Routine drinks for “shock” are not adequately supported

St John conditionally suggests small amounts of cool water for a conscious person without abdominal trauma or likely immediate operation. Selected current ANZCOR guidance does not include a generic oral-fluid instruction, and stroke, altered swallowing, nausea/vomiting, surgery, burns and heat illness create conflicting cases. The book will not tell readers to give water for undifferentiated shock pending clinician review. [S-108, S-117, S-119]

### Keep warm is not correct when the person is already hot

ANZCOR says maintain body temperature but not to add a blanket when the person feels hot, and to remove excess clothing when very hot. This must cross-reference heat illness rather than treat pallor or collapse as automatic permission for insulation. Direct heat is not used. [S-058, S-117, S-119]

### Oral sugar is conditional on safe swallowing

Possible hypoglycaemia can make a person confused and hard to direct. The strong boundary is not merely “conscious”: they must follow simple commands and swallow safely. Nothing is placed in the mouth of someone drowsy, seizing, unresponsive or unable to swallow. [S-120, S-121, S-122]

### Food examples are less reliable than measured glucose

Brand and serving sizes change, and “some jelly beans” is not a stable dose. The with-kit path should prefer a labelled glucose quantity if the item is approved. An ordinary-food fallback needs quantity verification and must be regenerated when products change. [S-120, S-121]

### Glucagon is personal, trained and product-specific

ANZCOR describes one injected product, but devices, preparation, dose, storage and effectiveness limitations can differ. The generic guide must not create a universal glucagon drawing or encourage use by an untrained bystander. [S-120, S-122]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These propositions are tracked in `CONTENT_REGISTER.md`; none is approved reader instruction.

### Acute asthma

1. Follow the person's current Asthma Action Plan and exact reliever; severe/rapidly worsening signs trigger immediate 000.
2. If there is no plan and the device is a recognised blue/grey puffer, retain IM-201 as a visibly unapproved, text-led Australian 4-by-4-by-4 working card until respiratory, pharmacy/device, first-aid trainer and human-factors review is complete.
3. Show combination/other inhalers only through their current device-specific plan or separately reviewed chart, never a hybrid dose picture.
4. When a person with known asthma and a known severe allergy has severe sudden breathing symptoms after possible contact with something they are severely allergic to, prioritise their current adrenaline product, then the asthma reliever/plan; show no adrenaline mechanics in the asthma panel.

### Suspected stroke

1. Use FAST to trigger immediate 000, including when signs resolve, while stating that other sudden neurological signs may still be stroke.
2. Record symptom onset/last-known-well time if known, monitor airway/breathing and give nothing by mouth.
3. Use blood glucose measurement only when trained/available and without delaying 000; no negative FAST or glucose result clears the person.

### Seizure

1. Stay, time, remove hazards, protect the head and follow the person's plan.
2. Do not restrain or place anything in the mouth; use recovery position when practical after convulsions for an unresponsive breathing person.
3. Make the five-minute, repeat, non-recovery, water, pregnancy, first/unknown, injury, breathing and plan-departure ambulance triggers visible.
4. Use prescribed emergency medicine only under the person's current plan and prior training.

### Suspected heart attack/chest pain

1. Recognise that chest pain is not required; stop activity, rest, call 000 at the final reviewed threshold and do not self-drive.
2. Assist with the person's prescribed angina medicine only as directed; stay, monitor and prepare for CPR/AED.
3. Withhold generic aspirin instructions until exact adult indication and contraindication wording passes clinical/pharmacy review.

### Serious illness/shock

1. Teach clusters of serious deterioration and early 000 rather than requiring a lay diagnosis of shock or sepsis.
2. Check response and normal breathing; use the matching condition position. Only if the person is conscious and no condition-specific position applies, help them lie down if comfortable or bring a chair. Match temperature protection to what you find and keep observing.
3. Do not raise legs routinely, use direct heat or offer fluid for undifferentiated shock.

### Diabetic emergency

1. Follow the person's plan; measure blood glucose only when trained/available and without delaying care.
2. Give a measured fast glucose only when suspected hypoglycaemia and the person can follow commands and swallow safely; reassess under reviewed timing.
3. Give nothing by mouth when drowsy, seizing, unresponsive or unable to swallow; use 000, recovery position/CPR and prescribed glucagon only if trained.
4. Do not alter another person's pump or improvise insulin dosing for high glucose.

## Required paired treatment paths

### With a first-aid kit

Candidate capability classes, not approved generic inclusions:

- the individual person's current Asthma Action Plan, exact reliever inhaler and compatible spacer, with trained companion use;
- the individual person's Seizure Management Plan and prescribed emergency medicine only where a named companion is trained;
- the individual person's Diabetes Management Plan, blood glucose meter/strips/lancets, measured 15–20 g glucose portions and prescribed glucagon where trained;
- the individual person's prescribed angina medicine and action instructions;
- an AED where carriage is realistic and already approved under `KIT-011`;
- watch/timer, written observation record, communication and lighting;
- suitable insulation and shelter without automatic warming of a hot person;
- a possible 300 mg aspirin capability only if later approved as a separate, tightly controlled kit entry.

Medicines are not made generic merely by placing them in a “comprehensive” kit. Every approved medicine/device requires exact indication, owner/prescription status, legal supply, dose, contraindications, interactions, storage/temperature, expiry, plan match, training and review.

### Without a first-aid kit

This also applies if the kit is missing the item needed for the condition.

- Asthma: upright comfort, 000 and airway/CPR care remain, but there is no improvised bronchodilator and no universal no-spacer or improvised-spacer technique. IM-201 does not invent one; any fallback must be separately tested and clinically approved. Smoke avoidance is not a substitute for reliever medicine.
- Stroke: FAST/other sudden-sign recognition, immediate 000, no food/drink, monitoring and recovery position/CPR as indicated do not require a kit. A reader does not delay the call while seeking a glucose meter.
- Seizure: environmental hazard removal, a folded soft item under the head, timing, staying, no restraint/no mouth objects, recovery position and breathing checks can use ordinary resources. There is no improvised emergency antiseizure medicine.
- Heart attack: stop, rest, 000, staying/monitoring and CPR if required remain possible. Do not substitute bush remedies or another person's prescription medicine. Aspirin is not assumed available or safe.
- Serious illness/shock: make safe, call, check response and normal breathing, and use the matching condition position. Only if the person is conscious and no condition-specific position applies, help them lie down if comfortable or bring a chair. Match temperature protection to what you find and keep observing. There is no generic “shock drink,” stimulant or legs-up substitute.
- Suspected hypoglycaemia: a verified ordinary sugary item may be a fallback only if the person can follow commands and swallow safely; serving quantity must match the reviewed glucose target. Nothing by mouth when swallowing is unsafe. There is no improvised glucagon or insulin.

### D-069 serious-deterioration-card working exception

IM-205 is a working review object, not an exception to the field-ready gate. Its reader-facing rule is: **Call 000 if there is any concern that serious illness may be present, or if the person, a parent, another carer or the first aider thinks something is wrong.** The reader does not need a lay diagnosis of shock or sepsis, a severe sign or a complete sign set. Its signs are examples, not a checklist: an omitted sign, normal device number or apparent short-term stability cannot clear the person. It must preserve immediate exits for current danger, abnormal or uncertain breathing and life-threatening bleeding, and route known causes to their existing canonical actions rather than reproduce treatment mechanics. [S-117, S-169–S-171, S-481]

The generic position applies only to a conscious person when no condition-specific position controls: lie down if comfortable, otherwise bring a chair. It is not a universal shock pose, routine legs-up instruction or permission to walk, carry, drive or self-evacuate. The recovery-position link is limited to an adult or child aged one year or older; infant side-position mechanics remain withheld. The card sets no lone-rescuer priority when abnormal breathing and life-threatening bleeding occur together. [S-117–S-119, S-170]

Temperature protection must match what is found: no automatic blanket for a person who is very hot and no direct heat. The project withholds a generic food-or-drink route because current ANZCOR supplies no such instruction and cross-condition swallowing and diagnosis conflicts remain. ANZCOR's conditional oxygen action is also withheld because IM-205 supplies no reviewed oxygen product, device or training route. Kit contents, personal plans and prescribed items enter only through the matching condition section, exact instructions or 000. There is no generic food, drink, medicine, oxygen, stimulant, bush remedy, measurement threshold, evacuation method or fixed all-clear time. [S-057, S-058, S-117–S-119]

The card remains blocked on Australian emergency, paediatric, resuscitation and remote-care review plus false-negative recognition, hot/cold, vomiting, anaphylaxis, spinal, infant, simultaneous-life-threat, connected/failed-contact, complete-card/detached-panel, greyscale and stressed-reader testing. Its 95%/75%/60% figures estimate wording, cross-condition boundaries and layout only; they are not probabilities of diagnosis, safety, treatment, recovery or survival. [S-117–S-119, S-169–S-171, S-481]

### D-063 seizure-card working exception

IM-199 is a working review object, not an exception to the field-ready gate. It may show only the shared, bounded actions supported by S-111–S-113: stay, start timing, remove nearby hazards, move only from immediate danger, use soft flat head protection, do not restrain, do not force the mouth or put anything in it, and do not force a side roll during convulsions. It must say that five minutes is one 000 trigger, not a period of inactivity when another trigger already applies.

After movements stop, the card may route a person who is unresponsive **and** not breathing normally—including gasping, absent or uncertain breathing—to the canonical CPR/AED cards, and an unresponsive person breathing normally to the existing recovery-position card. It may show that a responsive person should rest, be checked for injury and remain watched, but it may not supply a fixed all-clear or release-to-self-care time. Nothing is given by mouth until the person is fully awake and alert.

The kit branch may name a timer, current plan and soft flat padding. Prescribed rescue medicine is shown only as a trained-carer/current-plan/exact-product action. The no-kit branch has the same safety, call and breathing decisions and no improvised antiseizure medicine. IM-199 supplies no medicine dose, route, device or administration picture; no water-entry/rescue method; no forced-roll method; no remote self-movement rule; and no substitute for 000 or the person's plan.

Medical visuals are Level 3 claims. AI-generated device, dosing, patient-position or symptom illustrations will not be accepted as evidence and cannot enter the usable edition without exact specialist verification.
