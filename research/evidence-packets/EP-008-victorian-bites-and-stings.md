# EP-008 - Victorian bites, stings and animal exposures

**Created:** 4 September 2026  
**Chapter:** 06 First aid and evacuation  
**Dependencies:** 02 First minutes; 03 Emergency communication; EP-003 Critical first aid; EP-006 Choking and drowning; EP-007 Poisoning, eye injury and wounds  
**Status:** Evidence packet only - not field-ready guidance

## Purpose

Check the evidence, whether it applies in Victoria and unanswered questions about:

- spider bites, including suspected funnel-web or mouse spider, redback and other spiders;
- ticks, bee/wasp/ant stings and other uncomplicated insect bites or stings;
- when pressure immobilisation (a specific bandaging method plus keeping still) applies to different venomous bites or stings;
- Victorian marine stings and bites, including bluebottles, uncertain jellyfish, fish/stingray injury, blue-ringed octopus, cone shell and sea snake;
- bat contact and Australian bat lyssavirus exposure;
- other mammal bites with broken skin;
- what can be done with the listed equipment, and without it.

This packet does not approve species identification by photograph, antivenom, wildlife capture, tick removal with forceps, contact water rescue, wound closure or improvised medicines. The safest first aid differs sharply by creature: pressure immobilisation, cold, heat, vinegar, wound care or deliberate non-removal may each be correct in a narrow branch and harmful in another. No sequence below is field-ready until reviewed by the specified Australian specialists and tested using the exact equipment proposed.

## Evidence strengths

### Universal triage and pressure immobilisation

- Across Australian sources, immediate priorities remain scene safety, removal from continuing exposure when safe, assessment of response/breathing, 000 for collapse, abnormal breathing, severe symptoms or likely dangerous envenomation, and CPR when required. Anaphylaxis follows the separate current anaphylaxis plan. [S-150, S-154, S-158, S-160, S-161]
- ANZCOR restricts pressure immobilisation technique (PIT) to Australian venomous snakes including sea snakes, funnel-web spider, blue-ringed octopus and cone shell. It specifically excludes other spider bites including redback, jellyfish, fish/stonefish, scorpion, centipede and beetle stings. Arterial tourniquets, cutting and venom suction are not substitutes. [S-159]
- Victorian and St John sources add mouse spider to the funnel-web-style PIT branch because of potential severe envenomation and identification uncertainty. This local extension needs toxicology review against the narrower wording of ANZCOR's PIT list. [S-151, S-153, S-161]
- A photo can assist later identification only if it causes no delay or risk. ANZCOR expressly warns against live capture of a suspected funnel-web spider; a Victorian page's older “catch if possible” wording must not control the guide. [S-150, S-151]

### IM-189 boundary — 29 September 2026

Under D-050, IM-189 is a snakebite-specific, text-led working decision card and pressure-immobilisation concept map. It is not approved technique art and cannot be transferred as a generic PIT procedure to spiders, ticks, insects or marine injuries. Its local-first two-bandage wording is one ANZCOR-permitted snakebite method, not a proven best method; exact pressure, unknown-site, upper-limb, self-application, no-kit and lone/no-contact mechanics remain withheld.

The future creature-selection matrix and PIT yes/no panel must decide the treatment branch before any condition-specific technique reference appears. They must not make the presence of IM-189 look like permission to use PIT for redback/other spiders, ticks, insects, jellyfish, fish/stingray, scorpion or centipede. D-050 does not approve those selection visuals or any exact PIT technique, and `FIELD_READY_BUILD=NO` remains controlling.

### Spiders in Victoria

- Sydney funnel-web spiders are not found in Victoria. Victorian funnel-web species have not been implicated in fatalities or serious illness in the Museum Victoria sources reviewed, while mouse spiders can resemble them and at least one serious mouse-spider poisoning has been recorded. This makes both alarmist transfer from Sydney and casual visual dismissal unsafe. [S-151, S-152]
- ANZCOR, Victorian Health and St John agree that a suspected funnel-web bite is an emergency requiring stillness, 000, PIT and observation for deterioration/CPR. Victorian and St John sources apply the same action to suspected mouse-spider bite. [S-150, S-151, S-153]
- Redback and other spider bites use local cold for pain rather than PIT. Severe or persistent symptoms need medical assessment; children and vulnerable people require a low threshold. [S-150, S-151, S-153]
- The popular claim that white-tailed spider bites commonly cause tissue necrosis is not supported by the current sources reviewed. Worsening skin damage still requires medical assessment without assuming the spider was the cause. [S-150, S-151, S-152]

### Ticks and insects

- In Australasia, tick management differs from much overseas advice because disturbing a live tick can increase allergen-containing saliva and provoke anaphylaxis. ANZCOR, ASCIA and healthdirect prefer rapid freezing of an adult tick in place with an appropriate ether-containing product and allowing it to drop off, or leaving it in place for urgent professional removal. [S-154, S-155, S-156]
- Do not squeeze, twist, scratch, burn or coat a tick with methylated spirits, kerosene, petroleum jelly, oil, nail polish or another improvised chemical. Do not apply a cold pack over an attached tick. [S-154, S-155, S-156]
- Current ASCIA and healthdirect advice says not to insert tweezers under an attached tick. ANZCOR includes a narrow remote fallback using the finest forceps when freezing and consultation are impossible and there is no known tick anaphylaxis. That unresolved contradiction is too consequential for a generic no-kit removal instruction. [S-154, S-155, S-156]
- ANZCOR advises against freezing or permethrin close to the eye, genitals or inside the ear canal. Larval/nymph and adult branches also differ. A stressed lay reader may not distinguish them reliably, so those branches need specialist review rather than a single tick picture. [S-154]
- Tick anaphylaxis can occur without known previous susceptibility. Weakness, unsteadiness, unusual sleepiness, facial weakness, double vision or swallowing/breathing difficulty can indicate paralysis or serious illness and require urgent assessment. [S-154, S-155, S-156]
- For a visible bee sting, prompt removal without squeezing the venom sac is preferred; scraping with a fingernail or card is a practical method. Wasps and ants may sting repeatedly and may leave no sting. Local cold with a barrier can reduce uncomplicated pain/swelling, while mouth/eye stings, multiple facial/tongue stings or systemic symptoms require urgent care. [S-154, S-157]

### Marine envenomation in Victorian waters

- Remove the person from the water without creating another drowning victim, assess breathing, remain with them, monitor for deterioration and arrange urgent help for severe, extensive, uncertain or high-risk marine envenomation. Aquatic rescue method remains governed by EP-006. [S-158, S-160]
- There is no safe universal jellyfish treatment. Bluebottle and other clearly non-tropical localised stings use seawater/tentacle removal, no rubbing, no vinegar and heat for pain with a cold-pack fallback. Potentially lethal jellyfish use a different emergency branch in which 000/CPR/monitoring dominate and vinegar may be considered. [S-158, S-160, S-161]
- Potentially lethal jellyfish are primarily tropical, but ANZCOR records Irukandji syndrome in swimmers as far south as Port Phillip Bay and notes serious stings farther south than the usual tropical distribution. A small or initially mild sting can be followed 5-40 minutes later by severe generalised pain, vomiting, sweating, restlessness, breathing difficulty or collapse. Victoria therefore cannot use location alone to dismiss a severe or evolving sting. [S-158]
- ANZCOR's bluebottle method uses seawater, no rubbing/vinegar and water at 43-45 C for up to 30 minutes without causing a burn; healthdirect and St John commonly state hot water no hotter than easily tolerated for 20 minutes. Exact temperature, duration and a safe no-thermometer rule need clinical and physical validation. [S-158, S-160, S-161]
- Fish and stingray injuries can combine venom, major bleeding, penetrating trauma and retained barbs. An embedded object is not removed; bleeding/internal-injury priorities control, PIT is not used, heat is for pain in a suitable limb injury only, and hospital assessment is required. [S-158, S-160]
- Blue-ringed octopus and cone-shell envenomation can cause progressive respiratory paralysis after a small or painless injury. The person is kept still and reassured, 000 is called, PIT is applied when possible and CPR may need to continue for a prolonged period. A paralysed person may still hear. [S-158, S-159, S-160, S-161]
- A sea-snake bite is treated through the Australian snakebite/PIT pathway after safe removal from water, not through a generic marine-sting pathway. [S-158, S-159]

### Bats and other mammals

- Any Australian bat may carry Australian bat lyssavirus (ABLV). An untrained/unvaccinated person should not handle a bat or flying fox, even when injured. Wildlife handling equipment does not make an untrained rescuer safe. [S-164, S-166, S-167, S-168]
- A bat bite, scratch, saliva contamination of broken skin or the eyes/nose/mouth, or direct contact where a bite/scratch cannot be confidently excluded requires immediate cleansing and urgent medical assessment for post-exposure prophylaxis, even if the wound is small or the person was previously vaccinated. Do not wait for symptoms. [S-164, S-165, S-166, S-167]
- Victorian and national guidance specifies thorough washing with soap and copious water for about/at least 15 minutes, then a virucidal iodine or ethanol preparation if available; mucous membranes are flushed with copious water. This is a pathogen-specific exception to EP-007's generic prohibition on putting antiseptic into wounds. [S-164, S-165, S-166, S-167]
- Bites from other mammals that break skin require early medical assessment because of infection, deeper-structure and tetanus risks. Serious bleeding is controlled first; minor controlled wounds follow EP-007 irrigation/covering while awaiting care. [S-145, S-167]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### The correct treatment is creature-specific

PIT, cold, heat, vinegar and ordinary wound irrigation are not interchangeable “bite treatment.” Applying a single mnemonic to all bites and stings could worsen pain, discharge jellyfish stinging cells, disturb a tick, burn skin or delay breathing support. The final decision aid needs a clearly labelled identity/uncertainty branch, not a single linear list. [S-154, S-158, S-159]

### Victorian spider geography cuts both ways

The dangerous Sydney funnel-web is not Victorian, and Museum Victoria reports that Victorian funnel-webs have not caused serious illness. However, visual confusion with mouse, trapdoor and black-house spiders is common, and Victorian Health still directs PIT/000 for suspected funnel-web or mouse-spider bite. The guide cannot teach “large black spider equals Sydney funnel-web,” but it also cannot let a stressed reader rule out risk from a photo. A Victorian clinical toxicologist must define the emergency threshold using history, appearance uncertainty, location and symptoms. [S-150, S-151, S-152, S-153]

### Live capture creates another casualty

The Victorian spider page says “catch the spider if possible,” but ANZCOR warns that capture causes further bites and says not to bring a live spider to hospital. ANZCOR controls: do not capture or handle. A photograph is optional only after 000/stillness/PIT and only without approaching the animal. [S-150, S-151]

### Redback and funnel-web treatments point in opposite directions

PIT is used for suspected funnel-web/mouse-spider envenomation but not redback or other spider bites, where it can increase local pain. A picture-led identification error is foreseeable. Until a toxicologist approves the Victorian uncertainty rule, a final combined spider flow remains blocked. [S-150, S-151, S-153, S-159]

### Tick advice conflicts exactly where a remote guide matters most

ASCIA and healthdirect say to freeze the tick or leave it for urgent professional help and not use tweezers. ANZCOR adds a last-resort fine-forceps method when remote, unable to freeze/consult and with no known allergy. Prior reaction history does not prove there is no anaphylaxis risk, and a lone person may be unable to monitor themselves. The no-kit branch will not teach forceps removal unless an allergy/remote-medicine specialist resolves the scope. [S-154, S-155, S-156]

### Tick-freezing aerosol is not automatically a vehicle-safe kit item

Ether-containing rapid-freezing products can be flammable and have product-specific temperature, ventilation and storage warnings. A guide intended for hot vehicles cannot add one generically. Exact product, label, maximum storage temperature, ignition risk, application distance and disposal must be reviewed and physically tested. Near the eyes, genitals or ear canal it is not the selected method. [S-154, S-155, S-156]

### Existing public resources can be internally stale

The Better Health bites-and-stings page contains a top-level statement saying not to kill a tick before removal but later gives the current freeze-and-kill method and rejects tweezers. Its box-jellyfish section also retains bandaging language that conflicts with current ANZCOR's no-PIT jellyfish rule. It is useful for Victorian service context but cannot control tick or marine technique. [S-159, S-162, S-163]

### “Non-tropical” is not complete clearance in Victoria

Most Victorian jellyfish stings are managed for pain, but ANZCOR documents Irukandji syndrome as far south as Port Phillip Bay. Because species identification is often impossible and deterioration can be delayed, the final page must escalate an uncertain, extensive or evolving sting rather than rely solely on latitude. That does not justify putting vinegar on every Victorian jellyfish sting: vinegar is specifically wrong for a clear bluebottle. [S-158, S-160, S-161]

### ANZCOR's marine summary and detailed bluebottle branch differ

The 2026 summary says send for an ambulance in all marine envenomation, while its detailed bluebottle section describes stable localised cases that are not requiring an ambulance and provides escalation thresholds. The guide needs a toxicology/emergency reviewer to reconcile this into a conservative but usable Victorian flow. [S-158]

### Heat can treat pain and still cause a burn

The current sources vary between 20 minutes and up to 30 minutes and between measured 43-45 C and “no hotter than easily tolerated.” Pain, cold, age, neuropathy or impaired judgement can make tolerance unreliable. No heated-water illustration is approved until the exact container, temperature check, reassessment cycle and stop rule are tested. Heat never outranks bleeding, airway, 000 or safe water removal. [S-158, S-160, S-161]

### Jellyfish vinegar is a narrow branch

ANZCOR now says vinegar should only be considered for possible box-jellyfish/Irukandji-type stings and acknowledges conflicting experimental evidence. It must not be used for bluebottle. A coastal vinegar kit can only be justified with a reviewed Victoria-specific decision panel, adequate volume and storage testing; carrying vinegar is not evidence that it should be used. [S-158, S-160, S-161]

### Bat cleansing is a deliberate antiseptic exception

EP-007 withholds generic wound-bed antiseptics because they may damage tissue and advice varies. Lyssavirus guidance specifically requires immediate prolonged washing and then virucidal iodine/ethanol when available. The final guide must label this as a bat-exposure exception so it neither omits a life-preserving step nor teaches antiseptic for every wound. [S-164, S-165, S-166, S-167]

### Prior vaccination does not end the bat branch

Previously vaccinated people still require urgent assessment after a potential ABLV exposure. The book cannot calculate or provide vaccine/immunoglobulin schedules; current public-health clinicians decide PEP from the exposure, previous doses, immune status and timing. [S-164, S-165, S-166]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These propositions are tracked in `CONTENT_REGISTER.md`; none is approved reader instruction.

### Universal and spiders

1. Use scene safety, breathing/CPR and anaphylaxis checks before creature-specific care; use 000 for severe, systemic, uncertain dangerous or rapidly worsening cases.
2. Use a prominent PIT “yes/no” table and never apply PIT as generic bite/sting treatment.
3. For a suspected Victorian funnel-web or mouse-spider bite, keep the person still, call 000, apply reviewed PIT and monitor/prepare for CPR; the uncertainty threshold remains unresolved.
4. For redback and other non-funnel-web spider bites, use protected local cold for pain, no PIT, and escalate severe/persistent symptoms or vulnerable patients.
5. Do not capture or handle a spider; photograph only if already safe and without delaying first aid.

### Ticks and insects

1. Do not disturb an attached tick; use a reviewed rapid-freezing product when appropriate or leave it for urgent professional removal.
2. Keep eye/ear/genital and larval-tick cases out of the generic aerosol panel; escalate when unwell or anaphylaxis/paralysis is possible.
3. With no freezing product or consultation, do not teach remote forceps removal until the current source conflict is resolved.
4. Remove a visible bee stinger promptly without squeezing the sac, use protected cold for uncomplicated local pain/swelling and switch immediately to the anaphylaxis/emergency branch when indicated.

### Marine

1. Remove from water safely, assess breathing, monitor continuously and use 000 for severe, extensive, uncertain, high-risk or worsening marine envenomation.
2. For a clear stable localised bluebottle sting, use seawater, remove tentacles, do not rub/use vinegar, and use a reviewed heat method with cold fallback.
3. For an uncertain or potentially serious jellyfish sting in Victoria, prioritise 000/CPR/monitoring and use vinegar only under the reviewed dangerous-jellyfish branch.
4. For fish/stingray injury, control bleeding, leave embedded barbs, do not use PIT, apply heat only to a suitable limb injury and arrange hospital care.
5. For blue-ringed octopus or cone shell, call 000, keep still, apply PIT and be prepared for prolonged CPR; treat sea-snake bite through the snakebite pathway.

### Bats and mammals

1. Do not handle bats unless trained, vaccinated and appropriately equipped.
2. For a potential bat bite/scratch/saliva exposure, wash with soap and copious water for 15 minutes, use a reviewed virucidal preparation if available, flush mucous membranes and obtain urgent medical assessment regardless of wound size or prior vaccination.
3. For other mammal bites that break skin, control serious bleeding first, clean/cover only within EP-007 scope and obtain prompt medical/tetanus assessment.

## Required paired treatment paths

### With a first-aid kit

Candidate capability classes, not approved generic inclusions:

- existing broad elastic PIT bandages and splint capability (`KIT-007`, `KIT-008`) used only for the reviewed indicated creatures;
- a wrapped/insulated cold-pack system (`KIT-025`) that cannot cause direct cold injury and is kept away from attached ticks;
- an exact rapid-freezing tick product (`KIT-026`) only after label, storage, fire/ventilation and anatomical exclusions are resolved;
- a coastal dangerous-jellyfish vinegar supply (`KIT-027`) only if the Victoria-specific branch, adequate volume, packaging and leakage/storage risks pass review;
- a tested hot-water temperature/checking and immersion/pouring capability (`KIT-028`) that cannot delay emergency care or burn the person;
- gloves (`KIT-001`) for ordinary body-fluid/tentacle handling, without claiming universal venom, chemical or wildlife protection;
- wound/bleeding supplies from EP-003 and EP-007 for fish, stingray and mammal trauma;
- a selected virucidal iodine/ethanol product for bat exposure (`KIT-029`), labelled as pathogen-specific rather than general wound antiseptic;
- the individual's prescribed adrenaline product and current action plan (`KIT-010`) when relevant, with current product-specific instructions.

Antivenom, adrenaline for an unprescribed person, tick permethrin, forceps for attached-tick extraction, animal-capture tools, venom extractors, suction devices, arterial tourniquets, topical anaesthetic and unidentified pain medicines are not approved generic additions.

### Without a first-aid kit

This also applies if the kit is missing the item needed for the condition.

- Suspected PIT-indicated bite/sting: keep the person and affected limb still, call 000/activate rescue and do not substitute a cord, belt or arterial tourniquet. The exact broad-clothing alternative is not approved until PIT testing/review.
- Painful non-PIT bite/sting: use a cool wrapped/wet cloth if available, protect skin and reassess; do not apply ice directly, cut/suck, use plants/soil or create delay.
- Tick: do not scratch, squeeze, twist, burn or coat it. With no approved freezing product, leave it in place and obtain urgent professional/remote advice; the forceps fallback is withheld.
- Clear stable bluebottle: use seawater, not fresh water or vinegar, and do not rub. If safe controlled hot water is unavailable, a protected cold pack/cool material may be used after the reviewed branch; do not improvise dangerously hot water.
- Uncertain/severe jellyfish: use 000/monitor/CPR as required. Absence of vinegar does not justify another chemical, fresh water or delayed CPR.
- Fish/stingray: bleeding and embedded-object care control. Do not remove a barb or substitute tight limb bandaging/PIT.
- Bat exposure: begin soap and copious-water washing immediately and obtain urgent care. If virucidal antiseptic is absent, do not use bleach, plant material, ash or another improvised chemical and do not delay evacuation.
- Other mammal bite: follow bleeding/minor-wound paths and seek prompt care; there is no no-kit substitute for tetanus assessment, antibiotics, imaging or surgery.

## Visual requirements and prohibitions

Required candidate visuals:

- a treatment-selection matrix showing exactly which Victorian bite/sting branches use PIT, cold, heat, vinegar, washing or deliberate non-removal;
- a “PIT yes / PIT no” quick panel that treats IM-189 only as a visibly unapproved snakebite working card/concept map; a future generic technique cross-reference requires its own reviewed approval and cannot be inferred from D-050;
- Victorian spider uncertainty panel that distinguishes geography from bedside identification and never declares a bite safe from a photograph;
- attached-tick “do not disturb” and reviewed freeze/leave/escalate flow, including eye/ear/genital exclusion;
- bee-stinger removal and systemic-allergy interrupt;
- Victorian marine flow separating clear localised bluebottle, potentially serious/uncertain jellyfish, penetrating fish/stingray and paralysis-producing bites;
- hot-water burn-prevention setup using the exact tested equipment;
- bat no-handling and 15-minute wash/urgent-care panel, visibly labelled as an antiseptic exception.

Prohibitions:

- no image-led “safe spider” diagnosis, live capture, handling or staged bite;
- no PIT on redback/other spiders, ticks, insects, jellyfish, fish/stingray, scorpion or centipede;
- no cutting, sucking, venom extractor, arterial tourniquet or tight cord/belt;
- no attached-tick squeezing, tweezing, twisting, scratching, burning or household chemical treatment;
- no rapid-freeze aerosol near flame/embers or without exact label/storage controls;
- no vinegar on bluebottle and no one-treatment-fits-all jellyfish diagram;
- no fresh water/rubbing on retained jellyfish stinging cells; no unsafe heat or live-animal handling;
- no embedded barb removal or heat treatment that obscures major bleeding/internal injury;
- no untrained bat handling or image implying gloves alone make it safe;
- no ordinary-wound-antiseptic generalisation from the bat lyssavirus exception;
- no copied clinical/animal image without permission and no AI-generated identification image.

- Pharmacist/product-safety review is required for the exact tick aerosol, vinegar packaging, cold pack, virucidal antiseptic and any labelled medication. Hot-vehicle and ignition/storage tests are mandatory.
- Physical tests use inert props and the exact bandages, splints, cold pack, aerosol surrogate/product controls, water container/thermometer, vinegar bottle and wash setup. No person is bitten, stung, exposed to venom, sprayed with ether, subjected to harmful heat or used for a penetrating injury simulation.
- Comprehension tests must include wrong-creature traps: redback versus funnel-web, tick versus ordinary sting, bluebottle versus uncertain jellyfish, fish spine versus PIT creature, and ordinary wound versus bat exposure.
- Identification images require authoritative species confirmation, location/sex/life-stage metadata, diagnostic-feature review and rights records; they may support avoidance/recognition but cannot be the sole treatment gate.
- Final cross-chapter review aligns snakebite, anaphylaxis, drowning, bleeding, wounds, temperature, poisoning, communications and evacuation.

## Decision

Evidence packet complete. Core propositions may advance only to specialist review drafts. D-050's snakebite-specific IM-189 working card does not approve exact PIT art or advance any other creature branch. The following remain excluded or blocked: treating every large dark Victorian spider as a Sydney funnel-web, ruling out danger from a photo, live capture, one-treatment-fits-all bite/sting advice, remote tick forceps removal, generic hot-vehicle carriage of ether aerosol, permethrin instructions, vinegar on all jellyfish, untested hot-water treatment, embedded-barb removal, untrained bat handling, PEP schedules and any plant/bush remedy.
