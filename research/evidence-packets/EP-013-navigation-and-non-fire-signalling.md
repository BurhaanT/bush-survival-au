# Evidence packet EP-013 - Navigation and non-fire signalling

**Status:** Initial evidence packet complete; IM-184, IM-185 and IM-206 are visibly unapproved working aids; no lost-person movement rule, compass procedure or signalling product is approved  
**Chapters:** 03 Emergency communication; 04 Stay or move; 09 Navigation; 13 Equipment  
**Dependencies:** EP-001, EP-002, EP-009, EP-010, EP-012 and the PLB/phone-power assessments  
**Source snapshot:** core signalling sources S-021, S-056, S-172, S-240 and S-245 rechecked 30 September 2026; remaining packet sources retain their recorded dates

## Research question

Check how a lost or stranded person in Victoria can use a map, compass, satellite positioning (GNSS, including GPS) and visible landmarks to work out and report their location. Knowing a location must not become an unsafe instruction to move. Also compare ways to signal without fire, using more than one method. Check whether they help searchers on land or in the air without adding dangers, draining batteries or making false promises of being seen.

This packet does not approve a walkout, ridge climb, stream-following route, compass bearing, coordinate conversion, signal-fire, pyrotechnic, laser, smoke, flare, mirror-aiming technique, strobe product or ground-panel dimensions. Exact skills and products still require Victorian SAR/navigation review and field testing.

## Evidence strengths

### Navigation first means position and decision, not movement

- Victoria Police tells a lost person to stay calm, stop and think, re-check navigation/map, locate the last known point, seek assistance, find shelter, stay in one place, remain visible and keep a group together. Its same list also suggests a short retrace and gaining height, which must be treated as conditional techniques rather than automatic orders. [S-021]
- Bushwalking Victoria and independent government wilderness guidance converge on stopping, attempting orientation from the current/last known location, a bounded safe retrace before help is activated, and staying put once unable to orient or after help has been sought unless the site presents an immediate threat. [S-052, S-056, S-245]
- Current Australian SAR guidance says land SAR is managed by state police and that electronic navigation should have spare power and paper-map backup. It does not make possession of a GPS or compass evidence that an untrained person should navigate out. [S-223, S-240]
- The safest immediate navigation work can be done without changing the search area: record the present position/time, identify the last point known with confidence, orient the map, compare multiple distinctive terrain/constructed features, check device coordinates/accuracy and communicate the uncertainty. A location estimate and its uncertainty are useful even when they do not justify travel. [S-021, S-047, S-240, S-243, S-244]

### Victorian map system and offline redundancy

- Victoria's authoritative Vicmap Topographic maps are available at 1:25,000, 1:30,000, 1:50,000 and 1:100,000 scales in hardcopy and GeoPDF. Downloaded GeoPDFs can be viewed offline and geolocated in compatible apps, but the device remains dependent on prior download, battery, screen, GNSS reception and intact software. [S-241]
- Vicmap describes 1:25,000 as suited to bushwalking/cycling/fossicking, 1:30,000 as the same information adapted to smaller printing, 1:50,000 as suited to 4WD/bushwalking/camping and 1:100,000 as broader touring context. The guide should select detail/coverage for the place and task, not assign one scale to the reader's original activity. [S-241]
- Vicmap digital map sheets are normally updated on cycles measured in years, while web mapping updates more often. A printed or downloaded map can therefore remain usable for terrain context but cannot prove a track, bridge, gate, closure, fire boundary or access condition is current. [S-241]
- Paper map, waterproof protection and baseplate compass remove battery/network dependence, but only if the coverage is correct and the reader has practised the exact map/compass workflow. Victoria Police explicitly says to learn before the trip. [S-021, S-243]

### Coordinates, grids and datums

- Vicmap maps use Map Grid of Australia coordinates and align to Grid North for the relevant zone. Victoria spans MGA Zones 54 and 55; each map prints grid convergence and magnetic declination for the map centre. [S-242]
- GDA2020/MGA2020 are now the official Australian datum/grid, while older GDA94/MGA94 and much older AGD66/AMG maps remain in circulation. State guidance warns that GDA94 is no longer aligned with precise current GNSS; older AGD coordinates can be displaced by roughly 200 metres from geocentric coordinates. [S-242, S-243]
- A coordinate without its format and, for grid use, datum and zone can be ambiguous or wrongly plotted. The low-error emergency behaviour is to read the coordinates exactly as shown, identify the app/device and format, state datum/zone when known, add nearby landmarks/route context and repeat back. The guide should not require an untrained person to perform a datum conversion under stress. [S-047, S-171, S-242, S-243]
- Emergency+ uses the phone's GPS to help a 000 caller provide location details. Advanced Mobile Location may automatically assist during a compatible 000 call, but it does not work in every circumstance and currently does not work during emergency camp-on to another carrier. Verbal location remains necessary. [S-046, S-047, S-171]

### GNSS and electronic map limits

- GNSS satellite reception and mobile-network coverage are separate. A phone can sometimes calculate a GNSS position without mobile coverage if its receiver and sky view work, while an undownloaded online map, data service or ordinary emergency call may still be unavailable. “No mobile signal” does not equal “no GNSS,” and a coordinate does not create communications. [S-043, S-047, S-241, S-244]
- Receiver accuracy depends on satellite geometry, obstruction, reflected signals, atmosphere, receiver/antenna and mapping software. Trees, terrain, buildings and bridges can degrade a consumer fix; an accuracy circle is an estimate, not a guarantee. [S-244]
- A single device dot should be checked against repeated readings, the stated accuracy/quality indication, the map and identifiable terrain. A highly precise-looking coordinate on a wrongly selected map/datum or stale track is still unsafe. [S-242, S-243, S-244]
- Electronic location must not silently open the movement branch. After rescue contact/PLB, its most useful role may be reporting and monitoring a stationary position rather than plotting a walkout. [S-021, S-172, S-240]

### Compass limitations

- Magnetic North, Grid North and True North are not interchangeable. Map-to-compass travel requires the correct current grid/magnetic correction for that map/location and the exact compass design; a statewide Victorian declination number would be wrong and becomes stale over time. [S-242, S-243, S-246]
- Compass readings can be distorted by nearby ferrous objects, magnets, vehicles/equipment, powerlines and some geological conditions. A reading should be taken level, away from interference and checked for consistency against another observation/known terrain. [S-243, S-246]
- A digital compass may also require model-specific calibration and power. A mechanical baseplate compass avoids battery failure but still needs correct hemisphere/product, a clear capsule/needle and practised use. No exact model has yet passed the equipment gate. [S-246]

### Audible signalling

- Victoria Police recommends carrying a whistle. The current National Search and Rescue Manual recognises three short blasts together, regularly spaced, as a field distress signal; it also records search/acknowledgement and recall signals used by organised teams. [S-021, S-240]
- Bushwalking Victoria independently uses three whistle blasts or torch flashes as a help signal and tells lost people to make their location visible. [S-245]
- A whistle preserves voice and may be easier for land searchers to hear; the NATSAR manual says search assets may stop engines to listen for survivors' shouts or whistles. [S-056, S-240]
- The exact blast duration conflicts: the NATSAR manual defines short blasts as about one second, while a Bushwalking Victoria leader handbook says signals should last about three seconds. Until Victorian SAR review resolves the field-page wording, the stable core is **three distinct short blasts together, repeated at regular intervals**, followed by listening. [S-240, S-245]

### Daylight and continuous visual signalling

- Victoria Police advises bright clothing in an open area visible to land and air searchers. Independent wilderness guidance supports colour/contrast and making the stationary site conspicuous. [S-021, S-056]
- The signal site cannot overrule shelter and terrain safety. Bright material should be placed at or immediately beside the safe waiting site where possible; moving into a clearing, ridge, road, shoreline or outcrop must pass the same fire, flood, lightning, falling-tree, cliff, traffic, water and temperature gate as any other movement. [S-064, S-067, S-069, S-071, S-182–S-191]
- The current NATSAR manual recognises signal mirrors as effective daylight devices. Its detection distances are context-dependent observations, not a promise for Victorian forest, cloud, sun angle, user aim or search geometry. Exact mirror design and aiming must be tested and reviewed. [S-240]
- The current international ground-to-air code authorised in the Australian search-and-rescue region uses `V` for require assistance and `X` for require medical assistance; if in doubt, it directs use of `SOS`. The manual does not provide a pocket-guide universal panel size that guarantees detection. [S-240]

### Night and active visual signalling

- The NATSAR manual recognises flashing lights, shapes, panels and other signals, and notes that torches/strobes may be visible to aviation searchers. It also warns that some LED strobes/electronic flares are invisible to some night-vision equipment because of their emitted frequencies. “Very bright” or “emergency mode” therefore does not prove aviation detectability. [S-240]
- A phone screen or torch may help when a search asset is nearby, but it consumes the same finite energy used for communication/location. A light plan must set a preserved reserve, accidental-activation control and independent backup. [S-240, S-235]
- SOS is internationally recognised in the NATSAR appendix, and independent wilderness guidance gives a light pattern of three short, three long, three short. This candidate needs a simple visual and timed usability test; the guide will not make a stressed reader decode other Morse. [S-056, S-240]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### “Retrace briefly” versus “stay in one place”

Both appear in Victoria Police and independent guidance because they describe different stages. A short retrace may be reasonable before an emergency alert while the same obvious safe route and last known point remain certain. It becomes harmful if it is exploratory, loses the present point, crosses hazards, splits the group, continues after uncertainty or changes the search area after responders have a location. No universal time or distance was found; the final boundary needs Victorian SAR review. [S-021, S-052, S-056]

### “Gain some height” can make the emergency worse

Height may improve view or radio/visual line of sight, but can also add steep-ground falls, cliffs, wind chill, lightning, falling branches, bushfire exposure, whiteout and separation from shelter/known position. It is not a generic instruction and is excluded after help/PLB unless a current hazard or responder direction justifies movement. [S-021, S-067, S-069, S-071, S-186–S-189]

### “GPS range” is not mobile coverage

Victoria Police's shorthand that GPS/phones can fall out of range risks merging two systems. Satellite position can degrade under obstruction; mobile calls/data depend on carrier coverage. Offline maps remove data dependence but not receiver, battery, screen or map-error dependence. The final page must show these as separate failure columns. [S-021, S-043, S-241, S-244]

### Precise coordinates can still be wrong

The phone may show many decimal places even when its accuracy is poor, the app map is stale or the coordinate datum/format is misunderstood. Automatic AML can also be absent during camp-on. The caller must report what the device says and the uncertainty/landmarks, not announce false survey accuracy. [S-046, S-047, S-242, S-244]

### Map age versus live ground conditions

A mapped road, track, crossing or water feature may be closed, removed, private, overgrown, burned, flooded or seasonally dry. Topographic maps support position/terrain reasoning, not current permission or passability. Live land-manager/warning information controls when available. [S-184, S-185, S-191, S-241]

### Three-blast convention versus exact timing

Australian national SAR and Victorian bushwalking sources agree on three signals but differ on duration and some reply meanings. The guide can teach the recognisable three-signal distress core after operational review; it should not print a full multi-code vocabulary that may conflict with group/river/marine procedures. [S-240, S-245]

### Signal visibility versus site safety

An open ridge/clearing can improve visibility while worsening exposure or leaving the location already given to responders. The preferred design is a continuous high-contrast marker at a safe site plus active sound/light/mirror when searchers are likely nearby. A move solely for visibility needs a defined benefit, short safe route, recorded/communicated change and return/stop rule. [S-021, S-056, S-172]

### A generic strobe may be invisible to aviation night vision

Light output, colour, beam and flash mode are not interchangeable with NVIS visibility. No flashlight/headlamp “SOS mode” or electronic flare is approved without exact-device evidence and SAR review. [S-240]

### National SAR fire effectiveness versus Victorian fire prohibition

The NATSAR manual records that fire can be visible at night, while EP-012 found no general Victorian lost-person exemption to TFB/land/fire rules. Visibility evidence does not create legal or physical permission. Non-fire signalling remains the baseline. [S-215–S-221, S-240]

### Ground-air codes versus detectable construction

`V`, `X` and `SOS` have defined meanings, but vegetation, shadow, scale, orientation and search angle determine whether they can be seen. A tiny symbol near dense canopy can create false confidence. No dimensions or construction sequence enters the field guide until controlled distance/angle tests and SAR review are complete. [S-240]

### Natural “direction signs” versus validated navigation

Moss, sun, wind, drainage, animal paths and one ambiguous landmark cannot establish a safe route in broad Victorian terrain. Following a stream/downhill can lead into gorges, cliffs, floodwater, private land or greater remoteness. These may be environmental observations, not direction commands. No such shortcut has enough evidence to enter actionable navigation prose. [S-183–S-191]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These are research propositions, not approved reader instructions.

### Lost-position and movement lock

1. Stop; make the present point the reference location; record time, coordinates/device accuracy and the last point known with confidence before any movement.
2. Perform stationary checks first: map orientation, terrain/constructed-feature comparison, repeated GNSS fix, Emergency+ location display and communications attempt.
3. A short retrace is a pre-alert orientation technique only when the exact safe route remains obvious, the group stays together and the person can return to the reference point; no exploratory wandering or generic climb.
4. Once help is requested or a beacon activated, stay at the communicated safe location unless a current life threat or responsible responder directs movement.
5. If movement is unavoidable, communicate/record the reason, time, intended direction/destination and party state; maintain a traceable plan without creating misleading litter/markers.

### Map, coordinate and compass use

1. Carry correct-area current topographic coverage in protected paper form plus a previously downloaded offline electronic copy where appropriate.
2. Report coordinates exactly as displayed and name the app/device/format; include datum and grid zone when known and use landmarks/route context rather than improvised conversion.
3. Treat GNSS accuracy/quality as uncertain under canopy, terrain and obstruction; compare repeated fixes with the map and ground.
4. Use only a practised map/compass method with current map-specific grid/magnetic information; take compass readings away from metal/magnets/electrical sources and check consistency.
5. Never let a location dot, bearing, mapped track or natural clue become permission to cross a hazard or self-evacuate.

### Non-fire signalling

1. Layer signals: electronic alert/location, continuous high-contrast marker, audible whistle, daylight mirror and controlled night light, each with a different failure mode.
2. Use three distinct short whistle blasts together at regular intervals as the candidate distress signal; pause/listen and repeat without exhausting the user or signalling continuously.
3. Keep high-contrast material at the safe waiting site; do not climb or enter hazardous open terrain merely to display it.
4. Use a selected sighting signal mirror only after learning and testing its aim; range is not guaranteed.
5. Reserve torch/phone power for communication, care and signalling; use SOS only in a simple reviewed sequence and do not assume a generic strobe is visible to NVIS.
6. Use `V`, `X` or `SOS` ground-air symbols only after their meaning, construction, contrast, scale and placement pass review and field photography.
7. Do not use signal fire, smoke, flare, pyrotechnic or laser as the activity-neutral baseline; each needs its own legal/product/operational gate.

## With-equipment and without-equipment paths

### With selected equipment

- Protected current paper map plus a compatible baseplate compass and practised map-specific procedure.
- Exact offline map/GNSS system already downloaded and tested, with the approved phone-power system.
- Pealess whistle attached where separation from pack/vehicle does not remove it.
- High-contrast signal panel/garment sized and placed from completed field visibility tests.
- Sighting signal mirror with retention and practised aiming.
- Exact headlamp/torch with protected independent backup power and reviewed signal/runtime mode.
- Waterproof notebook/card and pencil recording location, time, movement and responder instructions.

### Without those supplies

- Stop and keep the group together; use stationary terrain/landmark reasoning and preserve the last known/reference point rather than choosing a folklore direction.
- Give 000/responders the best available location description: last confirmed place/time, route taken, visible terrain/constructed features, direction/distance estimates and current hazards, clearly labelled as estimates.
- Use conspicuous safe available material at the waiting site without dismantling essential shelter/clothing or moving into danger.
- Use voice or a safely struck object in the reviewed three-signal pattern when people/searchers are nearby, then listen; do not exhaust the casualty or abandon airway/bleeding/environmental care.
- No equipment cannot improvise satellite alert, guaranteed direction, accurate coordinates, aviation-visible light or a safe/legal signal fire. The guide must say when the capability is absent.

## Visual requirements and prohibitions

Required candidate visuals:

- a “position first, movement locked” flow that separates stationary orientation, bounded pre-alert retrace, post-alert stay and immediate-hazard movement;
- a map/compass/GNSS comparison showing battery/network, sky view, map currency, datum/zone and skill failures separately;
- a coordinate readback card with format/device/datum/zone/landmark fields and no invented conversion;
- a three-short-blast distress panel beside “pause, listen, repeat,” with reply-code ambiguity noted until review;
- a five-mode signalling panel: emergency contact/alert, continuous contrast, whistle/sound, mirror in sunlight and controlled light in darkness. IM-206 is the visibly unapproved text-led working implementation; it is not an authority-issued algorithm and does not fulfil the exact-product or field-photography specifications IM-041/IM-042;
- a ground-air `V`, `X`, `SOS` panel only after scale/contrast/placement testing;
- distance/angle field photographs of candidate high-contrast panels in forest, alpine, Mallee/open scrub and vehicle settings without implying statewide visibility;
- a do-not-transfer panel linking national fire visibility evidence back to Victorian fire prohibition.

Prohibitions:

- no generic climb-to-a-ridge, follow-water/downhill or “walk toward civilisation” arrow;
- no statewide fixed declination, datum-free grid reference or unlabeled coordinate format;
- no screen dot without accuracy/uncertainty and cross-check context;
- no map track represented as current/open/safe solely because it is printed;
- no exact compass/coordinate conversion copied without physical test and current source;
- no universal distance claim for whistle, mirror, clothing, panel, torch or strobe;
- no laser aiming at aircraft/people and no flare/pyrotechnic/smoke sequence;
- no signal material placed across road, water, cliff, unstable tree zone or essential shelter;
- no signal fire; and
- no AI-generated factual terrain, map, compass, signal geometry, aircraft acknowledgement or device UI.

## Validation and review required

- Victoria Police/BSAR-aligned reviewer resolves short-retrace, gain-height, post-alert movement, group cohesion and marker/notes boundaries.
- Qualified Victorian navigation instructor checks exact map-scale selection, map orientation, last-known-point method, grid/magnetic conversion, coordinate readback and no-movement framing.
- Geospatial reviewer checks Vicmap edition, datum, zone, GNSS accuracy language and any transformed coordinate example.
- Aviation/land SAR reviewer checks three-blast wording, mirror, light/NVIS, high-contrast panel and ground-air codes/acknowledgements.
- Exact map/compass/app/phone/light/whistle/mirror/panel records pass the equipment gate; app screens and manuals are rechecked on every change.
- Navigation tests use familiar controlled terrain first, then representative Victorian forest/open/alpine/coastal contexts with an independent safety observer, defined boundaries and no intentional lost-person scenario.
- Signalling visibility is photographed/measured from controlled land positions and reviewed for aviation relevance without distracting operating aircraft or using lasers/pyrotechnics/fire.
- Timed novice tests record wrong movement, coordinate transposition, north/datum/zone error, missed stop conditions, whistle-pattern error, battery depletion and signal placement hazards.
- Final layout test verifies that no navigation method bypasses the stay/move box and no signalling method bypasses shelter/fire/environment controls.

## Decision

Initial packet complete. Navigation will be taught first as **locate, record, cross-check and communicate**, not as a reason to walk. Victoria's current paper/GeoPDF map system and a practised baseplate compass provide a defensible additional layer, but map currency, datum/zone, declination, interference and GNSS obstruction must remain visible. D-070/IM-206 now make the non-fire signalling synthesis reviewable as five unnumbered parallel modes at one safe known or reported waiting place: emergency contact/alert, continuous contrast, three-short-blast sound, a purpose-made mirror in sunlight and controlled light in darkness. No authority publishes that exact combined card, and the modes are not assumed technically independent. Exact duration, products, panel dimensions, mirror method, runtime, NVIS visibility, ground-air construction and detection remain blocked. Signal fire, generic height gain, stream/downhill following, folklore direction cues and datum-free coordinates are excluded. `FIELD_READY_BUILD=NO` remains controlling.
