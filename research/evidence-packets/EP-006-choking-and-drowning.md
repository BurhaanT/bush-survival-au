# EP-006 — Choking and drowning

**Created:** 4 September 2026  
**Chapter:** 06 First aid and evacuation  
**Dependencies:** 02 First minutes; 03 Emergency communication; 05 Temperature and shelter; EP-003 Critical first aid; EP-004 Trauma first aid  
**Status:** Evidence packet only — not field-ready guidance

## Purpose

Check the evidence and unanswered practical questions about:

- an object blocking the airway in an adult, child or infant;
- a person choking while alone;
- safe rescue of a person in water;
- resuscitation and immediate care after drowning;
- drowning-related airway obstruction, vomiting, AED use, possible spinal injury and hypothermia;
- what can be done with the listed equipment, and without it.

This packet does not qualify a reader to enter water, perform an aquatic contact rescue or provide in-water resuscitation. No sequence below is approved as reader-facing treatment. Final entries must use `book/MEDICAL_ENTRY_TEMPLATE.md`, receive Australian resuscitation and aquatic-rescue review, and pass hands-on testing with adult, child and infant manikins where applicable.

## Evidence strengths

### Choking

- ANZCOR, healthdirect and St John Australia distinguish an effective cough from a severe obstruction. An effective cough is encouraged and continually observed; an ineffective cough, inability to speak/breathe or deterioration requires emergency activation and physical treatment. [S-123, S-124, S-125]
- The current Australian conscious-person sequence is up to five sharp back blows, checking after each, followed if needed by up to five chest thrusts, checking after each, then alternating while the obstruction remains. The aim is relief, not automatically completing every blow or thrust. [S-123, S-124, S-125]
- Abdominal thrusts/Heimlich manoeuvre are not recommended in current Australian guidance because of reported life-threatening complications. Blind finger sweeps are discouraged; only a clearly visible object is considered for careful manual removal. [S-123, S-124]
- Infants under one year require a separate supported head-down method for back blows and chest thrusts. A child must not be picked up and inverted. Adult/child and infant illustrations cannot be merged. [S-123, S-124, S-125]
- If the person becomes unresponsive and is not breathing normally, the response transitions immediately to the current basic-life-support/CPR flow. [S-123, S-124, S-125]
- For a person choking alone, healthdirect supports forceful coughing, staying as calm as possible and attempting to get help. The selected Australian sources do not support a generic chair-edge or self-abdominal-thrust technique. [S-124]

### Drowning and water rescue

- Rescuer safety controls the rescue. ANZCOR says rescue from land or craft is safest; Royal Life Saving orders lower-risk options from talk, reach and throw through progressively higher-risk water entries. A reader must not be encouraged to create a second casualty. [S-126, S-127, S-129]
- Once an unresponsive person is out of the water, ANZCOR and St John say to assess on the back with head and body at the same level, not head-down. If the person is not breathing normally, start CPR without waiting for oxygen or an AED. [S-126, S-129]
- Drowning is primarily a lack-of-oxygen event. ANZCOR, Royal Life Saving and the Wilderness Medical Society all emphasise rescue breaths/ventilation as part of resuscitation. Compression-only CPR is a temporary fallback when the rescuer is unable or unwilling to give breaths; it is better than doing nothing but does not correct the oxygen deficit. [S-126, S-128, S-130]
- Roll briefly to the side only when obvious fluid or matter obstructs the airway, clear visible foreign matter, reassess, and minimise interruption to CPR. Do not press the abdomen/stomach to drain water, and do not repeatedly try to remove re-forming froth. [S-126, S-129, S-130]
- Do not delay CPR while fetching an AED. When it becomes available, attach it and follow prompts; dry the chest if feasible. ANZCOR states that use on a wet surface such as poolside is not itself dangerous. [S-126]
- ANZCOR requires ambulance/medical assessment after every drowning incident, even one that seems minor or recovered. St John and healthdirect's child guidance corroborate this conservative Australian public rule. [S-126, S-129, S-131]
- Possible spinal injury can follow diving, surf, rocks or craft trauma, but rapid removal and airway/oxygenation take priority when an unresponsive person is not breathing normally. Ongoing wet exposure and cooling require concurrent hypothermia protection without delaying resuscitation. [S-126, S-130]
- In-water rescue breaths are a specialist technique for a trained, equipped rescuer when removal is delayed or impossible. Chest compressions are ineffective in water and must not be attempted there. [S-126, S-130]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### Lifeguard initial breaths versus the general Australian bystander flow

ANZCOR's drowning guideline directs CPR with rescue breaths and references the standard Guideline 8 flow. A 2025 Royal Life Saving position statement tells trained first responders such as pool lifeguards to deliver two initial rescue breaths before 30:2 cycles. Its stated scope is trained responders, not an untrained general reader. The personal guide will not add a two-initial-breath variant unless an Australian resuscitation clinician and aquatic-rescue trainer approve the exact audience, sequence and cross-reference. [S-126, S-128]

### Compression-only CPR is a fallback, not equivalence

Generic public campaigns often simplify CPR to compressions, but drowning arrest is hypoxic. The final page must encourage trained rescue breathing and use the barrier device when immediately available, without allowing its absence, retrieval or reluctance to stop all CPR. Exact wording must avoid both false equivalence and bystander paralysis. [S-126, S-128, S-130]

### “Clear the airway” does not mean drain the lungs

Visible obstructing matter and vomit require prompt airway management, but re-forming froth and water in the lungs are not removed by abdominal thrusts, turning the person head-down or pressing the stomach. A drawing that shows drainage by inversion would be actively dangerous. [S-126, S-129, S-130]

### Australian choking advice differs from familiar overseas advice

Many readers may recall the Heimlich manoeuvre from overseas media. Current ANZCOR expressly recommends back blows and chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts. The book must label this as Australian guidance and prevent an illustration from resembling an abdominal thrust. [S-123, S-124, S-125]

### Infants cannot be reduced to a small adult diagram

Infant head support, body angle, chest-thrust hand placement and airway position differ. A child over one year follows the adult/child branch, while an infant under one has its own panel. Exact technique requires current infant-CPR training and manikin testing; the guide cannot substitute for that training. [S-123, S-124, S-125]

### Solo choking lacks a validated self-treatment sequence in selected Australian guidance

healthdirect recommends forceful cough and getting help. The selected national sources do not provide a reviewed chair-edge or self-thrust method. The book will not import one from an overseas or unsourced graphic merely because it is common online. A resuscitation specialist must decide whether any additional self-rescue action can be safely taught. [S-123, S-124]

### Entering water may turn one drowning into two

Royal Life Saving's hierarchy progresses from talk/reach/throw to water entry, and contact towing is its last, most hazardous option. The guide can teach decision order and preparation equipment, but actionable swimming/towing technique belongs to practical accredited training, not a static bush-survival page. [S-126, S-127, S-129]

### “All need assessment” versus wilderness release criteria

The 2024 Wilderness Medical Society guideline contains conditional remote observation/release recommendations that depend partly on normal lung auscultation and clinical assessment. Current ANZCOR says all people involved in a drowning incident require medical assessment. A lay reader without a stethoscope and clinical training cannot reliably apply the WMS release grade. The book will use the conservative Australian assessment rule and will not print a lay four-to-six-hour clearance pathway. [S-126, S-130, S-131]

### Spinal precautions must not delay oxygenation

A traumatic mechanism should prompt gentle movement minimisation, but a non-breathing person must be removed and resuscitated promptly. This must cross-reference the spinal packet so that “do not move” cannot cause an airway/resuscitation delay. [S-097, S-126, S-130]

### Wet, cold and exhausted rescuers create a second treatment problem

After removal, resuscitation takes priority, while wet clothing, wind and ground contact continue cooling the person and may also cool the rescuer. The group branch should allocate a caller, resuscitator and shelter/insulation helper when possible. A solo branch needs testing so shelter work never delays CPR. [S-057, S-058, S-126]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These propositions are tracked in `CONTENT_REGISTER.md`; none is approved reader instruction.

### Choking

1. Judge whether the cough is effective; encourage and observe an effective cough without striking the person.
2. For an ineffective cough in a conscious adult or child over one, activate emergency help and use the reviewed Australian back-blow/chest-thrust sequence, checking after each action.
3. Use a completely separate infant panel; never invert a child and never use abdominal thrusts.
4. Remove only a clearly visible object; do not perform a blind finger sweep.
5. If unresponsive and not breathing normally, transition to BLS/CPR immediately.
6. If alone and choking, forceful coughing and attracting help are supportable; additional self-thrust methods remain withheld.

### Drowning

1. Call/shout for help and use the safest feasible rescue, preferring talk, reach, throw or rescue from land/craft; do not enter water without the skill and flotation required to avoid another casualty.
2. Once out, assess an unresponsive person on the back at one level; if not breathing normally, begin CPR with rescue breaths without waiting for oxygen or an AED.
3. If breaths cannot initially be given, compression-only CPR is a temporary fallback rather than no action; add breaths as soon as a willing/able rescuer or immediately available barrier permits.
4. Clear only obvious obstruction with minimal interruption; do not drain the person, press the abdomen or chase re-forming froth.
5. Attach an AED when available without delaying CPR and dry the chest if feasible.
6. Arrange ambulance/medical assessment for every drowning incident, even after apparent recovery.
7. Treat possible spinal injury and hypothermia concurrently, but never at the cost of delayed removal, airway care or resuscitation.
8. Do not teach in-water breathing or towing as a lay static-book technique; never perform chest compressions in water.

## Required paired treatment paths

### With a first-aid kit and the listed trip equipment

Candidate capability classes, not approved generic inclusions:

- an immediately accessible CPR face shield or pocket mask already tracked as `KIT-002`, used only if it does not delay resuscitation;
- an AED already tracked as `KIT-011`, where the activity/site makes one realistically available;
- a properly selected and maintained personal flotation device and purpose-designed reach/throw aid for water exposure; these belong in a future general emergency-equipment register, not the first-aid kit register;
- dry insulation, wind protection, ground insulation and spare dry layers from the shelter system;
- gloves, light, phone/PLB/satellite communication and a written observation/handover record;
- oxygen only for currently trained and certified personnel using suitable maintained equipment; it is not a baseline personal-kit item.

No airway-suction device, anti-choking device, improvised airway, oxygen set or aquatic-towing device is approved merely because it can be purchased.

### Without a first-aid kit or the listed trip equipment

- Choking treatment does not normally require a first-aid item: recognition, calling, correctly performed back blows/chest thrusts and transition to CPR remain possible. No improvised abdominal thrust, blind sweep, suction device or airway tool is substituted.
- In a water rescue, talk, shout, direct, reach from stable ground or throw an ordinary buoyant object may be possible, but material strength, grip, current and rescuer footing still control. Do not enter solely because no rescue aid is present.
- If CPR breaths are possible and the rescuer is willing/able, the absence of a barrier does not change the drowning person's physiological need for ventilation. If breaths cannot be given, start compression-only CPR rather than doing nothing and add breaths when capability arrives.
- Without an AED, continue CPR and rescue activation; do not delay care to travel for distant equipment unless the reviewed solo/group flow directs it.
- Use available dry clothing, packs, mats and shelter materials to reduce continued cooling only when this does not interrupt CPR or airway care.
- There is no improvised substitute for medical assessment after apparent recovery.

## Visual requirements and prohibitions

Required candidate visuals:

- effective-cough versus ineffective-cough recognition fork;
- adult/child-over-one back blow and chest thrust sequence, with a check-after-each marker;
- a physically separate infant panel showing support and angle;
- a bold Australian “no abdominal thrusts / no blind sweeps” warning;
- drowning rescue hierarchy from talk/reach/throw toward trained higher-risk options;
- out-of-water assessment, rescue-breath CPR, obstruction/vomit side-roll and AED sequence;
- paired kit/no-kit callouts and solo/group role allocation.

Prohibitions:

- no image that resembles an abdominal thrust or depicts hitting an upright person;
- no child held upside down;
- no blind finger sweep or tool inserted into the mouth;
- no head-down “drain the water” pose, abdominal pressing or repeated froth-clearing;
- no untrained swimmer making contact with a panicked person;
- no chest compressions in water;
- no AED retrieval image that abandons the person or delays CPR;
- no AI-generated anatomical/technique image used without manikin reconstruction, clinician review and measured step-by-step verification.

## 29 September 2026 drowning-card addendum

D-055 advances the drowning propositions only to two visibly unapproved working cards. IM-197 is a secure-land, non-contact route: call 000, stay on land, then talk, reach or throw only while the rescuer and aid remain secure. It explicitly gives no craft-handling, wading, swimming, towing, in-water-breath or contact-rescue method. A person already on a craft is told to stay aboard and use only its equipment and procedures within current training.

IM-198 covers the out-of-water general-bystander route: call 000 for every incident; assess an unresponsive person on a safe firm surface with head and body level; use the ordinary ANZCOR 30:2 path with breaths; keep obstruction-related CPR interruption as short as possible; add an AED when available and dry the chest if feasible; protect against cooling without delaying lifesaving care; and have ambulance clinicians assess the person even after apparent recovery. The trained-responder initial-breath variant remains excluded. The adult/child recovery-position mechanics apply only from age one; no infant side-position mechanics are supplied.

The 95% source-match and 60%/65% decision-layout figures are subjective editorial estimates, not probabilities of safe rescue, correct treatment, recovery or survival.
