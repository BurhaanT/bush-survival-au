# Evidence packet EP-010 — Shelter air, flash-flood and alpine hazards

**Status:** Evidence packet complete; all actionable propositions remain behind specialist and field/usability review  
**Chapters:** 04 Stay or move; 05 Body temperature and emergency shelter; 06 poisoning/monitoring intersections  
**Dependencies:** EP-001, EP-002, EP-007 and EP-009  
**Source snapshot:** checked 4 September 2026

## Research question

Check three shelter dangers that earlier notes did not cover fully:

1. fuel-burning equipment, vehicle exhaust and carbon monoxide (a poisonous gas) in a tent, vehicle or enclosed or partly enclosed shelter;
2. flash flooding and rapid water rise at an improvised shelter or stranded vehicle/camp;
3. the increased consequences of weather, navigation and shelter failure in Victoria's alpine and subalpine country.

This packet does not approve a stove/heater, carbon-monoxide alarm, floodwater crossing, snow shelter, avalanche technique or high-country route. It also does not turn a fixed waterway setback into a safety guarantee.

## Evidence strengths

### Carbon monoxide and combustion in shelter

- Victorian Health says carbon monoxide is colourless and odourless, arises from combustion including gas/wood heaters, cooking appliances, barbecues, generators and vehicle exhaust, and can cause non-specific headache, nausea/vomiting, weakness, breathlessness, dizziness, coordination difficulty, confusion, chest pain, unconsciousness, seizures and death. Multiple people or pets affected in the same space and improvement outside are important exposure clues. [S-178]
- Victorian Health explicitly says never use a charcoal grill, lantern or portable camping stove inside a home, tent or caravan; never operate fuel-powered engines in enclosed structures even when doors/windows are open; and do not place exhaust where it can enter an opening. A person who feels sick, dizzy or weak should get to fresh air and seek immediate medical advice. [S-179]
- Commonwealth Product Safety independently lists portable cookers, heaters, barbecues, generators and other fuel-powered equipment as CO sources and flags tents/caravans and poorly ventilated covered areas. [S-180]
- The US CDC independently confirms the colourless/odourless hazard, non-specific symptoms and prohibition on portable gas camp stoves or charcoal in enclosed spaces. Its current guidance also says opening garage doors/windows is not enough for generator use. [S-181]
- Victorian public guidance and the international check support a conservative field rule: an outdoor-use combustion appliance is not operated in a tent, swag, vehicle, enclosed awning, bothy or improvised enclosed shelter. “Ventilated” is not a novice clearance test, and a cracked door/window does not make prohibited use safe. [S-179, S-180, S-181]
- A CO alarm may be a useful backup in a context where the exact alarm and manufacturer instructions are applicable, but no alarm converts prohibited combustion into safe practice. The selected Victorian source discusses alarms for buildings, not a universal backcountry device. No alarm is proposed for the personal kit until standards, temperature, moisture, battery, placement and alarm-response limits are independently reviewed. [S-178, S-181]

### Suspected carbon monoxide exposure

- Early CO symptoms overlap with hypothermia, heat illness, food illness, exhaustion, dehydration, altitude effects and other medical problems. Smell, flame colour, one pulse-oximeter result or a companion feeling well cannot exclude exposure. Standard pulse oximetry cannot distinguish carboxyhaemoglobin and therefore cannot screen for CO poisoning. [S-118, S-178, S-181, S-190]
- Victorian Health directs suspected exposure away from the source into fresh air and immediate medical advice, with 000 for emergency signs. The generic poisoning packet already controls rescuer safety, 000 versus 13 11 26 and BLS. [S-132, S-133, S-134, S-178, S-179]
- Turning off equipment or opening a shelter is secondary to avoiding rescuer exposure. A reader is not instructed to enter or remain in a contaminated tent, cabin, caravan or vehicle to retrieve property, investigate a source or rescue a person without a safe plan. Immediate rescue of a collapsed person from a contaminated space is a hazardous-environment problem requiring emergency-service direction; a static pocket book cannot certify entry. [S-132, S-134, S-178, S-179]
- Oxygen is not a baseline lay kit item. ANZCOR supports high-concentration oxygen for suspected CO poisoning, but only an appropriately trained person using selected equipment and a separately approved oxygen system could act on that clinical capability. This packet does not add it to `FIRST_AID_KIT_REGISTER.md`. [S-178, S-190]

### Flash-flood site selection

- VICSES tells campers to use higher ground away from creeks, rivers, streams and other waterways, monitor warnings and leave before water starts to rise when a flood warning is issued. [S-182]
- VICSES describes flash flooding as rapid flooding associated with intense rainfall and warns people not to enter floodwater. Low-lying and riverside camping areas, drains, culverts, roads and underpasses can all be exposed. [S-183]
- Parks Victoria says rivers/creeks can rise quickly, flash flooding may occur with little or no notice, crossings can become impassable and wet ground can destabilise trees. [S-184]
- BOM says localised rain/thunderstorms may be difficult to predict; warnings may be issued shortly before impacts and not every hazardous condition will have a warning. Rising river levels may indicate heavy rain upstream even when the local sky or rainfall does not reveal it. [S-185]
- A legal/environmental campsite setback such as 20 metres is not a flood-safety contour. Flood reach depends on terrain, catchment, channel, upstream rainfall and local conditions. The emergency guide must show landform escape routes and relative high ground, not a magic tape-measure distance. [S-182, S-183, S-184, S-185]

### Flash-flood response and movement

- When a warning provides lead time, moving before water rises is materially safer than waiting until a crossing, road or escape route is flooded. Once floodwater is present, VICSES/Parks sources say not to walk, swim or drive through it. [S-182, S-183, S-184]
- Active rising water is an immediate-hazard exception to the lost-person stay rule. Move toward known higher ground without crossing floodwater, drains, culverts or an unstable bank; keep the group together and tell responders the new position when possible. Exact high-ground selection and casualty movement require VICSES/SAR review because a dark slope, cliff, falling trees or lightning may create competing hazards. [S-021, S-067, S-182, S-183, S-184]
- A vehicle is not automatically safe when parked in a flood path, causeway, dry creek bed, low point or beside a waterway. Conversely, leaving a vehicle into water or unknown terrain can be worse. The final diagram must separate pre-water relocation, water already around the vehicle and responder-directed action; no generic vehicle-escape technique is approved here. [S-183, S-184]
- Floodwater can conceal current, debris, damaged surfaces, drop-offs, contamination and unstable banks. “It looks shallow” is not a crossing test. [S-183, S-184, S-191]

### Victorian alpine and subalpine scope

- Parks Victoria identifies unstable/deep snow, steep icy slopes, cornices, avalanche and extreme weather in higher-elevation winter country and says safe backcountry travel requires training, experience, judgement, terrain knowledge and appropriate equipment. Cold, wet and wind together are particularly dangerous. [S-186]
- Parks Victoria says Alpine National Park weather can deteriorate suddenly without warning and snow can fall at any time of year, including midsummer. Campers should be self-sufficient, carry a tent and treat huts as emergency-use resources rather than planned accommodation. [S-187]
- BOM independently confirms snow in all seasons and explains that blizzard/whiteout, heavy rain, strong wind, fog/low cloud, ice, lightning and UV can combine navigation, exposure, falling-tree, tent-failure and rescue difficulties. [S-188]
- SnowSafe and Bushwalking Victoria independently say not to rely on huts: they can be occupied, unavailable or impossible to locate in bad weather. A tent or other appropriate emergency shelter is carried even on shorter alpine outings. [S-189]
- A hut can be life-saving emergency refuge when safely reachable, but the guide must not direct a lost or injured reader across whiteout, avalanche terrain, swollen water, ice or unknown country merely because a hut appears on a map. The stay/move and responder-direction gate remains upstream. [S-021, S-186, S-187, S-188, S-189]
- Snow caves, pits and other snow structures can fail through collapse, burial, poor ventilation, blocked entrances, wet clothing, time/exertion and unsuitable snow. Secondary sources describe techniques, but this packet does not establish a novice-safe construction instruction. Carried shelter/insulation and professional snow-safety training remain the default preparation approach. [S-064, S-065, S-186, S-189]
- Avalanche rescue requires terrain/snowpack judgement, transceiver/probe/shovel competence and prior practice. A generic survival chapter cannot responsibly teach a stranded novice to enter avalanche terrain or conduct a technical rescue from a diagram. [S-186]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### “Well ventilated” is not permission to burn fuel inside a tent

An older ACCC page says covered-area cooking/heating needs good ventilation, while current Victorian Health explicitly prohibits portable camp stoves, lanterns and charcoal grills in tents/caravans and prohibits engines in enclosed areas even with doors/windows open. A novice cannot measure safe ventilation from how open a shelter feels, particularly in wind-driven, snow-covered or partly enclosed arrangements. The final guide uses the clearer prohibition for tents, vehicles and enclosed/partly enclosed shelters. Covered outdoor cooking, if later included, needs the exact appliance manual, open-air separation, fire law and weather controls. [S-179, S-180, S-181]

### Warmth need can drive the exact behaviour that causes CO poisoning

Cold rain, snow or a stranded vehicle makes indoor stove/heater use feel like a survival measure. CO is undetectable by smell and early symptoms resemble cold/exhaustion, so the reader may close vents and continue burning. The shelter and hypothermia pages must offer non-combustion insulation/weather-protection actions and place the prohibition beside any stove, heater, engine or charcoal image. [S-057, S-061, S-178, S-179, S-181]

### “Stay put” changes when the site is becoming a watercourse

Staying findable is the default after rescue activation, but rising floodwater is a live hazard. Movement must happen early enough to avoid crossing water, yet a vague command to “go uphill” may lead into cliffs, lightning exposure, unstable trees or avalanche terrain. The final flow needs three states: warning/lead time, water visibly rising nearby, and water already surrounding/cutting off the site. The latter needs direct emergency-service review; no vehicle-exit or swimming method is inferred. [S-021, S-067, S-071, S-182, S-183, S-184]

### Distance from water does not equal height above flood level

Parks Victoria site rules often specify minimum environmental distances from water, but flash floods can cover broad floodplains and dry channels. A reader might treat “20 metres” as safety certification. The book will use landform and official-warning reasoning and will label any land-manager distance as a separate legal/environmental minimum, not a survival threshold. [S-182, S-183, S-184]

### Higher ground can conflict with trees, lightning, wind and cold

Flood safety favours higher ground; lightning and wind exposure may make a ridge or isolated tree hazardous; saturated soil destabilises trees; alpine ridges increase wind chill and navigation loss. “Higher” must mean outside the water path with the least additional hazard, not automatically the highest nearby point. Exact storm trade-offs require VICSES/BOM/SAR review and photographed scenario testing. [S-067, S-070, S-071, S-183, S-184, S-188]

### A hut is emergency refuge, not a navigation objective at any cost

Parks Victoria calls alpine huts emergency-use resources, while SnowSafe/Bushwalking Victoria warns that they may be occupied, unavailable or unlocatable. Directing a lost person to a mapped hut can contradict staying findable and create whiteout, ice, river or avalanche exposure. The final guide may show a hut as an option only when its location and route are actually known, conditions/terrain permit, and rescue direction does not say otherwise. [S-021, S-187, S-188, S-189]

### Detailed snow-shelter instructions may create false competence

Some specialist manuals describe snow pits/caves, but Parks Victoria frames winter backcountry safety as training- and experience-dependent. A novice construction diagram cannot establish snow stability, ventilation, burial/cornice/avalanche safety or the physical capacity to excavate before hypothermia. Snow structures remain excluded from the initial guide unless a qualified alpine/snow-safety specialist defines a low-error scope and supervised field testing passes. [S-064, S-186, S-189]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These are research propositions, not approved reader instructions.

### Shelter air and carbon monoxide

1. Do not operate outdoor-use fuel-burning stoves, heaters, lanterns, barbecues/charcoal or engines in a tent, swag, vehicle or enclosed/partly enclosed improvised shelter; an open flap/window is not a safe-use test.
2. Position any permitted outdoor combustion/exhaust so fumes cannot enter shelter openings, using the exact product manual and fire/weather rules.
3. Suspect CO when compatible symptoms affect people/pets sharing a combustion space, improve outdoors or follow heater/stove/generator/engine use; absence of smell is irrelevant.
4. Move exposed people to fresh air without creating another casualty, stop the source only when safe, use 000/BLS for emergency signs and obtain prompt Poisons/medical advice.
5. Do not use an ordinary pulse oximeter, one unaffected companion or an untriggered/unapproved alarm to clear the space or the person.
6. A CO alarm is a backup only if an exact device/context is separately approved; it never authorises prohibited combustion.

### Flash flood

1. Select a shelter/vehicle site on defensible higher ground outside dry channels, drains, culverts, floodplains and waterway margins, with a route to higher ground that does not require crossing water.
2. Treat warnings, intense/local rain, thunder, upstream rain clues or rising/dirty/noisy water as reasons to reassess early; warning absence is not safety.
3. If a warning directs leaving, do so before water starts to rise and follow the responsible service.
4. Never walk, swim or drive through floodwater; appearance/depth alone is not a safe crossing test.
5. Rising water is an immediate-hazard reason to leave the reported site for the least-hazard known higher ground; keep the group together, take communications/essential lifesaving supplies when immediately accessible and report the new position.
6. Do not give one fixed waterway distance, “go to the ridge” instruction or generic vehicle escape technique.

### Alpine/subalpine

1. Apply an all-season alpine warning: sudden cold, snow, wind, rain, fog/whiteout, ice, lightning and UV can overlap, including outside winter.
2. Carry shelter/insulation appropriate to alpine exposure and do not plan to depend on reaching a hut.
3. Treat whiteout/fog and ice as navigation/movement hazards; the existence of a hut or vehicle does not justify an unsafe unconfirmed route.
4. Use huts as emergency refuge when safely reachable, while preserving access and following current land-manager rules; do not imply availability.
5. Exclude snow-cave/pit and avalanche-rescue technique from the initial novice guide; direct preparation toward training, practised equipment and current snowpack/avalanche information.
6. Link all cold-injury treatment to EP-002 and lost/stay/move decisions to EP-001/EP-009 rather than create alpine duplicates.

## Required paired paths

### With relevant carried equipment

- Deploy selected non-combustion rain/wind shelter and ground insulation before heat loss forces dangerous stove/heater use.
- Use exact manufacturer-labelled cooking/heating equipment outdoors only within its approved open-air, clearance, weather and fire-law limits; no model is selected here.
- Keep communications, light, waterproof clothing, insulation and the selected shelter immediately accessible for a pre-water flood relocation.
- In alpine terrain, use a shelter rated/tested for the expected wind/snow load and a practised navigation/communication system; these require equipment-specific gates.
- A candidate CO alarm, if later considered for a vehicle/caravan context, needs a separate equipment ID, relevant standard, manufacturer-approved environment, alarm sound/battery/temperature/moisture testing and action protocol. It is not currently in the kit.

### Without those supplies

- Carbon monoxide: lack of a heater-safe shelter or alarm does not justify combustion inside an enclosure. Use non-combustion wind/rain barriers, dry insulation and group heat conservation within the reviewed hypothermia path; seek rescue rather than closing a fuel appliance into the space.
- Suspected CO: move into fresh air by a path that does not require entering/remaining in the contaminated enclosure. Use 000/BLS and Poisons/medical advice; no plant, smell test, pulse oximeter or re-ventilation interval clears exposure.
- Flood: move early to the least-hazard known higher ground before water blocks the route. Do not improvise a swim, wade, vehicle crossing or rope crossing.
- Alpine: use the carried clothing/materials that safely reduce wind, rain and ground heat loss without blocking the airway. Do not attempt a snow cave, avalanche rescue or unknown hut route from a book diagram.

## Visual requirements and prohibitions

Required candidate visuals:

- a cross-section showing forbidden combustion/exhaust sources outside a tent, swag, vehicle, enclosed awning/bothy and improvised shelter, with airflow arrows that never imply a cracked opening makes indoor burning safe;
- a CO recognition/escape panel showing multiple-occupant/pet clues, fresh air, 000/BLS and no pulse-oximeter clearance;
- a valley/floodplain site comparison showing watercourse, dry channel, culvert, floodplain, higher escape route and competing tree/lightning hazards without inventing a universal contour;
- a three-state flood panel: warning with lead time, water rising nearby, and water already surrounding/cutting off the site, with the third state referred to emergency services rather than a generic escape method;
- an alpine compound-hazard panel linking wind/rain/snow/whiteout/ice/lightning to temperature, navigation, tree/tent and rescue consequences;
- a “hut may not be reachable or available” panel linking carried shelter and stay/move decisions.

Prohibitions:

- no image of a lit stove, heater, lantern, charcoal, fire or running engine inside/attached to an enclosed shelter or vehicle;
- no “just ventilate” graphic, safe opening size, smell/flame-colour test or alarm-as-permission symbol;
- no fixed safe distance from water or visual that treats a dry channel as safe;
- no wading, swimming, vehicle-entry, rope crossing or floodwater depth test;
- no arrow that sends a lost reader blindly to a ridge/hut or through avalanche/cornice terrain;
- no snow-cave/pit construction, avalanche transceiver search or technical alpine rescue sequence;
- no AI-generated weather/terrain diagram used as factual evidence. Final diagrams must be constructed from reviewed geometry/scenarios and checked after layout.

## Validation and review required

- Victorian public-health/toxicology and fire/product-safety reviewers approve CO prevention, symptom uncertainty, fresh-air movement, 000/13 11 26 boundary and any equipment.
- CFA/land-manager review aligns outdoor stove/fire/exhaust placement with current Total Fire Ban and park/forest rules before any heating/cooking panel advances.
- VICSES/BOM and Victorian SAR reviewers approve flood-site, early-movement, higher-ground and reported-location language; a road/water-rescue specialist decides whether the guide should contain any surrounded-vehicle action. Default is exclusion.
- An experienced Victorian alpine guide/snow-safety or rescue specialist approves the all-season alpine scope, hut boundary, carried-shelter expectations and exclusion of snow/avalanche techniques.
- Field tests use inert/cold equipment only. No combustion occurs in or near a shelter; no person enters floodwater, unstable snow, avalanche terrain or a dangerous storm.
- CO visual tests specifically check whether a reader interprets a vestibule, open awning, cracked window or tent flap as permission to burn fuel.
- Flood scenario tests use contour models/photos/maps and include darkness, upstream rain with local clear weather, a vehicle in a low point, a casualty, saturated trees and lightning conflict.
- Alpine tests include loss of horizon/whiteout simulation, high wind/noise, gloved shelter deployment, an unavailable/full/unlocatable hut and rapidly falling temperature.
- Final layout audit checks that no crop separates a prohibition from the depicted stove, engine, floodwater, hut or snow hazard.
