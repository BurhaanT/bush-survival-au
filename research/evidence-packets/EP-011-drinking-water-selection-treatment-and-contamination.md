# Evidence packet EP-011 — Drinking-water selection, treatment and contamination

**Status:** Evidence packet complete; D-060 controls the current simple rolling-boil route; exact non-boil products and every field-ready treatment sequence remain behind specialist, product and usability review  
**Chapters:** 07 Water; 05 flood/fire intersections; 06 illness/poisoning intersections; 13 equipment  
**Dependencies:** EP-002, EP-005, EP-007, EP-009 and EP-010  
**Source snapshot:** checked 6 September 2026

> **Superseded workflow notice — 29 September 2026:** D-060 withdraws this packet's project-invented dry-rim, thermometer, transfer-ladle, temperature-gated and full-batch-storage workflow. Those details remain below only as a record of a rejected design. The current manuscript follows the simpler official core recorded in S-487 and R-482: screen the source; use a suitable unsealed heat-safe pot and lawful heat; bring the whole surface to a vigorous rolling boil; shut down heat as instructed; cool the stable pot while protecting it from new contamination; then use promptly or place in clean closed storage. A separate untreated collector is useful but optional. The method addresses most microbial hazards only, never salt, algal toxins, chemicals or metals, and still requires water-health, fire/legal, equipment and usability review.

## Research question

Check what a stranded reader in Victoria needs to decide about drinking water:

1. how to choose the least hazardous available source;
2. what boiling, filtration, chemical disinfection and portable UV can and cannot do;
3. when floodwater, harmful algae, mining/agricultural/industrial contamination, bushfire impact, groundwater or salt make a source unsuitable for ordinary field treatment;
4. how to stop clean water becoming contaminated again; and
5. what the guide can responsibly say when neither safe water nor working treatment is available.

This packet does not approve a filter, purifier, tablet, bleach dose, UV device, improvised filter, solar still, distillation apparatus or untreated-water decision. Exact products will require an equipment record, current manufacturer instructions and physical testing.

## Evidence strengths

### Treat natural water as suspect

- Parks Victoria says untreated creek, bore and sometimes rainwater can cause illness; recommends carrying drinking water and treating natural sources. Clear appearance is not proof of safety. [S-203]
- Current Victorian Department of Health guidance says rivers, creeks, dams and shallow bores are readily contaminated by disease-causing microorganisms from people/livestock/animals and by industrial or agricultural runoff. [S-204]
- Source protection is the first treatment barrier. Parks Victoria's practical hierarchy favours clear, free-flowing water without scum, debris or odour and avoids water downstream of camps, current/former mines, agriculture and unsewered towns. These features reduce risk but do not certify safety. [S-203, S-204, S-209]
- A marked untreated campground tank is not drinking water merely because it comes from a tap. Availability and status vary by site and season; current park signs/advisories control. [S-203]

### Boiling controls microorganisms, not all hazards

- Victorian Health and Parks Victoria both use a rolling boil followed by cooling as the clearest lay microbial treatment. The current Australian Drinking Water Guidelines say automatic-shutoff kettles reaching boil are sufficient for a boil-water advisory and define rolling boil as a continuous rapid stream of bubbles rising from the bottom. [S-203, S-204, S-208]
- The Australian source does not require the US “one minute” extension at Victorian elevations. A final Victorian guide should not combine incompatible timings or imply that longer boiling fixes chemicals. [S-208, S-210]
- Boiling does not remove harmful chemicals, heavy metals, dissolved salt or cyanobacterial toxins. Where those hazards are suspected, a different source is required unless a specifically validated treatment system covers the exact contaminant. [S-203, S-204, S-205, S-208, S-210, S-214]
- Boiling creates scald, fuel, fire and carbon-monoxide risks. Cooling and clean storage are part of the method; all heating remains subject to the open-air combustion/fire controls in EP-010. [S-179, S-203, S-204, S-208]

### Portable filters, chemicals and UV are not interchangeable

- Australian drinking-water guidance uses a multiple-barrier approach because no single disinfectant works equally across all organisms and conditions. Turbidity, temperature, pH, contact time and the organism affect performance. [S-209]
- Chlorine is effective against bacteria and viruses and many protozoa, but normal safe drinking-water concentrations do not reliably inactivate *Cryptosporidium*. Particles/turbidity can shield organisms. [S-208, S-209]
- CDC's current hiking/camping guidance independently says most portable filters do not remove every germ class; pore size and verified performance matter. It recommends filtering then disinfecting when boiling is not possible and requires the product instructions. [S-210]
- Portable UV treatment requires clear water, working power, correct volume/agitation/exposure and the exact device instructions. It provides no continuing protection if treated water or the clean side is recontaminated. [S-209, S-210, S-211]
- “Filter”, “purifier”, “carbon”, “one micron” and “removes 99.9%” are not enough by themselves. The final equipment record must state whether a claim covers protozoa, bacteria, viruses and any named chemical under an applicable test standard, and distinguish absolute from nominal/mean pore size. [S-210]
- Household bleach dosing in the Australian Drinking Water Guidelines applies only to an exact unscented sodium-hypochlorite concentration and water clarity. Concentration, additives, storage degradation, drop size, temperature/contact time and *Cryptosporidium* limitations create a high-error field method. No bleach recipe advances into the initial guide unless a water-health specialist approves an exact carried product and measured-dose protocol. [S-208, S-209]
- Iodine has product and health limitations; no generic iodine dosing or universal-use recommendation is accepted. A selected commercial tablet may be considered only from its current label and contraindications. [S-203, S-210]

### Harmful algae require source rejection

- Victorian Health says some cyanobacteria produce toxins affecting the gut, liver or nervous system and that boiling algae-affected water will not inactivate the toxins. Avoid contact with visibly affected water and follow signs/manager advice. [S-205]
- Harmful algae may not always be visible or detectable by smell. Victoria's water authority maintains current alerts and explicitly says not to drink from affected Gippsland Lakes because boiling does not remove the risk. [S-206]
- EPA Victoria says floodwater may contain microbial and chemical contaminants and can be associated with harmful algal blooms. Clarity is not a safety test. [S-207]
- A static field guide cannot teach a reader to distinguish toxic from non-toxic blooms or declare water safe after a bloom disappears. Algae-affected or officially warned water is rejected; the guide does not improvise activated-carbon treatment. [S-205, S-206, S-207]

### Flood, bushfire, groundwater and coastal water

- Floodwater is presumed contaminated and is not collected as drinking water. It can contain sewage/microorganisms, chemicals, debris and algal hazards, and entering it is independently prohibited in EP-010. [S-183, S-191, S-207]
- Bushfire smoke, ash, debris, dead animals and aerial retardants can contaminate roof tanks and catchments. Victorian Health says unusual taste, appearance or smell is reason to stop using affected tank water; chemical contamination is not corrected by boiling or chlorination. [S-204, S-213]
- Groundwater can contain naturally occurring arsenic or pollution even when it appears fresh. An unknown bore, well or spring is not presumed potable. [S-204, S-212]
- Seawater cannot hydrate a person; excreting its excess salt causes a net water loss. Ordinary boiling, disinfection and backcountry microfiltration do not desalinate it. [S-210, S-214]
- Brackish or estuarine water presents both salt and contamination problems. Taste is not a quantitative salinity test, and a survival guide should not give a “slightly salty is acceptable” threshold. A validated desalination system is a separate technical product, not a generic water filter. [S-210, S-214]
- Snow can be melted when no liquid source is safely reachable, but melting is energy-consuming and does not itself treat microbial or chemical contamination. Melted snow must re-enter the same source-screen and treatment process as other natural water. CDC and NSW National Parks agree on those boundaries but print different timed-boil instructions; neither timing is transferred into this Victorian guide, which retains its separately reviewed Australian rolling-boil endpoint. [S-456]

### Treated water can become unsafe again

- WHO/CDC guidance treats protected storage as a separate barrier because collection, transport, dirty hands, bottle threads, hoses and clean/dirty components can reintroduce organisms after treatment. [S-211]
- The final process needs visibly distinct dirty-water and clean-water sides; a clean container with a protected opening; no dipping hands/cups into treated water; and manufacturer-prescribed cleaning, drying, backflushing and storage. [S-210, S-211]
- A filter's untreated inlet/hose and dirty bottle are contaminated components. A diagram must not allow them to touch a clean outlet, clean bottle mouth or stored treated water. This product-specific workflow requires physical testing. [S-210, S-211]
### Rejected historical transfer design — retained for audit only

The next three findings describe the withdrawn design. D-060 and the superseded-workflow notice above control current instructions.

- WHO guidance separately recommends boiling, cooling and holding water in the same covered container to reduce handling and transfer recontamination. That principle did **not** validate the project's earlier one-pot field route: a close-fitting cover could contact the raw-water-wetted rim, and a ladle used at the mouth could contaminate the batch when re-dipped. The route has been withdrawn. [S-454, S-457]
- CDC safe-storage guidance calls for clean, sanitised, tightly covered storage and favours an opening that lets water be poured without hands or objects entering. WHO community material allows a clean long-handled ladle when a tap is unavailable; *Self Care for Health* says the ladle should be cleaned daily and no one should drink from it. None of these sources validates open cooling or the project's centre-to-storage ladle geometry. [S-457]
- For this working draft, **protected clean** means the transfer-only ladle, food-thermometer probe, exact storage sufficient for all intended drinking water and a separate personal cup for each person were washed, sanitised and dried before departure under their exact maker instructions and current public-health guidance, then packed closed or wrapped against dirty contact until used. The thermometer instructions must permit measuring hot liquid across the needed range, and each storage maker must state a maximum fill temperature. Loss of known-clean state stops the route; the guide supplies no improvised field sanitising dose or reset after contamination. [S-457, S-458]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### Rolling boil versus a timed boil

Victorian and current Australian sources direct a rolling boil and cooling, while current US camping guidance says one minute (three minutes above 1,981 metres). Victoria's highest land is below that US altitude threshold, but the Australian boil-water guidance specifically says an automatic-shutoff kettle is sufficient. The proposed Victorian wording stops at confirmed rolling boil rather than importing a longer US time. A water-health reviewer must confirm that this remains appropriate for untreated field water rather than a reticulated boil-water advisory. [S-203, S-204, S-208, S-210]

### Clear, flowing water is preferable but still unsafe untreated

Source-selection cues can be misread as a drinking clearance. “Clear/free-flowing/upstream” only helps choose between suspect sources; wildlife, people, carcasses, faecal contamination and chemicals may be invisible. Every source-choice visual must feed into treatment, not a green “safe” symbol. [S-203, S-204, S-209, S-210]

### Boiling is microbiologically strong but operationally costly

Boiling can cover bacteria, viruses and parasites without choosing a filter, but requires a vessel, fuel/heat, time and cooling, and may create burns, fire or CO. In extreme heat or scarce-fuel conditions another validated treatment may be operationally preferable. The book should compare capabilities and failure modes, not declare one method universally available. [S-179, S-203, S-208, S-210]

### “Filter” can mean sediment improvement, organism removal or chemical treatment

A cloth, coffee filter, improvised sand/charcoal column, carbon taste filter and microbiological purifier may all make water look better, but have different verified effects. Appearance improvement is not disinfection. No improvised filter will be labelled as making water safe; at most it may be a pre-treatment before a separately effective method. [S-209, S-210]

### Chemical disinfection is easy to carry but easy to overgeneralise

Chlorine, chlorine dioxide and iodine differ in organism performance, contact time, health limits and product formulation. Cold/cloudy water can reduce effectiveness, and a reader under stress can confuse drops, tablet volumes or bottle sizes. Only an exact selected product, intact label and tested measuring workflow can enter the equipment path. [S-208, S-209, S-210]

### An algal bloom may be invisible

Visible scum/discolouration is a reject cue, but absence of a visible bloom does not clear a warned water body. The current local warning, sign or water-manager advice controls over a visual check and over this static guide. [S-205, S-206]

### Severe dehydration versus untreated water

Avoiding an unsafe source indefinitely can itself become life-threatening, while drinking contaminated water can cause acute illness and worsen dehydration. No reviewed Australian source in this packet supplies a simple time, symptom or source threshold for drinking untreated water. The final no-treatment emergency branch remains excluded pending a remote/wilderness clinician and water-health specialist decision. Activating rescue, reducing exertion/heat load and seeking a treatable source remain upstream, but the book will not pretend the last-resort trade-off has one universal answer. [S-204, S-210]

### Water for drinking versus water for wound or eye care

Water judged acceptable after microbial treatment is not automatically sterile or appropriate for an injured eye, deep wound or invasive procedure. EP-007 controls irrigation. Scarce-water allocation across cooling, drinking and wound care also has no fixed rule and remains condition-specific. [S-139, S-142, S-145, S-204]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These are research propositions, not approved reader instructions.

1. Prefer carried/sealed or currently designated drinking water; treat every natural source and marked untreated tank as suspect.
2. Reject floodwater, seawater/brackish water, algae-affected/warned water and water with a plausible chemical/industrial/agricultural/mining/fire-retardant contamination pathway rather than relying on ordinary boiling or disinfection.
3. If natural fresh water must be used, choose the highest-quality source available: clear and free-flowing where possible, without scum/odour/debris, and away/upstream from camps, waste, livestock/agriculture, mining and settlement—but never treat those cues as proof of safety.
4. For a microbiological hazard, bring water to a confirmed rolling boil, then cool and protect it from recontamination. Boiling does not correct chemicals, metals, salt or algal toxins.
5. If not boiling, use only a selected product or validated combination whose instructions and verified capability cover the relevant organism classes and current water conditions.
6. After a whole-surface rolling boil, shut down the lawful heat source as instructed. Let the stable pot cool naturally on a non-combustible heat-safe surface where ash, dirt, splash, insects, hands and animals are unlikely to recontaminate it. Use the water promptly when practical; otherwise pour it into clean closed drinking-water storage without the openings touching. A separate untreated collector is useful but optional. No dry rim, thermometer, transfer ladle, fixed transfer temperature, full-batch storage or separate cup for every person is part of the minimum route. [S-203, S-204, S-208, S-211, S-454, S-457, S-487]
7. Do not present improvised cloth/sand/charcoal filtration as purification or disinfection.
8. Do not print a generic bleach/iodine/tablet dose, UV exposure, filter-life claim or desalination claim without an exact approved product and test record.
9. Do not give an untreated-water “last resort” threshold until specialist review resolves the dehydration/contamination trade-off.

## Required equipment and no-equipment paths

### With selected carried equipment

- Use the least contaminated fresh source available and the exact approved treatment workflow.
- The equipment record must show the treatment volume, source-water limits, organisms/chemicals actually covered, prefilter/turbidity rule, contact or processing time, battery/fuel needs, temperature/altitude constraints, service life, freeze/drop/storage risks and failure indication.
- For the boil route, carry a suitable unsealed heat-safe cooking pot and use only a lawful heat source that passes Chapter 8's current gates and can be operated exactly as instructed. Keep the pot stable, cool it on a stable non-combustible heat-safe surface protected from new contamination, and have a clean cup for prompt use or clean closable drinking-water storage when the water will not be used promptly. A separate untreated collector is useful but optional. Campfire boiling remains unavailable because no exact grate, pot support and sustained-boil cycle is validated. Keep raw-water-contact surfaces away from clean storage interiors and openings.
- Retain the original instructions and expiry/batch information for any chemical product; no memory-only dosing.
- Carry a backup method that fails differently from the primary method when the environment justifies it; the combination itself must be tested.

### Without working treatment equipment

- Activate rescue/communication early, reduce heat load and unnecessary exertion, inventory all potable liquids and search only when movement is justified under the stay/move gate.
- Reject floodwater, seawater/brackish water, algae-affected/warned water and suspected chemically contaminated water; a cloth or improvised sand/charcoal device does not make these safe.
- Boiling remains available only when a suitable unsealed heat-safe pot, lawful controllable heat, a stable protected cooling place and a way to use the water promptly or keep it in clean closed storage are available. Campfire boiling is unavailable. If those minimums are missing, no compact non-boil or zero-equipment treatment route has passed this guide's validation gate.
- No instruction to drink untreated natural water is approved. The genuine last-resort balance requires specialist review and cannot be replaced by a fixed number of hours or a visual “best-looking stream” rule.

- an algae panel using verified photographs of several appearances plus a prominent “may be invisible—warnings control” statement;
- flood, mine/agriculture, bushfire/retardant, unknown-bore and salt/brackish source-rejection panels.

Prohibitions:

- no AI-generated photograph of a water body, algal bloom, mining runoff or treatment outcome used as identification evidence;
- no “clear = safe”, “moving = safe”, universal upstream distance or taste-based chemical/salt clearance;
- no improvised filter labelled purifier, no single pore-size badge treated as full capability and no generic “99.9%” claim without organism/test context;
- no printed chemical dose detached from exact concentration, volume, contact time, temperature/turbidity limits and expiry;
- no stove boiling inside a tent, vehicle or enclosed/partly enclosed shelter;
- no ordinary boiling, tablet, filter or UV image attached to algae, chemical, flood or salt water as if it makes that source safe;
- no untreated-water last-resort decision tree until specialists approve the trade-off.

## Decision

Evidence packet and both exact-product desk assessments are complete. D-060 controls the current boil proposition: natural water remains suspect; reject a source when a specific salt, algae, flood, warning or chemical red flag is present or reasonably suspected; otherwise choose the least-contaminated reachable fresh source for treatment. Use a suitable unsealed heat-safe pot and lawful heat that can be operated exactly as instructed. Bring the whole surface to a vigorous rolling boil, shut down heat as instructed, let the stable pot cool naturally where new contamination is unlikely, then use promptly or pour into clean closed drinking-water storage without the openings touching. A separate untreated collector is useful but optional. The route addresses most microbial hazards only; it does not remove salt, algal toxins, chemicals or metals and is not a drinking-safety guarantee. The dry-rim, thermometer, 50°C, transfer-ladle, full-batch-storage and one-cup-per-person requirements are withdrawn project inventions, not current equipment minimums. Campfire pot support is unavailable, and no exact compact non-boil or dependable zero-equipment treatment route has passed the validation gate. Plan potable capacity for the actual trip, split carried drinking water and keep an on-person reserve separate from vehicle water. Named treatment, container and stove candidates remain research systems. Generic dosing, improvised sanitising or purification, unsupported certification, Total Fire Ban treatment-only boiling, current-warning substitutions and an untreated-water last-resort rule remain excluded or blocked.
