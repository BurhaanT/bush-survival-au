# EP-002 — Body temperature and emergency shelter

**Created:** 4 September 2026  
**Chapter:** 05 Temperature and shelter  
**Related medical chapter:** 06 First aid and evacuation  
**Status:** Evidence packet only — not field-ready guidance

## Purpose

Check how to prevent and recognise dangerous cold or heat, protect a person from Victorian weather and choose a place for emergency shelter. The final advice must explain what can be done with the listed first-aid and shelter equipment, and without it.

This is not a treatment card. Advice about dangerous cold or heat could cause serious harm if wrong (Level 3). It still needs Australian medical review, search-and-rescue review for movement decisions, equipment tests and a check of the final pages.

## Evidence strengths

### Cold exposure and hypothermia

- ANZCOR is the primary Australian first-aid authority in this packet. It applies to bystanders and first aiders and supports recognition by observable function and behaviour, ambulance/rescue escalation, warm/dry shelter, reduced activity, replacement of wet clothes, wind protection, insulation plus a vapour barrier and guarded active rewarming. [S-057]
- healthdirect independently supports the Australian emergency framing and warns that hypothermia can progress slowly without the affected person recognising the danger. [S-059]
- The Wilderness Medical Society adds remote-setting detail: field temperature measurements can be unreliable; clinical condition matters; severe cases require gentle, substantially horizontal handling; ground insulation and protection of the head/neck, wind and wet insulation matter; wet clothing should be handled in a way that does not expose the person further. [S-061]
- St John Australia broadly converges on recognition, ambulance activation and heat-loss prevention, while also exposing a wording conflict about how heat sources should be placed. [S-063]

### Heat illness

- ANZCOR identifies a spectrum rather than a safe binary distinction. Red flags include inability to continue, high body temperature, dizziness/faintness, gastrointestinal symptoms, shock-like features, weakness/poor muscle control and reduced consciousness, confusion or seizure. Lack of sweating occurs in only a proportion of serious cases. [S-058]
- ANZCOR supports cooling as soon as practical without delaying ambulance assistance, with continuous attention to consciousness and airway. It distinguishes children aged five and under from older people in its cooling methods. [S-058]
- healthdirect independently treats suspected heatstroke as a 000 emergency and supports shade/cool surroundings, removing excess clothing, skin wetting/fanning and carefully limited oral fluid when conscious. [S-060]
- The 2024 Wilderness Medical Society guideline provides a current, evidence-graded remote-setting cross-check for recognition, cooling and evacuation. [S-062]

### Emergency shelter and site selection

- Bushwalking Victoria and the New Zealand Mountain Safety Council agree that an emergency shelter must be chosen for the weather, group, terrain and equipment actually available; different shelter types have material trade-offs and require practice. [S-064, S-065]
- Bushwalking Victoria recommends deciding to stop early enough to retain time and daylight for site choice and setup. [S-064]
- Parks Victoria warns that limbs and whole trees can fall unpredictably, especially in hot, windy or severe weather, and advises against camping under trees. VICSES and WorkSafe add consequence and storm context. [S-066, S-067, S-068, S-069, S-070]
- The Bureau of Meteorology says there is no safe place outdoors during a thunderstorm and prefers a hard-top vehicle or solid building; a fabric tent is not lightning shelter, and a tree is not a safe substitute. [S-071]

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### A temperature number is not a sufficient field decision rule

healthdirect pages currently use both 39°C and 40°C language for serious heat illness. ANZCOR gives “high body temperature” as one red flag and makes impaired function, behaviour and consciousness central. WMS similarly warns against relying only on a field temperature for hypothermia. The guide should not make a novice wait for a particular reading or falsely reassure them when a consumer thermometer reads below a threshold. Exact wording requires a clinician. [S-057, S-058, S-059, S-060, S-061, S-062]

### Sweating does not safely rule heatstroke in or out

Some simplified material contrasts sweaty heat exhaustion with dry heatstroke. ANZCOR explicitly says lack of sweating appears in only a proportion of serious cases, while healthdirect acknowledges that a person with heatstroke may still be sweaty after exercise. A field card must not require dry skin before 000/cooling. [S-058, S-060]

### Wet-clothing removal can worsen exposure if sequenced badly

ANZCOR says to move the person to warm, dry shelter and replace wet clothes with dry clothes. WMS adds that removing clothes while still exposed can increase heat loss and movement, and recommends cutting them off only once in a warm environment for a significantly hypothermic person. The final instruction needs a sequence, not the vague command “remove wet clothing”: first protect from wind/rain and prepare dry insulation, then minimise exposure and movement during replacement. The exact severity boundaries require clinical review. [S-057, S-061]

### Active heat-source wording is inconsistent in its safety margin

ANZCOR permits several active heat methods but warns that some can burn skin when applied directly and says to follow manufacturer directions and use specified insulation. St John’s short fact sheet uses more direct placement wording. healthdirect warns against direct heat in its consumer advice. The book will not specify heat-pack or hot-water-bottle placement until a clinician reconciles the sources and the exact kit products have been tested. [S-057, S-059, S-063]

### Body-to-body contact is not a general replacement for insulation and evacuation

ANZCOR lists body-to-body contact among active rewarming options. WMS treats it more narrowly and warns that it can add complexity and should not displace effective packaging or evacuation. It must not be presented as the default no-kit solution, particularly when it exposes the rescuer or patient. [S-057, S-061]

### Cold-water immersion is clinically effective but operationally constrained

For a person over five, ANZCOR describes cold-water immersion for 15 minutes as the most effective cooling method, while also giving alternatives when immersion is unavailable. In the bush, immersion may create drowning, supervision, water-quality, access, conservation, mobility and later-cooling risks. The field guide must not tell a confused or poorly controlled person to enter a river, lake or steep bank. A clinician must define when the method is feasible and what the safer non-immersion branch is. [S-058, S-060, S-062]

### “Clear sky” reduces tree-strike risk but is not a universal shelter site

Parks Victoria’s clear-sky advice addresses falling trees. An exposed position may increase wind, rain, solar load and lightning exposure. During a thunderstorm, BOM says neither an isolated tree nor an open outdoor location is safe, and a tent is not protective lightning shelter. Site selection therefore needs a live hazard comparison; no diagram may label one generic site “safe.” [S-066, S-067, S-069, S-070, S-071]

### Lower ground may reduce wind while increasing flood risk

A hollow or watercourse can appear sheltered but can collect cold air or water and may become hazardous in rain or upstream storms. Conversely, a ridge may improve drainage but increase wind and lightning exposure. A Victoria-specific site-selection flow requires flood, weather and terrain expertise plus field testing; it cannot be reduced to “high” or “low” ground. [S-067, S-071]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

The propositions below are tracked in `CONTENT_REGISTER.md`. They are not approved instructions.

### Cold pathway

1. Suspect harmful cooling from exposure plus changes in coordination, speech, thinking, responsiveness, shivering or functional ability; do not depend on an ordinary field thermometer alone.
2. Escalate suspected hypothermia to 000/ambulance or rescue using an Australian-clinician-approved threshold.
3. Prevent further loss to wind, wet surroundings and ground; protect first, then minimise exposure and movement while replacing wet insulation where feasible.
4. Keep a substantially impaired person resting and handle gently; do not direct them to generate heat by exercise.
5. Give food or sugary fluid only when the person is responsive and can safely swallow; exclude alcohol and caffeine from treatment advice.
6. Use active heat only under final reviewed placement, barrier, temperature, monitoring and stop rules.

### Heat pathway

1. Treat altered behaviour, confusion, collapse, seizure, inability to continue or poor control in heat as red flags even if the skin is sweaty or no thermometer is available.
2. For suspected heatstroke, call 000 and begin safe cooling without waiting for a temperature target or delaying the call.
3. Keep reassessing response, breathing and the ability to protect the airway during cooling.
4. Give oral fluid only when fully conscious and able to swallow, under final clinician-approved wording.
5. Separate the five-and-under pathway from older-child/adult methods; do not compress them into one icon.
6. Provide a non-immersion cooling route that is usable without ice, a tub or a large expendable water supply.

### Shelter pathway

1. Define shelter by its required function: stop wind, shed rain, provide shade, insulate from the ground, retain warmth, allow ventilation or improve rescue visibility.
2. Choose the site by comparing the active hazards rather than applying one universal rule.
3. Screen for overhead/falling-tree exposure, flood/watercourse exposure, lightning, wind, unstable ground, rockfall, fire/smoke and escape/access before committing to a site.
4. Prefer carried equipment when it can be deployed safely; natural-material construction must justify its time, exertion, injury and environmental cost.
5. Decide early enough to use daylight when an unscheduled night out is becoming likely.

## Required paired treatment paths

Every eventual cold- or heat-illness page will use `book/MEDICAL_ENTRY_TEMPLATE.md` and split only after common immediate actions.

### With a first-aid kit and the listed shelter supplies

Candidate supply classes, not approved kit items:

- dry insulating layers or a sleeping bag;
- ground insulation;
- windproof/waterproof outer barrier or shelter;
- vapour-barrier material suitable for patient packaging;
- manufacturer-labelled warming devices with safe-use instructions;
- thermometer specifically validated for the intended field use;
- water-delivery, wetting, fanning or cold-pack equipment for heat illness;
- communication, lighting and patient-monitoring aids.

Each final reference must use an item ID from `FIRST_AID_KIT_REGISTER.md`, name the product capability rather than assume all kits contain it, state training requirements and include product-specific limitations.

### Without a first-aid kit or the listed shelter supplies

The no-kit branch may use clothing, shade, windbreaks, dry ordinary materials, a vehicle or building, safe water wetting/fanning, and improvised barriers only where the exact use has been validated. It must state:

- which loss or gain mechanism the action addresses;
- when the ordinary material creates a new hazard;
- what cannot be improvised safely;
- how lack of equipment lowers the threshold for rescue or evacuation;
- how the rescuer avoids becoming a second patient.

No-kit wording must not imply that leaves, branches, skin contact, fire or entering natural water is automatically safe or equivalent to purpose-designed equipment.

### 29 September 2026 carried-sheet working visual

IM-196 now provides a bounded project-synthesis sequence for a carried sheet. Its entry gate requires intact equipment, suitable existing attachment parts, a stable intended support, mild enough setup conditions, an exact setup already known and practised, and capacity to pitch without climbing, unsafe effort, stopping essential care or leaving an injured person. It then screens the site, lays out bedding and weather orientation, uses only the known equipment arrangement, forms a continuously sloping roof and applies inside failure checks.

This does not close the exact-system blocker. IM-196 gives no dimensions, knots, anchor geometry, wind or temperature rating, no-equipment construction or natural-material shelter method. It cannot certify a site, support or attachment. Its subjective estimates are 95% for hazard/prohibition wording, 70% for the project-synthesis setup order and 60% for the new layout; none is a probability of safety, warmth, holding strength or survival. Outdoor and search-and-rescue, exact-equipment, weather, practical, print/reflow and stressed-reader review remain required.

No illustration may label a site, shelter or patient package as “safe”; captions must name residual risks. AI-generated imagery will not be used as evidence of a correct medical technique or a real site hazard.

## Specialist questions

### Australian clinician / wilderness-medicine reviewer

1. What observable signs should control 000 activation when no reliable low-reading or high-reading core thermometer is available?
2. How should the guide sequence sheltering, wet-clothing replacement and movement for mild versus moderate/severe suspected hypothermia?
3. Which active-rewarming methods and body locations are appropriate for a lay rescuer, with what barriers and monitoring?
4. Should any body-to-body technique appear in a compact personal field guide?
5. How should heatstroke and heat exhaustion be differentiated without encouraging delay when signs overlap?
6. When is cold-water immersion appropriate in a remote Victorian setting, and what exclusions must be prominent?
7. What exact oral-fluid wording is safe for cold and heat illness?
8. How should infants/children, older people, pregnancy, chronic disease and prescribed fluid restriction alter the quick pathway?

### Search-and-rescue / outdoor / weather reviewers

1. How should a novice compare overhead-tree risk with wind, lightning, flood, cold-air pooling and rescue visibility?
2. Which site checks are reliable enough for a quick-reference panel, and which give false confidence?
3. When does moving a cold, hot or injured person to shelter outweigh the default stay/findability principle?
4. Which carried shelter types should be tested across alpine, wet forest, dry forest, Mallee and coastal wind scenarios?
5. What ventilation, stove/fire, condensation and carbon-monoxide warnings must be visible for enclosed emergency shelters?

## Field and usability tests required

- Timed deployment in darkness, rain simulation and strong but safe wind for each proposed carried shelter.
- One-person and group setup while wearing gloves and with reduced dexterity simulation.
- Water-shedding, wind ingress, ground insulation and condensation observations; no human cold-stress experiment.
- Shelter-site selection exercises using photographed Victorian scenarios with hidden hazards revealed after choice.
- Patient-packaging rehearsal using a healthy volunteer or mannequin under clinician-approved conditions; no deliberate heating/cooling stress.
- Cooling-path rehearsal with measured water use and continuous airway-access checks; no induced heat illness.
- Novice comprehension test focused on “sweating does not rule it out,” “do not wait for a number” and “a tent is not lightning shelter.”
