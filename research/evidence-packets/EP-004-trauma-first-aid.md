# EP-004 — Burns, head/spinal injury and limb trauma

**Created:** 4 September 2026  
**Chapter:** 06 First aid and evacuation  
**Dependencies:** 02 First minutes; 03 Emergency communication; 04 Stay or move; 05 Temperature and shelter; EP-003 Critical first aid  
**Status:** Evidence packet only — not field-ready guidance

## Purpose

Check the evidence and questions still needing an answer about serious injuries in the Victorian bush:

- burns from heat, breathing smoke or hot gases, electricity and chemicals;
- suspected head injury;
- suspected spinal injury;
- fractures and dislocations of limbs;
- the communication, shelter, hypothermia and evacuation decisions that interact with these injuries.

This packet does not authorise treatment. It deliberately does not cover crush injury, chest/abdominal trauma, eye injury, wound cleaning, pain medicines or prolonged care; those require separate evidence work. Every eventual medical entry must follow `book/MEDICAL_ENTRY_TEMPLATE.md`, show both kit and no-kit paths, and receive qualified Australian clinical review with remote-care competence.

## Evidence strengths

### Burns

- ANZCOR is the primary national source. Its bystander sequence is to make the scene safe, stop the burning process, attend to airway/breathing and other injuries, cool a thermal burn with cool running water for at least 20 minutes as soon as practicable, then cover it loosely. Cooling may still help when started within three hours. [S-093]
- healthdirect and St John independently converge on cool running water, no ice, no creams/lotions, no blister breaking or removal of adherent material, and a light loose non-stick covering. [S-094, S-095]
- ANZCOR expressly permits any clean cool liquid when water is unavailable, but does not quantify how much scarce drinking water a remote person should allocate. A safe remote decision rule therefore remains unresolved. [S-093]
- Cooling the burn is not permission to cool the whole person. ANZCOR and the Royal Children's Hospital require protection of unburnt areas and prevention of hypothermia; paediatric guidance allows pausing or cooling smaller areas sequentially when hypothermia or significant distress develops. [S-093, S-096]
- Chemical, electrical and inhalation injuries cannot be hidden inside one generic burn panel. Chemical exposure requires protection from contamination, removal/brush-off as applicable, prolonged irrigation, Poisons Information advice and no neutralisation. Electrical scenes may remain dangerous. Inhalation injury can deteriorate after an apparently stable interval. [S-093, S-094]

### Suspected spinal injury

- ANZCOR's current sequence places scene safety and 000 first, then airway, breathing and circulation before spinal care. Airway management takes precedence even when movement is required. [S-097]
- A conscious person should be encouraged to remain still and comfortable, without forceful restraint. If movement from immediate danger is necessary, support the injured area and minimise spinal motion. An unconscious person who is breathing should be placed in the recovery position with gentle handling and alignment; preserving the airway overrides a motion-restriction goal. [S-097]
- ANZCOR warns against relying on motion-restriction devices, especially for an unconscious person. The Wilderness Medical Society independently rejects rigid collars and backboards as wilderness immobilisation tools and favours goal-oriented spinal cord protection rather than absolute immobilisation. [S-097, S-098]
- The WMS guidance is valuable remote-context evidence, but its clinical clearance tools and specialist packaging methods are beyond an untrained reader. The book must not teach a lay reader to “clear” their own spine. [S-098]

### Head injury

- ANZCOR warns that significant head injury can exist without visible injury, loss of consciousness or amnesia and may deteriorate hours later. These features must not be used as clearance tests. [S-099]
- Immediate ambulance escalation is required for loss or alteration of consciousness and other red flags; all head injuries require professional assessment before return to activity. Airway, breathing, significant bleeding and spinal precautions remain linked priorities. [S-099]
- St John and the Victorian Better Health Channel corroborate the possibility of delayed deterioration and the need for observation, 000 for serious injury, careful movement and attention to both head and spine. [S-100, S-101]
- No selected current national source supports a simple “keep them awake” rule. The book should teach observation and escalation, not sleep deprivation or a lay concussion score. [S-099, S-101]

### Limb fracture or dislocation

- It may be difficult for a layperson to distinguish fracture, dislocation, sprain and strain. St John and Better Health advise treating uncertainty as fracture and not forcing a broken or dislocated structure back into place. [S-102, S-104]
- Australian sources converge on keeping the injured part still, controlling serious bleeding, covering wounds, supporting/splinting where appropriate, and seeking medical care. An open fracture, unusual shape/colour, severe pain, major accident, unconsciousness or possible head/neck/back injury raises urgency. [S-102, S-103, S-104]
- A support or bandage can create a new circulation problem. healthdirect and St John require checks for numbness, pallor/coldness or other loss of circulation after support is applied. [S-103, S-104]
- The selected public sources do not adequately resolve whether an untrained, injured, possibly solo reader should construct a splint before rescue, use the body as a splint, or leave a comfortable limb undisturbed. That choice depends on injury, environment, evacuation time, movement necessity and demonstrated skill. It remains blocked for remote-clinician review and field testing.

## Important contradictions and operational tensions

### Twenty minutes of cooling versus whole-person hypothermia

Cooling a thermal burn promptly improves outcomes, but large-area cooling, cold ambient conditions, wet clothing and children can cause dangerous whole-person heat loss. The final entry must show simultaneous protection of unburnt areas and a reviewed stop/pause branch for hypothermia or significant distress. It cannot reduce this to either “always finish 20 minutes” or “stop when uncomfortable.” [S-093, S-094, S-096]

### Burn cooling versus scarce bush water

ANZCOR allows any clean cool liquid if running water is unavailable, yet does not answer how to balance burn benefit against dehydration when a stranded person has only limited potable water. Surface water may also carry biological or chemical contamination. The guide will not prescribe a fixed allocation until a burns/remote-medicine clinician and water-safety reviewer resolve the scenario. [S-093]

### Cling film is a covering, not a universal burn treatment

ANZCOR permits clean plastic cling film laid loosely over an appropriate thermal burn, but not wrapped around a limb and not on the face. It also prohibits cling film and hydrogel on chemical burns. The diagram must not show circumferential wrapping or let a reader transfer the material to every burn type. [S-093, S-094, S-096]

### A breathing person may still have a dangerous inhalation injury

Facial burns, soot, black sputum, hoarseness, respiratory distress or an enclosed-space fire can indicate inhalation injury, and deterioration may be delayed. A reassuring appearance must not authorise staying in place or self-clearing. Fresh air is approached only when the atmosphere and route are safe; a rescuer must not enter a toxic or burning atmosphere. [S-093, S-096]

### Airway care outranks spinal stillness

Older first-aid messages are often remembered as “never move them.” Current ANZCOR says an airway must be opened and an unconscious breathing person placed in the recovery position even when spinal injury is suspected, using gentle aligned handling. The field visual must make this hierarchy unmistakable and must not imply that a lone rescuer can achieve perfect immobilisation. [S-097, S-099]

### “Immobilise the spine” can cause harmful improvisation

Current ANZCOR says not to rely on devices, and the WMS recommends against rigid collars and backboards as wilderness immobilisation tools. Better Health's older suggestion to place rolled cloth around the neck is, at most, a comfort/reminder concept; it must not be converted into a tight collar or presented as preventing movement. No improvised cervical-collar or backboard construction will be taught. [S-097, S-098, S-101]

### Immediate-danger movement versus waiting for rescuers

ANZCOR allows movement from water, roads or another immediate danger while minimising motion. Remote settings also create secondary threats such as cold, fire, flood, falling limbs and an unsafe vehicle. Those threats are not permission for routine self-evacuation. The final decision must join the injury page to the stay/move and shelter logic and distinguish a live danger from mere discomfort or rescue delay. [S-097, S-098]

### Head-injury improvement does not clear the person

A person may briefly improve and then deteriorate, and a significant injury may exist without loss of consciousness. The guide must not use “feels better,” normal conversation, a single memory question or lack of external injury as a move/wait clearance test. [S-099, S-101]

### A first-aid splint is not a fracture reduction

Public sources support padded splinting and repeated circulation checks but prohibit forcing the structure into place. An untested improvised splint can increase pain, move bone ends, conceal bleeding, compromise circulation or consume critical time. Exact kit and no-kit methods require hands-on trials, including application by a solo person and through clothing. [S-102, S-103, S-104]

### Lost circulation after dislocation is an emergency, not a massage task

St John says to call 000 when circulation is absent but also includes an isolated instruction to massage the limb gently. The other selected Australian sources support emergency escalation and prohibit lay reduction, but do not corroborate massage. The book will omit massage unless a current emergency/orthopaedic reviewer supplies a clearly bounded indication. [S-103, S-104]

### Pain medicine is not a generic kit/no-kit branch

healthdirect lists common pain medicines for fractures, but suitability varies with allergy, age, pregnancy, bleeding, dehydration, kidney/liver disease, other medicines and injury type. Medication instructions require a separate evidence packet, medicine register and clinician/pharmacist review. No analgesic dose enters this trauma packet. [S-103]

## Candidate propositions under gate control

These propositions are tracked in `CONTENT_REGISTER.md`; none is approved reader instruction.

### Burns

1. Make the scene safe, stop the burning process and address airway/breathing and other immediate threats before focusing on the skin injury.
2. Cool an appropriate thermal burn with cool running water for at least 20 minutes as soon as practicable, while keeping the rest of the person warm and monitoring for hypothermia/distress.
3. Remove constricting items and non-adherent wet clothing without peeling away anything stuck; then cover the cooled burn loosely with a reviewed non-stick material.
4. Do not use ice/ice water, creams/lotions/powders, break blisters or attempt to neutralise a chemical burn.
5. Separate thermal, inhalation, electrical and chemical branches, including scene hazards, 000/13 11 26 escalation and covering exceptions.

### Head and spinal injury

1. Treat airway and breathing before spinal motion restriction; call 000 early for suspected serious head/spinal injury.
2. Encourage a conscious person to remain still and comfortable, but move them from an immediate danger when necessary with support and minimum practical motion.
3. Place an unconscious breathing person in the recovery position with gentle aligned handling; use CPR when not breathing normally.
4. Do not use loss of consciousness, visible injury, memory, apparent improvement or a lay concussion score to clear a head injury.
5. Do not construct or apply a rigid improvised collar/backboard as a medical immobilisation tool.

### Limb fracture or dislocation

1. When the injury type is uncertain, manage it as a possible fracture and do not force it straight or back into place.
2. Control serious bleeding and protect wounds without pushing exposed bone or an object back in.
3. Support the injury in a comfortable position with a reviewed kit or no-kit method only when doing so does not require harmful manipulation or delay a more urgent action.
4. Recheck circulation and sensation after any support/bandage and escalate urgently for numbness, pallor/coldness, unusual colour or severe deterioration.
5. Link severe/open/axial injuries and an inability to move safely to 000/rescue rather than an unsupported walk-out rule.

## Required paired treatment paths

### With a first-aid kit

Candidate capability classes, not approved kit entries:

- non-latex gloves and a safe contamination barrier;
- abundant clean water access or a reviewed irrigation capability that does not imply a small saline ampoule can replace 20 minutes of cooling;
- a large loose non-adherent burn covering, with cling-film constraints made explicit if selected;
- sterile non-adherent wound dressings and bleeding-control materials;
- broad bandages, triangular bandages/slings, padding and a selected malleable or rigid limb-splint capability;
- insulation and weather protection for the whole person during prolonged care;
- communication, lighting, time recording and monitoring materials.

No rigid cervical collar or backboard is proposed for the personal kit. Any exact burn dressing, splint or bandage must receive a `KIT-` ID, size/quantity/storage rules, training prerequisite, clinician sign-off and task-based testing before it appears in a checklist.

### Without a first-aid kit

This also applies if the kit is missing the item needed for the condition.

The no-kit path cannot pretend that every manufactured item has an equivalent:

- Burns: use safe available cool running water or another clinician-reviewed clean cool liquid; protect the rest of the body from cold. A small drinking-water reserve is not automatically sacrificed under an unreviewed rule. A clean, loose, non-fluffy ordinary covering may be considered only after material and contamination review.
- Head/spine: the central tools are scene safety, 000/contact, airway care, the person's own stillness, gentle aligned handling and environmental protection. Do not build a rigid neck collar/backboard. Soft clothing used for comfort or as a movement reminder must not constrict breathing or claim immobilisation.
- Limb trauma: the least-manipulation option may be support in the position found. Clothing, a blanket, the uninjured body part or found rigid materials are only candidates after circulation, pressure-point, durability and solo-application tests. Do not straighten a deformity to fit an improvised splint.

Every no-kit page must identify what cannot be improvised, what added risk comes from missing equipment, and when maintaining manual support while awaiting rescue is safer than attempting equipment construction.

### 30 September 2026 ordinary-thermal-burn working card

D-067/IM-203 now put the current S-093–S-096 agreement into a three-part text-led prompt. Part 1 stops at fire, toxic-air and live-electrical danger, sends abnormal breathing to the canonical CPR route and makes serious-or-uncertain 000 escalation visible. It allows only an ordinary external heat burn in a safe scene to continue. Part 2 shows the at-least-20-minute cool-running-water route, non-adherent-item removal and simultaneous whole-person warmth. Part 3 shows broad kit/no-kit loose-cover classes, prohibited actions, continued monitoring and the Chapter 3 failed-contact route.

The card deliberately supplies no burn-depth or body-area diagnosis, scarce drinking-water allocation, untreated-natural-water substitute, chemical decontamination mechanics, electrical approach distance, smoke-entry or inhalation clearance, cling-film technique, product/medicine instruction or self-directed hypothermia pause threshold. The displayed 95% core-source-wording, 70% remote-boundary and 60% layout figures are subjective editorial estimates, not probabilities of treatment safety, success, recovery or survival. Australian burns/emergency, paediatric, remote-care, toxicology/electrical, practical, complete-card, detached-panel and stressed-reader review remain open. `FIELD_READY_BUILD=NO` remains controlling.

## Specialist questions

### Burns / emergency / remote-medicine clinician

1. What remote rule safely balances 20-minute cooling with scarce potable water, questionable surface water and long rescue times?
2. What observable whole-person signs should pause or modify cooling, especially for children, large burns, alpine weather and a wet casualty?
3. Which ordinary cool liquids and coverings are safe enough to name when running water or a sterile dressing is unavailable?
4. Which burn sizes/sites/mechanisms require immediate 000 rather than delayed professional review in Victoria?
5. How should the field guide handle inhalation injury when mobile reception is absent and self-movement may be unavoidable?
6. Are any commercial hydrogel or burn-dressing products useful enough to carry, given ANZCOR's water-first rule and chemical-burn prohibition?

### Head/spine / wilderness-emergency clinician

1. Which mechanisms and red flags should a lay reader use to suspect spinal injury without attempting clinical clearance?
2. What is the safest lone-rescuer recovery-position method when spinal injury is suspected and the person is breathing?
3. How should “position of comfort,” manual support and movement from live environmental danger be illustrated?
4. Is any soft improvised support appropriate to show, or is verbal stillness plus manual support safer for this guide?
5. What observation interval and record fields are useful for head injury without implying that repeated checks can clear the injury?
6. What exact head/spinal presentations prohibit self-evacuation, and what exceptions exist when no rescue contact is possible?

### Orthopaedic / emergency / remote-medicine clinician

1. Which limb injuries should be supported in the position found rather than splinted by a novice?
2. Which one or two splinting methods are worth teaching, and can a solo injured person apply them without harmful manipulation?
3. What distal circulation/sensation check can a lay reader perform reliably, and what change requires loosening support versus urgent rescue only?
4. How should an open fracture be covered and bleeding controlled without pressure on exposed bone?
5. Should cold-pack/elevation advice appear in an emergency field guide, or does it distract from support and evacuation?
6. Confirm that limb massage for absent circulation after dislocation should be excluded.

## Validation tests required

- Run burn scenarios in cold/wet and hot/dry conditions using measured water volumes, without applying harmful temperatures to a person.
- Test candidate loose coverings for adherence, circumferential constriction errors, contamination, wind handling and face/chemical transfer errors.
- Use manikins or trained actors for airway/recovery-position and immediate-danger movement tasks; record twisting, delays and comprehension errors.
- Test candidate limb supports on task trainers with objective distal circulation/pressure checks, including gloved, cold, low-light and solo application.
- Run delayed-recall tests at one week and one month; confidence without correct action counts as failure.
- Test every trauma page against the communications, stay/move, temperature and shelter chapters for priority contradictions.
