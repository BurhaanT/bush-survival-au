# Personal locator beacon candidate assessment

**Assessment date:** 4 September 2026  
**Scope:** Personal Victorian bush-emergency guide; activity-agnostic land use, with incidental inland/coastal water exposure  
**Gate status:** Conditional shortlist only. No device is approved for purchase or reader-facing recommendation until the pre-purchase and field-usability checks below are completed.

## Decision now

Compare three personal locator beacons (PLBs) supplied for Australia. Each uses satellite positioning (GNSS/GPS) and the 406 MHz distress-alert system. The model names and technical specifications below remain exact so the equipment can be checked:

1. **GME MT620GR (Australian-coded model MT620GRAUS)** — provisional field-test leader. Its material advantage is Return Link Service (RLS), which can acknowledge that the distress alert has been received and routed. It does not establish that a rescue has been launched or when rescuers will arrive.
2. **GME MT610G (Australian-coded model MT610GAUS)** — value alternative. It retains the same core 406 MHz, 121.5 MHz homing, GNSS, buoyancy, environmental and minimum operating-duration capabilities without RLS.
3. **Ocean Signal rescueME PLB1, supplied and coded for Australia/New Zealand** — size/weight alternative. It is substantially smaller and lighter, but it lacks RLS and relies on the permanently attached flotation pouch for Australian buoyancy compliance.

This is not a brand endorsement. The preferred product can change after expiry-date inspection, servicing quotation, Australian coding confirmation and physical testing. The ACR ResQLink range and marine-focused AIS/RLS products are not rejected as unsafe; they are outside this first shortlist because no clearer activity-neutral land advantage was established from a comparably verified Australian supply/service record.

## Capability boundary

A PLB is for grave and imminent danger when emergency services cannot be reached by phone or radio. It sends an identity and location-capable distress alert over the Cospas-Sarsat system and transmits 121.5 MHz for local homing. It is not routine messaging, tracking, weather data or a two-way conversation. A satellite messenger/phone is assessed separately under `EQ-004` and must not be allowed to obscure the PLB's simpler independent role.

AMSA advises that a land PLB stays with the individual rather than the vehicle and is physically carried on the body or within easy reach. Buying a PLB only to leave it in a vehicle, boat or inaccessible pack does not satisfy the project's separation scenario.

## Material trade-offs

### MT620GR

**Why it leads:** RLS can reduce a dangerous temptation to move, repeatedly manipulate the beacon or assume it failed merely because no response is visible. Australian manufacture and a single identified Sydney service route simplify the maintenance record.

**Why it may lose:** It costs more, is newer with fewer owner reports, and its app/NFC feature can be overvalued. The app is not part of the emergency activation path. RLS itself must be taught precisely: acknowledgement is not a two-way message, rescue confirmation or arrival estimate.

### MT610G

**Why it remains strong:** The core distress, GNSS, homing, duration, flotation and environmental specifications closely match the MT620GR at a lower current price. Its simpler indication set may be easier to teach.

**Why it may lose:** There is no acknowledgement that the system received/routed the distress alert. A stressed user therefore needs stronger training to deploy it correctly, leave it on and continue shelter/signalling/monitoring without an electronic reassurance.

### PLB1

**Why it remains strong:** The lower mass and smaller body make on-person carriage more likely, which may matter more than extra features if the alternative is a PLB left behind. Ocean Signal's manual provides a cradle/tether workflow and AMSA lists a certified Melbourne servicing agent.

**Why it may lose:** The Australian flotation pouch adds bulk and attachment complexity. The device must be kept above water and is not self-righting. Its manual also specifies a tightly limited GNSS-test budget, so the exact test schedule must be followed rather than improvised.

## Non-negotiable pre-purchase checks

The purchaser or delegated checker must record all of the following for the exact unit offered:

- model/SKU and Australian country coding, not a visually identical US/Canadian/imported unit;
- ability to register the HEX ID/UIN with AMSA before relying on it;
- marked battery replacement/expiry date and remaining service life;
- sealed packaging condition, included lanyard/pouch/case and current manual revision;
- current Australian manufacturer-approved service route, battery replacement price, turnaround and freight;
- current manufacturer and ACCC safety/recall search for the exact model and batch/serial, recognising that a negative search is not proof of no defect;
- current price from at least two authorised Australian sellers;
- return rights if coding, expiry, condition or registration is wrong.

Do not buy a cheaper overseas-coded or US/Canadian beacon for Australian registration. AMSA warns that these may not satisfy Australian standards/coding and may be impossible to register without manufacturer recoding.

## Registration and maintenance control

Before carriage:

1. Register the exact HEX ID/UIN with AMSA; registration is free.
2. Record at least one 24-hour emergency contact and give that person the trip/location/vehicle/group information they need.
3. Update trip details for the outing and update owner/contact/status information when it changes. Current AMSA registrations no longer have a routine expiry, but stale details can still delay response.
4. Carry accessible proof of registration where legally relevant and keep an offline copy with the emergency plan.
5. Perform only the manufacturer-prescribed self-test at the prescribed frequency. Over-testing consumes the sealed emergency battery.
6. Arrange manufacturer/approved service before marked expiry, after any activation or on a failed/used-battery indication. A successful self-test does not prove continuing compliance after unauthorised opening or battery work.

Permanent storage in an exposed vehicle is not approved. The manuals impose maximum storage temperatures, and GME warns against continuous direct sunlight and chemical/solvent contact. Actual on-body and vehicle storage arrangements need temperature and contamination checks.

## Safe field-usability test

No live distress activation is permitted. Use the manual's self-test only within its frequency/energy limits, plus an inert/demonstration rehearsal if available.

Test and record:

- Australian registration and current trip-detail workflow;
- secure on-person attachment and tether, including separation from pack/vehicle/boat;
- retrieval with either hand while seated, prone and wearing likely outer layers;
- identification and dry deployment of antenna/control sequence in daylight, darkness, rain simulation and gloves;
- ability of the owner and at least one companion to distinguish activation, GNSS fix, RLS wait/receipt where relevant, test failure and battery/service indications;
- stable land deployment with antenna vertical, GNSS area uncovered and broad sky view without holding the unit or creating a trip hazard;
- above-water/lifejacket configuration only under the lifejacket and beacon manufacturers' instructions—no immersion activation test;
- safe re-stow after a non-distress rehearsal without bending/damaging the antenna or creating accidental-activation pressure;
- comprehension that the beacon stays on after genuine activation until rescue-service advice, and that RLS does not promise launch or arrival;
- access to shelter, first aid, non-fire signalling and monitoring while the PLB remains correctly deployed.

A pass requires zero critical errors by the owner and trained companion after a delay, not merely successful performance immediately after reading the manual.

## Provisional recommendation

**Field-test the GME MT620GR first**, with the MT610G as the direct value comparator. Test the PLB1 only if its lower mass/volume is likely to change whether it will truly be carried on the body. Select the cheapest candidate that the user will reliably carry, can deploy without critical error and can maintain through its service life; do not pay for app features as if they improve distress transmission.

The book may eventually recommend a capability and selection checklist rather than one permanent model. Product names, prices, manuals, coding and service routes are currentness-sensitive and must be rechecked for every edition.
