# Shelter and insulation system assessment

**Status:** Conditional research shortlist - no product or reader-facing procedure is approved  
**Scope:** Personal Victorian guide; activity-agnostic; cold, wet, wind, heat and rescue-wait use  
**Evidence basis:** EP-002 and EP-010, S-057, S-061, S-064-S-071, S-178-S-191 and S-247-S-258  
**Checked:** 4 September 2026

## Short conclusion

Plan shelter as several jobs, not one “emergency blanket”. The proposal needs a checked site, quick protection from wind and rain, insulation from the ground, dry layers that hold warmth around the person, and protected storage. It also needs a separate shade option with airflow. Exact methods and products still need testing and review.

No single bivvy, bothy, tarp, pad, sleeping bag, reflective sheet or vehicle performs all of those functions.

## What this assessment does and does not approve

This assessment identifies products worth obtaining for non-hazardous comparison. It does not tell the reader that any candidate is adequate for a particular Victorian night, casualty or storm. Product dimensions, warmth, waterproofing, stock and prices can change. Exact samples still require identity checks, controlled deployment and wet-weather tests, fit testing, specialist review and a recall/currentness check.

The final book must preserve four distinctions:

1. **Personal emergency enclosure:** something one person can deploy quickly around themselves or a casualty.
2. **Ground insulation:** a separate barrier against conductive heat loss.
3. **Body insulation:** dry clothing or a tested sleeping system with enough loft and coverage.
4. **Overhead/group shelter or shade:** useful working and waiting space, but not a substitute for individual cold packaging.

## Evidence that changes the product decision

### Compact emergency bags help, but they are not equal to a full system

Wilderness Medical Society guidance treats insulation, wind/vapour barriers, ground insulation and head/neck protection as components of an enclosure. It also notes that more effective systems are often heavier and bulkier. [S-061]

In a small crossover study of seven healthy young men who were shivering and near-hypothermic, a multi-layer metallised Blizzard Survival bag produced higher skin temperatures and required less shivering heat production than a single-layer polyethylene survival bag during three hours at 0 C. Core rewarming and thermal comfort were similar across the tested portable methods. The study did not establish performance in women, children, older people or injured casualties, and Blizzard supplied the products without charge. [S-249]

A separate still-air, dry-condition thermal-manikin comparison found that the Blizzard bag reduced cooling more than a plain plastic survival bag, but substantially heavier casualty systems insulated better. This is useful contradiction evidence against the manufacturer's "total warmth and shelter" language. It also does not simulate Victorian rain, wind, wet ground, fit or human ventilation. [S-250]

The result is a limited but defensible conclusion: a multi-layer emergency bag is a better research candidate than a plain single-layer plastic or foil bag, but it does not remove the need for ground insulation, dry loft, safe positioning, rescue or clinical review.

### A pad rating compares pads, not whole-person survival

ASTM F3340 measures steady-state vertical thermal resistance under controlled compression. ASTM explicitly warns that real-world edge losses, uncovered areas, compression and posture can make actual performance differ. [S-248]

A closed-cell foam pad does not deflate after puncture, but is bulky and a low R-value model may be inadequate in colder conditions. An insulated inflatable can provide more measured resistance for its packed volume, but adds puncture, valve, inflation, moisture and repair failure modes. The final system may use both, but no combined warmth promise is approved.

### Sleeping-bag labels are conditional

ISO 23537-1:2022 provides a standard method and labelling basis for adult recreational sleeping bags with limit temperatures of -20 C and higher. It does not cover children, babies, military bags or extreme-climate expedition products. [S-247]

The eventual selection must use the relevant **comfort** result and a conservative personal margin, not the "extreme" figure. The rating is a baseline comparison, not a guarantee: fit, pad, shelter, clothing, wind, wetness, age, health, fatigue and individual cold sensitivity still matter. [S-247, S-257]

## Rejected shortcuts

- **Single thin foil blanket as the primary cold shelter:** rejected. It can serve as a wind/vapour-barrier component when properly used, but it does not supply dependable loft or ground insulation. [S-061, S-249, S-250]
- **One shared bothy instead of one cold package per person:** rejected. An occupant-supported group shell can fail when someone is alone, supine, unconscious or must be treated.
- **A tarp as an unpractised universal shelter:** rejected. It is useful only when site, anchors, cordage, setup time, wind and drainage work together.
- **R-value as a safe-temperature conversion:** rejected by the scope and limitations of ASTM F3340. [S-248]
- **Sleeping-bag extreme rating as a buying target:** rejected. Use a reviewed comfort-based selection with margin. [S-247, S-257]
- **Survival bag as permission to remain in a fire, flood, falling-tree, lightning, CO or unstable alpine hazard:** rejected. Site and immediate-hazard decisions remain upstream.
- **Vehicle as a universally safe shelter:** rejected. A hard-top vehicle may be preferred for lightning, but location, flood, fire, heat, exhaust/CO and tree hazards still control. Do not burn fuel or idle for enclosure heat under the present evidence architecture.
- **Reflective cold enclosure for heat illness or sun shelter:** rejected. Hot-weather shade requires airflow and monitoring; an enclosed vapour barrier can worsen heat stress.

## Recommended system architecture

### Layer 1 - carried per person

- A personal, high-visibility emergency enclosure that can be reached without unpacking the whole kit.
- A dependable way to separate torso and preferably the whole body from cold/wet ground.
- Dry head/body insulation protected in its own tested closure.
- The PLB, whistle, light and location record remain usable without leaving the shelter system.

The Blizzard BPS02 and full-length Z Lite SOL are the first compact enclosure/ground pair worth obtaining for controlled testing. That is a test priority, not a purchase instruction or a declaration that the pair is sufficient for all Victoria.

### Layer 2 - vehicle, camp or group augmentation

- A correctly sized ISO-rated conventional sleeping bag for each person.
- A higher-R mat or a layered foam/inflatable system, with failure testing.
- The Sandfly Reflect for shade/rain working space where its extra mass is acceptable.
- A size-matched bothy only as an additional short-duration group shell.
- Extra insulation and dry storage sized for a casualty who cannot share gear or sit upright.

### Layer 3 - no carried shelter

The no-equipment path is not allowed to imply equivalence. It may eventually use existing clothing, a pack, vehicle components and low-risk site features only after each exact action is tested and reviewed. Cutting live vegetation, stripping bark, unstable debris shelters, entering mines/caves/drains, sleeping in watercourses and burning fuel in or beside an enclosure remain excluded.

## Controlled test programme

No test deliberately cools, overheats, injures or exhausts a person. Use an able volunteer only for benign fit and usability; use a dressed manikin or weighted dummy for casualty packaging.

### 1. Identity and document check

- Record exact manufacturer, model, SKU, revision, size, colour, batch and purchase source.
- Photograph labels, instructions, dimensions and mass.
- Reconcile every web/manual/packaging discrepancy in writing with the manufacturer.
- Check Australian stock, warranty, repair route, recall notices and replacement interval.

### 2. Dry deployment and fit

- Time deployment from the real storage location in daylight, darkness and gloves.
- Test solo use, helper use and a supine dummy without covering the face or obstructing monitoring.
- Confirm length, shoulder/hip/boot room, entry/exit, zip/drawcord snagging and access to phone/PLB/whistle/light.
- Record errors, assistance, repacking time and whether the duty unit can remain sealed.

### 3. Benign wet and wind test

- Use controlled hose/rain and fan conditions on an unoccupied or manikin-loaded setup.
- Inspect seams, closure, drainage, flapping, anchors, runoff onto the sleep area and water pooling.
- Weigh insulation before/after and inspect for inner condensation; do not infer human warmth.
- Stop for uncontrolled movement, tearing, water entry near the face or anchor/site instability.

### 4. Ground-system test

- Measure actual body coverage on firm, uneven and damp representative surfaces without exposing a person to cold stress.
- For inflatables, perform overnight leak checks, safe puncture simulations on a spare/sample and timed field repair.
- Test foam wind retention and whether the pad can be positioned beneath a supine dummy with minimal movement.

### 5. Shade and ventilation test

- Measure shade footprint and air temperature at multiple heights/times against an unsheltered reference under safe weather.
- Test silver/rain orientations, ventilation, guy-line trip visibility and wind stability.
- Do not use a benign shade result to claim treatment of heatstroke or safe occupation during extreme/catastrophic fire weather.

### 6. Storage and lifecycle test

- Pack the exact dry insulation; mark closure depth and confirm it can still be opened with wet/gloved hands.
- Use dry paper indicators and controlled spray/splash tests; no intentional deep submersion unless the exact product is rated and the test is safely designed.
- Inspect after heat/cold storage cycles within manufacturer limits, compression and ordinary transport abrasion.
- Establish calendar inspection, airing/drying, reproofing, repair and replacement triggers.

## Approval blockers

IM-196 is now a bounded generic carried-sheet setup and failure-check visual. It reduces the earlier absence of any practical visual but does not satisfy the exact-system illustration or testing blocker below: it deliberately omits dimensions, knots, anchor geometry, ratings and no-equipment or natural-material construction, and it cannot prove site or attachment safety.

Reader-facing instructions remain blocked until all of the following are complete:

- current product/manual and exact-sample reconciliation;
- Victorian outdoor/SAR review of deployment, visibility, casualty and movement interactions;
- wilderness-clinical review where the system packages a cold, injured or unresponsive person;
- controlled use tests above, with recorded errors and stop rules;
- personal and casualty-dummy fit checks;
- a separate hot-weather shade/ventilation decision;
- exact illustrations made from the tested system, then checked after page layout;
- final currentness/recall/stock check.

## Present recommendation

Proceed with a small comparative test set rather than buying a complete permanent kit:

1. one Blizzard BPS02 test sample plus, if supply permits, a separate sealed duty sample;
2. one full-length Z Lite SOL and one Ether Light XR Regular for failure-mode comparison;
3. one Sandfly Reflect for the vehicle/group shade-rain role;
4. the founder's existing sleeping bag, mat, dry storage and clothing recorded before buying duplicates.

Do not buy the SOL Escape, bothy or a new sleeping bag until the first tests show which gap remains. The current evidence gives each of those a legitimate comparator role, but not enough benefit to justify immediate duplication.
