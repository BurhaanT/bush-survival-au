# Wild-food third-wave review

**Date:** 7 September 2026  
**Scope:** River mint, cranberry heath, sweet quandong and old-man saltbush for the private Victorian bush-survival guide.  
**Status:** Research synthesis and editorial gate only. No entry is approved for first-time field identification or consumption. Twelve source-labelled, rights-recorded photographs have been selected and visually checked; none has been botanically authenticated for this guide.

## Decision summary

| River mint — SP-019 | **Education only; no eating, tea, oil or extract** | Exact taxon, Victorian wet-habitat occurrence and documented Djab Wurrung/cooking context are available | A 2026 volatile study found consistent pulegone in sampled profiles, including one Victorian-source plant with a 70% relative peak share; no absolute leaf exposure or human serving can be calculated |

These decisions concern inclusion in a high-consequence emergency guide. They do not declare traditional foods inherently unsafe, and they do not replace advice from knowledge holders, botanists, toxicologists or food-safety professionals.

## Evidence and inference rules used here

- **Evidence** means the cited source directly supports the stated taxon, feature, measured composition, recorded use or legal rule.
- **Inference** means a conclusion drawn from evidence but not directly tested, such as likely low calorie return from a very small pulp volume.
- **Editorial precaution** is a deliberately conservative guide rule adopted because consequential evidence is missing. It must not be worded as a proven biological fact.
- A percentage in a chemical extract is not a percentage of the fresh plant and is not a human dose.
- A commercial or cultivated food result is not automatically representative of a Victorian wild plant.
- A livestock toxicity or intake limit is not a human food limit.
- Taste, smell, animal feeding and a small trial bite are never identification or safety tests.

## Shared legal, site and cultural boundaries

1. Regulation 41 of the Victorian National Parks Regulations 2024 generally prohibits cutting, picking, removing, taking, damaging or destroying vegetation in a park. Source: [National Parks Regulations 2024](https://www.legislation.vic.gov.au/in-force/statutory-rules/national-parks-regulations-2024) and [authorised PDF](https://content.legislation.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2024-08/24-76sra001-authorised.pdf).
2. Victorian state-forest produce controls can cover leaves, seeds, flowers, fruit and bushfoods. Source: [Forest produce licence](https://www.vic.gov.au/forest-produce-licence).
3. Threatened and protected flora have additional controls. Source: [Victorian protected-flora guidance](https://www.environment.vic.gov.au/conserving-threatened-species/protected-flora-and-listed-fish).
4. Private land requires the landholder's permission. The guide should not attempt to settle whether an emergency necessity defence would apply; it should teach lawful preparation and avoidance of foreseeable need.
5. Reject any plant with uncertain spray, sewage, livestock-waste, roadside, mine, industrial, flood or harmful-algal exposure. Washing and cooking do not prove chemical contamination has been removed.
6. A public cultural source permits accurate attribution of what it says; it does not automatically grant permission to reproduce a method, detach knowledge from Country or turn it into generic “Aboriginal knowledge.”

## SP-018 — Cranberry heath

### Taxonomy, range and season — evidence

- Accepted Victorian taxon: *Styphelia humifusa* (Cav.) Pers. [VicFlora accepted account](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/7875b303-cd9e-40dd-9894-304dbe0c29b9).
- *Astroloma humifusum* (Cav.) R.Br. is a homotypic synonym, not an additional edible species. [VicFlora synonym record](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/6fd6774a-6076-4e07-8f64-3a81f4bf94ac).
- VicFlora describes it as common and widespread from near-coastal to inland Victoria, especially in heathy to grassy woodland and open forest, across lowland, montane and alpine bioregions. Flowers January–July.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Prostrate, decumbent or erect shrub to about 70 cm.
- Crowded linear to oblanceolate leaves 5–18 mm long and 0.6–3 mm wide, with a sharp mucro and sparsely toothed/ciliate margins.
- Red cylindrical corolla tube 7–12 mm long, with five internal hair tufts or rarely a complete hair ring; lobes are densely bearded internally.
- Spherical to ellipsoid fruit 5–10 mm long. It is green when ripe, sometimes purple-tinged or ultimately dark purple.
- VicFlora calls the species variable and its [key to *Styphelia*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/key/2309) uses flower, anther and filament characters. Fruit colour alone is not a safe shortcut.

### Food, taste and energy

- The [Australian National Botanic Gardens historical summary](https://www.anbg.gov.au/apu/plants/astrhumi.html) describes a small fruit with sweet, sticky pulp surrounding a stone and records raw use. A [south-eastern NSW flora resource](https://apps.lucidcentral.org/plants_se_nsw/text/entities/styphelia_humifusa.htm) also describes the fleshy fruit as edible and green to purple when ripe.
- These sources support the existence of a food history. They do not establish an emergency serving, universal palatability or the safety of a specimen identified only from fruit.
- No defensible species-specific fresh-pulp kilojoule, moisture, macronutrient, allergen-threshold or human serving dataset was located.
- **Inference:** the 5–10 mm fruit and large stone suggest little recoverable pulp and probably poor energy return. This has not been quantified for Victorian wild fruit and must not be printed as calories.

### Risks, contradictions and unknowns

- “Cranberry” is a common-name analogy; it does not make the plant equivalent to commercial cranberries.
- A green ripe fruit contradicts the familiar assumption that colour change proves ripeness.
- The hard stone presents an ordinary tooth/choking risk, but no frequency data exist.
- No contemporary human toxicology or allergy study was located. Absence of a report is not evidence of unrestricted safety.
- A Mount Alexander seasonal calendar compiled by Dja Dja Wurrung Elder and cultural adviser Uncle Rick Nelson, Karl Just and named collaborators records **Gurndi** and berries ready to eat. A separate Surf Coast page says its cranberry-heath information was supplied by the then-named Wathaurung Aboriginal Corporation, now Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation, and records small edible fruit sought by children. These are local, Nation-specific public records; they do not provide one statewide season, a serving or republication permission. See S-377.

### Current gate

Keep education-only. Before any actionable version: a Victorian botanist must approve a flowering-and-fruiting diagnostic plate and matched local *Styphelia* comparisons; a food-safety/toxicology reviewer must approve the exact edible-part wording; a nutrition reviewer must either validate a wet-basis result or retain “unknown”; and the relevant Dja Dja Wurrung and Wadawurrung custodians must decide any fuller cultural scope or reuse. Run a novice false-positive test without tasting.

## SP-019 — River mint

### Taxonomy, range and season — evidence

- Accepted taxon: *Mentha australis* R.Br. [VicFlora](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/c07e6892-942b-41de-bb6e-515108ee79ba).
- Authoritative interstate morphology and habitat comparison: [PlantNET](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Mentha~australis&page=nswfl).
- It is associated with clay-rich margins of rivers, creeks, swamps and wet depressions. A [Yarra Ranges local profile](https://www.yarraranges.vic.gov.au/PlantDirectory/Herbs-Groundcovers/Mentha-australis) records flowering mainly September–March locally. PlantNET gives February–October in NSW. These differing regional records mean the guide must not promise a single Victorian availability window.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Soft herb, often sprawling but sometimes erect; branches may be purple-tinged.
- Opposite narrow-ovate to lance-shaped leaves, commonly 1.2–6 cm by 8–20 mm, pointed, shortly hairy and with approximately 4–14 teeth.
- Dense clusters of three to twelve white or pink flowers occur in the leaf axils.
- PlantNET records a 13-veined calyx with pointed hairy lobes. This is more diagnostic than aroma but requires flowers and close inspection.

### Food, taste and cultural evidence

- A [South Australian government native-food sheet](https://cdn.environment.sa.gov.au/landscape/docs/mr/Native-food-fact-sheet_river-mint.pdf) describes fresh or dried leaves/stalks as mint-like culinary flavouring. This supports culinary use but is not a Victorian toxicology study or dose.
- DJAARA's [DJAKITJ enterprise](https://djakitj.com.au/) records leaves traditionally crushed and inhaled for coughs and colds and separately presents river mint as a contemporary culinary herb on Djandak. [GLaWAC's Whole-of-Country Plan](https://gunaikurnai.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Gunaikurnai-Whole-of-Country-Plan-ONLINE.pdf) separately records use by Gunaikurnai ancestors for chest and breathing problems. [Wandoon Estate Aboriginal Corporation's Plants of Coranderrk](https://nespurban.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Coranderrk-Plant-Brochure-pdf-002.pdf) records **Panaryle** and food/medicine icons in a Coranderrk/Woiwurrung context. None supplies a clinical treatment or safe wild dose.
- [Museums Victoria/Bunjilaka](https://museumsvictoria.com.au/bunjilaka/about-us/the-plants-of-milarri-garden/) attributes **Poang-Gurk** and use of leaves to flavour earth-oven food specifically to its Djab Wurrung entry. Keep the people, spelling, context and source attached. This is a museum account, not direct present-day Djab Wurrung permission.
- No species-specific fresh-leaf energy or useful serving result was located. At herb-scale use it should be treated as flavouring, not survival calories.

### Exact 2026 volatile evidence

T.C. Wilson, P.I. Forster, D.J. Duval and J.J. Brophy, “A Comparison of Volatile Components Across Native Australian *Mentha* (Lamiaceae),” *Plants* 2026, 15(5), 778. DOI: [10.3390/plants15050778](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants15050778). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41829810/); [open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12986924/).

The study:

- extracted volatiles from three or four leaves, about 0.05–0.1 g fresh or 0.02–0.05 g dry, using pentane and GC/MS;
- reports oil yields consistently below 0.2% w/w for freshly dried and herbarium specimens;
- reports aggregate *M. australis* relative volatile values of about 25% menthone, 9% isomenthone and 24% pulegone;
- reports fresh commercial-provenance sample JB6174 at approximately 32% menthone, 11% isomenthone and 31% pulegone; and
- reports JB6180, cultivated from a Hall Reserve, Clifton Hill, Victoria source, at about 20% menthone and 70% pulegone.

The authors state that their purpose was relative rather than absolute quantification. The percentages are integrated peak shares of the recovered volatile profile. They are **not percentages of fresh leaf mass**, and they do not provide milligrams of pulegone per gram of leaf or per cup of tea.

The [European Medicines Agency pulegone/menthofuran statement](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/scientific-guideline/public-statement-use-herbal-medicinal-products-containing-pulegone-and-menthofuran-revision-1_en.pdf) supplies provisional exposure limits for herbal medicinal products and advises exposure be kept as low as practicable. Those limits cannot be converted to a river-mint leaf amount without absolute concentration and preparation-transfer data.

### Lookalike and toxicity boundary

- Naturalised pennyroyal, *Mentha pulegium*, overlaps wet creek and river habitat. [PlantNET pennyroyal account](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Mentha~pulegium&page=nswfl).
- Pennyroyal usually has smaller, rounder or blunt leaves, a 10-veined calyx with unequal lobes and pale mauve/lilac flowers. River mint tends toward narrower pointed toothed leaves, a 13-veined calyx and white/pink flowers. These are expert-source distinctions, not proof that a novice can apply them reliably in every life stage.
- Concentrated pennyroyal oil is a recognised cause of serious liver injury and death. [NIH LiverTox](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK548673/). This does not prove that culinary river-mint leaf has the same risk; it supports strict separation and a prohibition on oils/concentrates.

### Evidence versus precaution

- **Evidence:** *M. australis* has a culinary history; sampled leaf volatile profiles contain pulegone; relative composition varies; one Victorian-source cultivated specimen reached 70% pulegone within its volatile profile.
- **Unknown:** absolute pulegone plus menthofuran per gram of fresh/dry leaf, transfer into an ordinary infusion or cooked food, safe human portion, effects in pregnancy or liver disease, and species-specific allergy incidence.
- **Editorial precaution:** no eating, tea, essential oil, concentrated infusion or extract instruction in this edition. This is a response to dose uncertainty, not a finding that every traditional culinary use is poisonous.

### Current gate

Education-only. Any future action requires a Victorian botanist-approved plate, a toxicologist/food-safety assessment using absolute chemistry from realistic preparations and a defined serving, explicit pregnancy/health review, decisions from the relevant named Traditional Owner organisations about cultural scope and reuse, and a novice no-consumption identification test.

## SP-020 — Sweet quandong

### Taxonomy, range and season — evidence

- Accepted taxon: *Santalum acuminatum* (R.Br.) A.DC. [VicFlora](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/83d92c86-0107-4c54-9eea-2a24c3fc810d).
- VicFlora describes it as generally uncommon but widespread in northern and north-western Victorian woodland. It flowers mainly October–May.
- [Australian National Botanic Gardens](https://www.anbg.gov.au/gnp/interns-2002/santalum-acuminatum.html) describes ripening the following spring in cultivation. A [South Australian industry source](https://sanativefoods.org.au/quandong/) gives approximately August–December for commercial South Australian harvest. Neither establishes one guaranteed Victorian wild-fruiting period.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Shrub or small tree to about 6 m, often producing suckers, with hanging angular branchlets.
- Opposite, narrow lance-shaped and often curved yellow-green leaves, approximately 3–9 cm long and 3–15 mm wide.
- Small four-tepalled green to orange flowers in terminal pyramidal panicles.
- Mature drupe bright shiny red, rarely yellow, about 15–25 mm, with a persistent perianth and deeply pitted stone.
- Do not use taste as confirmation, and do not treat any generic red fruit as quandong.

### Flesh food, preparation, taste and nutrition — evidence

- Food-history sources record fully ripe flesh eaten raw, dried, pounded and reconstituted. The flavour is commonly described as tart or tangy, with variable sweetness. Taste is expected-result information after identity, not an identification test.
- The ANBG source describes only about 3–5 mm of flesh around the stone. **Inference:** collecting 100 g edible flesh may require many fruits and may be a poor use of time when rescue, warmth, shelter and water are more urgent. The guide must not promise yield.
- Current Australian Food Composition Database Release 3 food F007571, “Quandong, fruit, flesh,” reports per 100 g edible flesh: energy with fibre 213 kJ (about 51 kcal), energy without fibre 179 kJ, moisture 71.9 g, protein 2.5 g, fat 0 g, total sugars 8.1 g, dietary fibre 4.2 g and sodium 116 mg. [FSANZ food group listing](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/science-data/food-nutrient-databases/afcd/search/food-groups/9/40); [FSANZ food record](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/science-data/food-nutrient-databases/afcd/search/food/F007571).
- FSANZ describes commercially supplied frozen flesh with kernels removed. It is not a whole-fruit value, a Victorian wild-population average or evidence of a safe amount for every person.

### Kernel evidence and decision

The [RIRDC bush-food safety review](https://www.ausbushfoods.com/bushfoodsonline/reports/Safety/bush_food_safety-01-28.pdf) reports kernel values of roughly 56–67% oil and 13–19% protein, but also:

- santalbic acid at approximately 32–46% of kernel fatty acids;
- altered liver metabolic/biochemical measures in rats when kernel supplied about 50% of dietary energy, an extreme animal diet that does not establish a human threshold;
- methyl benzoate varying approximately 32–1294 micrograms per gram fresh kernel and associated with an unpleasant aftertaste, with roasting not removing it in the reviewed work; and
- no detected hydrogen cyanide in the reviewed sample.

**Evidence:** the kernel is nutrient-dense and has a food history, but it also has unusual chemistry and incomplete safety evidence.  
**Unknown:** a human field serving, whether ordinary traditional preparation controls the identified concerns, species/population variability and allergy incidence.  
**Editorial precaution:** withhold the kernel entirely. Do not imply that the extreme rat diet proves one kernel poisons a human, and do not imply that roasting detoxifies it.

### Lookalike and conservation boundary

- *Santalum lanceolatum* is Critically Endangered under the FFG listing recorded by [VicFlora](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/3e8ef0a8-298f-4447-8f75-c2115bbe237d) and occurs in only a few small Victorian populations.
- Its fruit is smaller, approximately 7–10 mm, blue to purplish and has a smoother stone/apical scar; *S. acuminatum* fruit is usually shiny red, 15–25 mm and deeply pitted. A complete plate must verify these and other local comparisons. Any uncertain, immature or non-red *Santalum* is a no-pick stop.

### Cultural boundary

[Peer-reviewed Murray–Darling archaeology and ethnobotany](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6774476/) documents raw/dried fruit use and includes specifically named Barkandji and Wiradjuri evidence. DOI: [10.1371/journal.pone.0222680](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222680). Preserve those named peoples and the study's context. Do not turn it into generic Victorian authority. No Wotjobaluk or other Victorian group name/use should be added without confirmation and permission from the relevant Traditional Owner organisation.

### White-cedar host-transfer paper — exact evidence and limit

E.R. Loveys, S.D. Tyerman and B.R. Loveys, “Transfer of photosynthate and naturally occurring insecticidal compounds from host plants to the root hemiparasite *Santalum acuminatum* (Santalaceae),” *Australian Journal of Botany* 2001, 49(1), 9–16. DOI: [10.1071/BT99080](https://doi.org/10.1071/BT99080). [University of Adelaide record and abstract](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/fb792c3f-1572-4d56-aac6-b17a17e09988).

The paper reports that:

- radiolabelled glucose moved from host *Myoporum parvifolium* into quandong;
- quandong-fixed carbon occurred mainly as mannitol, which was detected in fruit, leaf, stem, root and xylem sap;
- direct-infusion electrospray mass spectrometry supplied evidence of an insecticidal compound in fruit from quandongs growing near *Melia azedarach*; and
- apple-moth larvae fed only that fruit suffered higher mortality than larvae fed fruit from quandongs growing away from *Melia*.

It did **not** test human or mammalian consumption, establish a human-toxic fruit concentration, define a safe or unsafe distance from white cedar, prove every nearby tree was haustorially connected, or show that edible flesh must be rejected.

**Decision:** the paper does not support a categorical reader-facing claim that quandong fruit near white cedar poisons people. Keep the finding as an unresolved safety-review note. Do not reproduce unsupported online wording about “neurotoxins harmful to mammals” transferring into fruit. A proximity stop would be an editorial precaution, not evidence; it should not enter the action box without human toxicology and a practicable connection/proximity test.

### Current gate

The only possible future action is fully ripe flesh from an expertly confirmed *S. acuminatum* at a lawful, uncontaminated site. Required first: botanist authentication of the complete mature-fruit/leaf/habit/stone/lookalike plate; food-safety/toxicology sign-off on flesh and kernel exclusion; nutrition wording tied to FSANZ's commercial frozen kernel-removed sample; relevant Traditional Owner scope; and a novice false-positive test without consumption. Kernel remains withheld.

## SP-021 — Old-man saltbush

### Taxonomy, range, season and conservation — evidence

- Accepted species: *Atriplex nummularia* Lindl. [VicFlora species account](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/1c3973ea-5147-4b9a-9009-f089bd793e6f).
- Victoria contains subsp. *nummularia* and subsp. *omissa* Aellen. VicFlora explicitly says the two Victorian subspecies are not always clearly distinguishable.
- [Subsp. *nummularia*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/bf801dbc-11b6-4b2f-9dd8-1a9ff3c3fe3d) is associated particularly with Murray floodplain heavy, somewhat saline soils downstream of Kerang and fruits broadly October–April.
- [Subsp. *omissa*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/d4fb3488-1942-403c-8d8c-9b257cb4cafe) is FFG Endangered. Its [Victorian assessment](https://bio-prd-naturekit-public-data.s3.ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/species_assessments/Atriplex_nummularia_subsp_omissa_504233.pdf) places it in extreme north-western Victoria, including the Neds Corner/Walpolla/Lindsay area, and records fruit mainly October–November.

### Diagnostic evidence

- Erect shrub to about 3 m and mostly dioecious.
- Broadly oval, elliptic or rhombic grey-green leaves, commonly 15–40 mm long and wide; margins may be entire, wavy or coarsely toothed.
- Male flowers form globose clusters along spikes; female flowers occur in leafless panicles or upper axils.
- Fruiting bracteoles are roughly 6–10 mm, mealy grey, thick and corky for most of their area.
- These species features do not solve the subspecies problem for a novice. Grey colour, salty taste and a common name are not diagnostic.

### Culinary and nutrition evidence

- [SA Native Foods](https://sanativefoods.org.au/old-man-saltbush/) describes commercially grown leaves/flower sprays as salty and used blanched, sautéed or as seasoning. This is culinary context, not proof that an unknown Victorian wild plant has a safe field serving.
- A small Australian dried-leaf study reported approximately 20.1 g protein, 41.5 g fibre, 1.44 g calcium, 4.13 g sodium, 0.90 g magnesium and 11.68 mg iron per 100 g dry matter. [Proceedings paper](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/proceedings/proceedings-36-00083/article_deploy/proceedings-36-00083.pdf). It is dry material, not fresh edible yield or usable energy from Victorian wild leaves.
- An older study reported about 18.7 g dry matter and 4.70 g protein per 100 g fresh leaf. [PubMed PMID 3939488](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3939488/). It was not a Victorian field-serving or safety study.
- No defensible human-metabolisable energy result for a fresh Victorian wild serving was located. Livestock gross or metabolisable energy must not be substituted.

### Chemistry, contamination and preparation limits

- Field shoots have shown approximately 2.4–6.4% oxalate by dry mass depending on site and nitrogen treatment. DOI: [10.1071/FP13060](https://doi.org/10.1071/FP13060). This is plant/livestock-oriented research, not a human safe dose.
- [NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/agriculture/pastures-and-rangelands/species-varieties/pf/factsheets/old-man-saltbush) warns that old-man saltbush accumulates salt, oxalate and nitrate and discusses livestock risk from large amounts of young growth. Livestock access and water rules cannot be converted into a human field portion.
- *A. nummularia* has been studied for contaminant phytoextraction; leaves accumulated arsenic under experimental contaminated-soil conditions. [PubMed PMID 27000320](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27000320/). This does not mean every plant contains dangerous arsenic. It makes provenance a necessary rejection check.
- General evidence that boiling lowers soluble oxalate in some vegetables does not prove a particular time makes wild old-man saltbush safe, and it cannot remove nitrate, sodium or environmental contaminants as a group. No detoxifying boil is authorised.
- No species-specific human allergy incidence, fresh wild safe amount or preparation validation was located.

### Cultural boundary

A Yarkuwa Wamba Wamba language resource records **tyulern** for saltbush: [resource](https://www.yarkuwa.org.au/wurrekangurr/resources/26/document). It does not establish that the word refers specifically to *A. nummularia*, supply a preparation method or grant permission to generalise it across Victoria. Do not put the word in the species heading without Wamba Wamba confirmation and agreed scope.

### Evidence versus precaution

- **Evidence:** the plant has a commercial culinary role; its leaf composition can be nutritionally interesting; salt, oxalate and nitrate can accumulate; Victorian subspecies are hard to separate; subsp. *omissa* is Endangered.
- **Unknown:** fresh wild human energy, a safe emergency serving, the chemistry of an encountered plant, a validated preparation and whether a novice can exclude subsp. *omissa* and other *Atriplex* reliably.
- **Editorial precaution:** withhold wild-eating instructions and present cultivated culinary use only. This is not a claim that lawful, correctly prepared commercial old-man saltbush is poisonous.

### Current gate

Wild eating remains withheld unless a botanist resolves the final field characters and subspecies/conservation problem, a human food-safety/toxicology specialist validates defined fresh material and preparation, a nutrition reviewer supplies wet-basis edible-part results, a legal reviewer confirms the site rule, and relevant Traditional Owners decide whether any cultural content is appropriate. An expert-reviewed cultivated-food note may remain separate from survival foraging.

## Cross-species contradictions and unresolved questions

| Issue | What the evidence says | Editorial consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional food versus modern dose evidence | Documented use is real evidence of knowledge and practice, but the located sources rarely define modern specimen verification, grams, vulnerable groups or contaminant conditions | Attribute respectfully and seek collaboration; do not invent a universal field dose |
| River-mint culinary use versus pulegone | Both are supported: culinary history exists and sampled volatile profiles contain sometimes-high relative pulegone | Do not erase the food history or claim poisoning; keep the entry non-actionable until absolute exposure is known |
| Cranberry-heath “ripe berry” expectations | VicFlora says ripe fruit may remain green | Require floral/whole-plant identification; colour is not a maturity shortcut |
| Quandong flesh versus kernel | Flesh has an official current composition entry; kernel has unusual chemistry and inadequate human dose evidence | Permit only a conditional flesh pathway and withhold kernel |
| White-cedar insect evidence versus human poisoning claims | Transfer/insect mortality was shown; human toxicity, dose, connection and distance were not | Research note only; no categorical proximity poisoning rule |
| Commercial saltbush versus wild saltbush | Commercial culinary use exists; wild chemistry, exact subspecies and site history can differ | Cultivated context only; no wild eating or generic boiling instruction |
| Energy figures | Quandong has a current wet edible-flesh value; the other three lack a suitable fresh Victorian edible-part value | Print the FSANZ sampling limit with the number; state “not established” for the others |

## Release gate for any future actionable plant page

1. Current VicFlora taxon and Victorian distribution rechecked at edition date.
2. Complete final image set authenticated by a Victorian botanist: whole plant, leaves both sides, stem/bark, flower, immature and mature fruit, cut fruit/stone where relevant, scale and matched local lookalikes.
3. Edible part, maturity, preparation, serving and vulnerable-person exclusions independently reviewed by a toxicologist or food-safety specialist. Unknown serving remains unknown.
4. Every energy value tied to exact taxon, part, wet/dry state, preparation, provenance, sample and database release.
5. Legal status, tenure, landholder permission and contamination history checked; threatened/lookalike stop rules visible before any action.
