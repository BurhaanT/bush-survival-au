# RB-005 — Emergency information design and usability review

**Required reviewer:** information designer or human-factors/usability practitioner experienced in emergency, health or high-stress instructions. Clinical and operational reviewers remain responsible for factual approval.  
**Review object:** the same dated prototype and all quick-reference panels after subject-matter amendments, including the visibly unapproved IM-194 adult/child choking card, IM-195 infant/collapse card, IM-196 carried-sheet shelter card, IM-197–IM-198 drowning cards and IM-205 serious-deterioration card.

## Questions to answer

1. Can a cold, wet, gloved, stressed novice enter at the right page within seconds?
2. Is there one obvious path for danger, response, breathing, catastrophic bleeding and getting help?
3. Are **With a first-aid kit** and **Without a first-aid kit** equally easy to find, without implying that improvised equipment works equally well? Does the reader know to use the second section if their kit is missing an item?
4. Can a reader distinguish an arterial tourniquet from pressure immobilisation and select the correct bite/sting branch?
5. Can the reader see how to position the person and when not to give food or drink before taking the affected action?
6. Do cross-references have unique names and physical page destinations rather than vague “see above” language?
7. Does any warning rely on colour alone, tiny type, a footnote or a page turn?
8. Can notes about the person be filled in when time allows without interrupting care, and can the reader write “unknown”?
9. Do diagrams show the same sequence, equipment, body position and stop conditions as the words?
10. Does the page remain interpretable in grayscale, low light, rain protection and at final physical size?
11. Can someone who has not seen the draft explain its terms, first action, next action, reasons to stop and missing-equipment option in their own words, without hints?
12. Have existing captions, labels, tables and instructions passed the same plain-language check as new text?
13. Can a stressed reader route correctly among an effective cough, an ineffective cough in a responsive adult or child aged one year or older, a responsive infant under one year, and the all-ages unresponsive/not-breathing-normally CPR branch? Test ages immediately below and above the one-year boundary so the two physical techniques cannot be visually merged.
14. On IM-194 and IM-195, can readers identify the back-blow and chest-thrust anatomical targets without choosing the abdomen, striking an upright person, inverting a child, changing the stated sequence, automatically completing five actions after the object clears or performing a blind finger sweep? Any anatomy or posture ambiguity is referred back to the clinical/instructor reviewer rather than “fixed” by design alone.
15. Do the tall choking cards at 1000 × 2380 and 1000 × 2750, the carried-sheet card at 1000 × 4200, the secure-land water-rescue card at 1000 × 3150 and the out-of-water drowning card at 1000 × 3820 survive final-size print, PDF pagination, responsive-browser reflow, grayscale and crop/bleed checks without reducing actionable type, clipping labels or separating an action from its warning, stop, failure, craft, infant, CPR or aftercare condition? If not, split or reflow and repeat subject-matter plus usability review; shrinking is not acceptance.
16. On IM-196, can readers correctly reject the route when equipment, practice, physical capacity, site or weather gates fail, without inventing a knot, anchor, dimension or no-equipment method from the schematic?
17. On IM-197–IM-198, can readers distinguish secure-land non-contact help from craft/contact rescue; move from water to the correct out-of-water branch; recognise that infant side-position mechanics are absent; and reach CPR, obstruction, kit/no-kit and mandatory ambulance-aftercare actions without delay?
18. Can a reader enter IM-205 quickly, treat the signs as examples rather than a checklist, call 000 without waiting for diagnosis or a device number, and reach the CPR/AED, severe-bleeding, age-one-plus recovery-position or known-cause card without delay? Test that the infant limit, condition-specific position override, hot/cold rule, no-generic-intake boundary and solo dual-threat uncertainty survive complete-card and detached-panel use.

## Required test set

- participants with current first-aid training and participants without specialist training;
- solo and group role-play using mannequins/trainer devices only;
- adult, child and infant choking-manikin tasks covering age routing, anatomy target, check-after-each, stop-on-clearance and the transition to the single CPR flow;
- inert exact-equipment IM-196 tasks covering the practised setup, missing/damaged equipment, failed physical-capacity gate, injured companion, worsening weather, runoff/pooling, blocked exit and attachment failure; no dangerous weather, climbing or reliance on the shelter for protection;
- secure-land IM-197 talk/reach/throw scenarios in which the bank, aid or rescuer becomes unstable, plus a craft prompt that must not lead to invented craft handling, water entry or contact rescue;
- IM-198 wet adult/child/infant manikin and representative AED-trainer scenarios covering normal versus abnormal breathing, the absent infant side-position mechanics, rescue-breath CPR, the shortest possible obvious-obstruction interruption, kit/no-kit equipment delay and mandatory ambulance aftercare;
- timed retrieval, comprehension and action-selection tasks;
- immediate and delayed-recall tests that do not let participants reread the card between learning and the delayed task;
- final-size colour and grayscale printouts, narrow responsive-browser views and deliberate edge-crop/page-break variants for IM-194–IM-198; reject any rendering that hides a label, warning, practice/capacity gate, land/craft boundary, infant limit, stop condition, CPR gate or aftercare rule;
- harmless test prompts about no signal, only a personal locator beacon (PLB), an unavailable phone, darkness, rain gloves, noise and a person getting worse; represent heat and illness in the scenario, not by exposing participants to dangerous conditions;
- cross-condition traps from the audit, especially anaphylaxis/asthma, stroke/hypoglycaemia, spinal/airway and bluebottle/uncertain jellyfish;
- IM-205 complete-card and detached-panel cases covering concern before any severe sign, person/parent/carer/first-aider concern, incomplete child/infant examples, a normal device reading, absent rash, abnormal breathing, life-threatening bleeding, both threats with one rescuer, unresponsive normal breathing below/above age one, anaphylaxis, vomiting, spinal concern, heat, cold, kit/no-kit and failed contact;
- failure coding that distinguishes content, layout, navigation, terminology and training limitations;
- revision and retest after every consequential layout change.

## Acceptance standard

No numeric pass rate has yet been approved. The subject-matter reviewers and human-factors reviewer must set conservative criteria before testing. Any error that could plausibly delay 000/CPR, create a serious-illness false all-clear, transfer recovery-position art to an infant, let a generic position override anaphylaxis or another condition, select the wrong choking age/anatomy branch, continue blows or thrusts after clearance or loss of response, select the wrong envenomation treatment, allow unsafe oral intake, cause unnecessary movement, interrupt lifesaving care, start an unsafe shelter build, encourage untrained water entry/contact rescue, delay drowning CPR or imply lay field clearance is a critical defect requiring revision and retest.

The final output must identify exact page/component versions, tasks, participant profile, errors, near misses, completion times, amendments and residual risks. Satisfaction ratings alone are not evidence of safe use.

Use [the plain-language rules](../../EDITORIAL_STYLE_GUIDE.md). A readability score is a screening aid, not a pass. Record what readers actually understand and do with harmless props. No reader tests have yet been completed.
