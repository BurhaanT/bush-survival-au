# Book style and safety language

Read [Writing the guide in plain language](../EDITORIAL_STYLE_GUIDE.md) first. It applies to existing and future text, including captions, picture labels and review drafts. This file gives the book's page patterns; it does not replace the book-wide writing rules.

## Voice

- Calm, direct and non-dramatic.
- Written for a capable reader without specialist training.
- Explain priorities and reasons without burying the immediate action.
- Use Victorian/Australian terminology and metric units.

## Instruction pattern

Every urgent section starts with the smallest safe action layer:

1. **Do this now** — three to six short steps where the subject allows it.
2. **Choose your situation** — short `if … then …` branches.
3. **Do not** — the few mistakes that would cause serious harm.
4. **Check** — what to watch and when to stop or get more help.

Put explanation, uncommon cases, sources and review detail after that action layer. The action layer must make sense on its own.

Where more technique detail is needed, use:

1. **Use this when**
2. **Do not use this when**
3. **What you need**
4. **Steps**
5. **Check that it worked**
6. **Common failures**
7. **Alternatives**
8. **Regional/seasonal limits**
9. **Evidence and review date**

Do not repeat a method already given elsewhere. Link to its single main set of steps. Move internal content IDs, source reconciliation and ordinary image-licence prose to the project records unless the reader needs them to make the immediate decision.

Mention an activity only when it changes the safest action. Do not create separate “for hikers,” “for campers” or “for 4WD” versions of the same survival priority.

## Medical entry pattern

Every practical first-aid entry uses this order:

1. **Recognise it**
2. **When to call for help or move to medical care**
3. **Do this immediately**
4. **With a first-aid kit** — name the items needed.
5. **Without a first-aid kit** — also covers a kit missing those items.
6. **Do not do this**
7. **Keep checking for**
8. **While waiting or moving to care**
9. **Evidence, clinical reviewer and review date**

If there is no safe replacement for a missing item, say so. Do not invent a substitute just to fill both sections.

## Warning language

- Put a warning beside the step it qualifies.
- State the action to avoid and the consequence in plain language.
- Do not use vague labels such as “be careful” without saying what to check.
- Do not hide a stop condition in a footnote.
- When evidence cannot support a safe novice decision, say “do not attempt/use/consume.”

## Human life and ordinary rules

- In a true immediate threat of death or really serious injury, human life comes before ordinary activity rules.
- Require the only reasonable response, the least harmful workable option, no more action than needed, emergency contact where possible and a clear stop when the danger is controlled.
- State that this is not blanket permission and that exact legality remains fact-dependent.
- Do not let this boundary invent a missing method, make unknown food or contaminated water safe, or weaken a safety prohibition whose purpose is preventing another lethal hazard.
- Repeat the short boundary on any detached card that could otherwise make a routine legal or permission gate look absolute.

## Evidence language

- Distinguish “is,” “may,” “often,” “reported,” “historically documented” and “unknown.”
- Never turn absence of evidence into safety.
- Never imply that traditional documentation is clinical proof.
- Do not describe a species as edible without the usable part, stage, preparation, lookalikes and limits.
- Date legal, medical, fire, communications and access information that may change.

## Citations

- Use stable source IDs linked to the source register in working drafts.
- Add a short source note where it helps the reader see who issued the advice and when it was checked.
- Retain complete URLs, titles, publishers/authors and access dates in the register.
- Do not cite search results or AI output as evidence.

## Visual language

- Label which way the picture is viewed, sizes, step order and features needed for identification.
- Use high contrast that remains legible in print and low light.
- Never rely on colour alone to convey a warning, even when colour is also needed for species identification.
- Captions must state what the reader should notice, not merely name the subject.
