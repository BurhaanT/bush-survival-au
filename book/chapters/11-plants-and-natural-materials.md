## Do this now

1. Deal with [urgent care](06-first-aid-and-evacuation.md), [getting help](03-emergency-communication.md), [cold, heat and rain](05-temperature-and-shelter.md), and [water](07-water.md) before food.
2. Use carried food first. It is safer, easier to ration and usually gives far more useful energy than leaves or a few berries.
3. **Do not identify or trial a plant for the first time while stranded.** A similar photograph, smell, animal feeding, skin rub, lip test, tiny taste or cooking is not a safety test.
4. Use a wild plant only if you learned that exact species before the emergency and every feature, usable part, preparation step, place and stop rule still agrees.
5. Do not eat plants beside roads or sprayed tracks, drains, mine or industrial land, flood contamination, treated grounds or anywhere chemical history is unknown. Rinsing does not remove that uncertainty.
6. After possible poisoning, call **13 11 26**. Call **000** first for collapse, abnormal breathing or serious symptoms. Do not induce vomiting.

> **ORDINARY COLLECTION RULE — PICKING MAY BE ILLEGAL**
>
> Victorian parks, State forests and protected flora have collection controls. For planned learning and ordinary food gathering, use cultivated plants or a place where both owner and land manager permit collection. Do not take from a threatened population or culturally sensitive place.
>
> If someone instead faces an immediate risk of death or really serious injury, use the book-wide [life-first emergency test](01-how-to-use-this-guide.md#when-a-rule-conflicts-with-saving-a-life). This is not blanket permission, and it does not identify an unknown plant, make a contaminated plant safe, supply missing preparation steps or justify a slow food search when rescue, warmth, shelter or water can address the danger sooner.
>
> **Evidence confidence: High** for the linked ordinary Victorian rules and the statutory emergency test; **Moderate** for this condensed field distinction. **Wording confidence: 94% (subjective).**

## Only if you learned the plant before the emergency

Inspect **one connected plant**. Stop at one contradiction.

1. Follow the proposed food to its own stem; nearby plants may be tangled through it.
2. Match the whole plant, size, growth form, stem or bark.
3. Match a complete leaf: arrangement, edge, top and underside.
4. Match flowers and fruit when the entry requires them. Measure features; an unscaled photograph cannot supply size.
5. Match the habitat, part of Victoria and season.
6. Check every lookalike and stop condition in the entry.

Expected taste is only a final spoilage warning **after positive identification**. It never identifies a plant. The full six-step gate is an original organising aid based on the cited botanical and [Poisons first-aid](https://www.poisonsinfo.nsw.gov.au/first-aid) sources; it is not a botanical key.

> **WARNING — NON-CRITICAL LOADS ONLY**
>
> **Evidence confidence:** High for the knot and braid forms; Moderate for any improvised material. **Wording confidence: 84% (subjective).** Never use these for a person's weight, climbing, rescue, towing, a main shelter support or anything overhead.

### Three-strand braid

1. Align three flexible strands and tie their starting ends together.
2. Hold the knot. Separate the strands into left, middle and right.
3. Cross **left over middle**, then **right over the new middle**.
4. Repeat, keeping even tension. Tie the finish before letting go.
5. Test only on a small bundle close to the ground. Reject splitting, slipping or opening; recheck after wetting or drying.

![Three-strand braid diagram: A crosses over B, then C crosses over A; the new order is B C A](../assets/braid-sequence.svg)

### Join two cords with a sheet bend

1. Fold the thicker cord end into a U.
2. Pass the thinner cord up through the U, behind both arms, then under its own crossed section.
3. Keep both short ends on the same side and leave visible tails.
4. Pull the long parts to snug the knot. Reject it if a tail creeps or the shape changes; recheck after load or wetting.

If you cannot recognise the finished knot, use a familiar fastening instead. Detailed evidence and limitations appear after the plant atlas.

## Reference — read food-value numbers honestly

Australian labels usually use kilojoules (kJ). A food Calorie is a kilocalorie (kcal). For a rough conversion:

**kcal = kJ ÷ 4.2**

If a value is given per 100 g and the edible portion was actually weighed:

**portion kJ = kJ per 100 g × edible grams ÷ 100**

Do not turn “one handful”, “one cup” or a photograph into grams. Hand size, packing, water content, fruit size and discarded parts make that arithmetic unreliable. Fresh and dried values are not interchangeable. Boiling and draining can change both weight and nutrients.

## Native raspberry — Rubus parvifolius

**Status — CONDITIONAL FOOD:** Use only after the species, ripe fruit, lawful site and contamination status have already been confirmed. This page is not a first-time emergency identification key.

> **WARNING — CONFIRMED PLANT ONLY**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High that multiple sources document people eating the ripe fruit of this species; Moderate for this personal-use preparation summary; Insufficient for identifying your specimen.** The photographed specimens and this final page have not been independently authenticated. Do not eat fruit from an uncertain plant.
>
> **Wording confidence: 82% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

Also called small-leaf bramble. This is a low, scrambling, prickly plant, not a tree bearing smooth red berries.

### Features to check together

- Slender trailing or scrambling stems, often around a metre long, with curved prickles.
- A leaf divided into **three or five leaflets**. Individual leaflets are usually about **1–4 cm long**.
- Leaflet tops green and wrinkled, edges distinctly toothed; undersides whitish and hairy.
- Small pink or red flowers.
- A rounded fruit about **1 cm across**, made of separate fleshy beads. Mature fruit can be red or orange and comes away from its central support when ripe.

**Habitat and Victoria:** widespread through cooler parts of the state, from near sea level to the subalps, in forest, woodland and scrub, sometimes amongst rocks. It is not a promised food supply in every region.

**Season:** VicFlora gives flowering mainly November–February; Yarra Ranges gives October–December and fruit December–April. These are regional guides, not conflicting dates to average into a guarantee. Look for the actual fruit stage.

**Botanical checks:** [VicFlora](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/bc19413d-6b07-4c94-84ee-4b5fef7e2f98), [NSW PlantNET](https://plantnet.rbgsyd.nsw.gov.au/cgi-bin/NSWfl.pl?lvl=sp&name=Rubus~parvifolius&page=nswfl) and [Yarra Ranges](https://www.yarraranges.vic.gov.au/PlantDirectory/Climbers-Creepers/Rubus-parvifolius). Neither flora alone establishes food safety.

### Fruit use and preparation

For correctly identified native raspberry from a permitted, uncontaminated source:

1. Select ripe fruit still associated with the identified plant. Leave mouldy, damaged or contaminated fruit.
2. Pick without dragging your hand along the prickly stem. Keep the fruit separate from unknown plants.
3. Remove attached stalk or leafy pieces and rinse with drinking-quality water.
4. Eat the fruit fresh. This entry does not recommend the leaves, roots, an herbal tea or a medicinal preparation.

**Expected taste:** an Australian National Botanic Gardens plant profile describes the separate fruit beads as juicy. No controlled sweetness or acidity data were located. Ripeness and site can change the result. An unexpectedly unpleasant taste is a reason to spit it out and stop, not proof of a different safe species.

**Food value:** no measured species-specific energy value was located. An older botanical table reports an average fruit mass of about **0.285 g**, and an ANBG account says fruit can be sparse. That small study value does not predict your plant or convert into calories. Treat the fruit as a supplemental snack, not a ration. Do not substitute the nutrition value of cultivated European raspberry. No serving size or allergy-free claim has been established.

**Food-use checks:** [Yarra Ranges](https://www.yarraranges.vic.gov.au/PlantDirectory/Climbers-Creepers/Rubus-parvifolius), [The Hills Shire Council](https://www.thehills.nsw.gov.au/News-and-Publications/Plant-of-the-Month-December), [ANBG Aboriginal Plant Use](https://www.anbg.gov.au/aborig.s.e.aust/rubus-parvifolius.html), [ANBG plant profile](https://www.anbg.gov.au/apu/plants/rubuparv.html) and the [Cunninghamia seed-biology table](https://www.botanicgardens.org.au/sites/default/files/2023-06/Volume-6%284%29-2000-Cun6Ben1016-1202.pdf). The ANBG words are favourable descriptions, not measured taste results. Cleaning and contamination precautions are general food-handling measures, not a detoxification treatment.

### Lookalikes and stop conditions

Other brambles, introduced blackberries and hybrids can confuse identification. VicFlora and PlantNET both record hybridisation with another native Rubus. Do not convert this entry into “every red berry is edible” or “anything prickly is safe”.

If the fruit is a single smooth berry, the leaves do not match, you cannot see their pale hairy underside, or the fruiting stem cannot be traced confidently, stop. The three photographs are not a complete comparison with poisonous red-fruited plants. A flower view, measured leaf-underside view and expert-checked lookalike panel are still needed.

**Coranderrk cultural record:** Wandoon Estate Aboriginal Corporation's *Plants of Coranderrk* project records **Eepaeep** for *Rubus parvifolius* and marks it as a food plant. The brochure says its Woiwurrung names were checked with a current custodian and language expert. This exact Coranderrk record is not presented as a statewide name, and its food icon is not used to expand the preparation above. [Source brochure](https://nespurban.edu.au/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Coranderrk-Plant-Brochure-pdf-002.pdf). **Entry:** SP-003; botanical, food-safety and cultural-scope review outstanding.

## Native pigface — Carpobrotus rossii

**Status — CONDITIONAL FOOD:** Use only after the exact species, ripe fruit, lawful site and contamination status have already been confirmed. The current photographs do not show ripe fruit.

> **WARNING — SPECIES AND RIPENESS MUST ALREADY BE KNOWN**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High that multiple sources document people eating the specified ripe inner fruit; Moderate for this preparation summary; Insufficient for novice identification from the two incomplete images.** Neither picture shows demonstrably ripe fruit or a complete lookalike comparison. Use this as a reminder after learning the plant, not as permission to identify a new food during an emergency.
>
> **Wording confidence: 81% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

The name “pigface” is shared by several plants; the common name alone is not an identification. This edition does not assign a Traditional Owner name because the available sources do not establish one authorised Victorian group-by-group attribution.

### Features and location

This spreading succulent forms low growth on coastal dunes and cliffs. Its thick, fleshy leaves are triangular in cross-section, commonly **3.5–10 cm long**, and tend to curve inward near the tip. Flowers have many narrow pink-purple parts, often paler near the centre. The fleshy fruit is roughly rounded to oval, around **1.5–2.5 cm long** in VicFlora's description.

VicFlora places it commonly along the Victorian coast and notes doubtful origin for some inland records. Do not apply this entry to every inland succulent. Flowering can occur through much of the year; fruit availability must be checked on the plant.

**Identity/distribution:** [VicFlora](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/c01a1b80-5d85-460c-ab13-094247ad1ca4). Its indexed full description was available during this check although direct page retrieval failed. The South Australian identification resource and Victorian nursery references provide separate context.

### Ripe fruit: usable part and preparation

For confirmed **Carpobrotus rossii**, gathered lawfully from an uncontaminated site:

1. Select intact ripe fruit; the sources describe a red to pinkish-red stage. Colour alone does not establish either species or ripeness.
2. Rinse off sand and dirt with drinking-quality water.
3. Open the tough outer skin with clean fingers and squeeze out the soft inner flesh. Eat the inner fruit flesh, leaving the skin.
4. Discard spoiled or contaminated fruit. Do not transfer this procedure to an unknown succulent.

**Expected taste:** one horticultural source describes ripe *C. rossii* fruit as salty-sweet, but that description was not independently corroborated. Treat it only as a variable expectation after identification, never as an identity test. This entry does not recommend eating the leaves.

**Food value:** a study of fruit collected near Warrnambool found roughly 84–85% water in the fresh fruit but did not publish food energy. Its protein, carbohydrate, fat and fibre results were reported on a dry basis and must not be copied as fresh-food percentages. Treat the small inner portion as supplemental food, not a calorie or water ration.

This entry deliberately covers the ripe fruit, not leaf consumption, drinking extracted sap or treating wounds. It gives no guaranteed calorie yield or serving size.

**Food/preparation checks:** [Nature Glenelg Trust's Portland garden](https://natureglenelg.org.au/current-projects/community-nursery-project/indigenous-display-gardens/portland-native-plant-display-garden/), [Bili Nursery and Landcare](https://westgatebiodiversity.org.au/carpobrotus-rossii-native-pigface/), horticulturist Karen Sutherland's [ABC Organic Gardener article](https://www.organicgardener.com.au/edible-succulents/), and [Njume and colleagues' fruit study](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijfs.14400). The paper reports collection with help from elders of the Martang Djab Wurrung community. Its ethics statement separately says the Warrnambool collections followed the blessing of Indigenous Elders. Neither statement is this project's permission or current cultural authority. The [Natural Resources South East cards](https://cdn.environment.sa.gov.au/landscape/docs/lc/aboriginal-bush-foods-identification-cards-gen.pdf) present their material as Bunganditj knowledge, acknowledge Neville Bonney and Des Hartman, and warn against eating without experienced instruction. We have not copied their pictures, language names or medicinal claims.

### Lookalikes, hybrids and exclusions

Other Carpobrotus species and hybrids can look very similar. Round-leaved Disphyma is also called pigface. A pink flower and a succulent leaf do not settle which one you have.

Do not transfer this entry to **Carpobrotus aff. rossii (N.W. Victoria)**. VicFlora treats that as a separate, critically endangered taxon of uncertain affinity. It is not a permitted gathering substitute. [Separate taxon record](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/d5d1c6a0-3c30-4fce-ba6c-d69033a1dc02).

A complete comparison of these plants, and photographs of demonstrably ripe fruit and the whole mat, remain required. **Entry:** SP-004; conditional working prose only, no independent approval.

## Warrigal greens — Tetragonia tetragonoides

**Status — CONDITIONAL FOOD:** Learn the species and this exact boil-drain-discard process before travel; do not identify or trial it for the first time while stranded.

Also called New Zealand spinach. The accepted Victorian spelling is **tetragonoides**. Some other databases and older sources spell it *tetragonioides*; a spelling match is not an identity check.

### Features to check together

- A low, sprawling plant with branches that can reach about 1 m.
- Thick, soft green leaves about **2–8 cm long and 1–5 cm wide**, broadly triangular or diamond-shaped, on short stalks.
- A finely dotted or slightly blistered-looking surface.
- Small green flowers where the leaf joins the stem. Victorian sources describe **five** flower-like segments; PlantNET records four or five. In this Victorian guide, finding four is a reason to stop and seek expert confirmation, not proof of another species.
- A hard or only slightly fleshy fruit about **1 cm** across, with four angles, wings or short horns.

**Habitat and Victoria:** occasional, mainly near the eastern Victorian coast, growing among rocks or on sandy or silty ground. It is not a statewide fallback. VicFlora records flowering through much of the year.

### Conditional preparation — only after the stop check passes

Use this section only for Warrigal greens that you learned to identify before the emergency and have positively identified from the complete plant—not from this book for the first time. Use only leaves from a lawfully gathered, uncontaminated plant.

1. Remove dirt and unknown plant material. Rinse with drinking-quality water.
2. Use enough drinking-quality water to keep every leaf submerged and allow the water to return quickly to a full boil. No validated field water-to-leaf ratio has yet been established.
3. Put in the leaves. Keep the water boiling for **3–5 minutes**.
4. Drain completely. **Discard all the cooking water**; do not use it as soup, tea or drinking water.
5. Immediately rinse the leaves thoroughly with cold drinking-quality water, then drain them again.
6. If you have never safely eaten correctly identified and correctly prepared Warrigal greens before, do not make an emergency your first tolerance test. If you have eaten them before, keep the amount modest. If symptoms occur, stop eating and call the Poisons Information Centre on **13 11 26**; call **000** for collapse, breathing trouble or serious symptoms.

Boiling lowered soluble oxalate substantially in a published New Zealand-spinach analysis, but did not remove all total oxalate. The exact field batch, leaf age, growing conditions and water ratio will vary. If you lack enough safe water and lawful fuel for boiling and discarding, choose another food.

**Expected taste:** a current NSW government article describes the leaves as slightly bitter and says their salty quality can weaken away from ocean air. This is subjective NSW context, not a Victorian identity test. An unexpected taste is a reason to stop, not a way to decide the species.

**Food value:** the Australian government card gives **61 kJ per 100 g** but does not state whether the sample was raw or cooked. An independent US food dataset gives **59 kJ per 100 g raw** and **51 kJ per 100 g boiled and drained**. The required boiled form is about **12 kcal per 100 g** in that dataset. This is a low-energy vegetable and cannot carry the calorie load of a stranded person.

### Lookalike and stop rule

The old Victorian Resources Online page separated it from bower spinach partly by five flower segments rather than four and by its green flower and drier fruit. PlantNET's four-or-five count means segment number alone is not a dependable separator. If you cannot check flower attachment and mature fruit on the same plant, if you find four segments, or if any feature disagrees, stop. Do not use “succulent on a beach,” a leaf photograph, taste or the presence of one expected feature as identification.

2. Use only an intact fruit at a ripe stage learned beforehand for that positively identified plant. Sources describe ripe fruit as yellow, red, orange or pink. Do not eat green fruit, and never use colour alone to prove identity or ripeness.

4. The small fruit and its central seed are documented as edible. This entry gives no use for the leaves, tea or medicine.

**Expected taste:** two separate public sources describe the fruit as **salty-sweet**. That is an expectation after identification, not an identity test. Spit it out and stop if it is unexpectedly unpleasant, but a salty-sweet taste does not prove the plant safe.

**Food value:** no defensible fresh-fruit kJ or kcal result was found. A 1993 Australian bush-food table records the energy for *E. tomentosa* fruit as **not determined**, although it reports about 65% water. Missing energy cannot be calculated from selected nutrients. The fruit is tiny. Treat it as flavour, not a ration or drinking-water source.

**Food and cultural sources:** the [South Australian government garden fact sheet](https://cdn.environment.sa.gov.au/landscape/docs/mr/Native-food-fact-sheet_ruby-saltbush.pdf) independently supports the edible fruit, seed and salty-sweet description. [DJAARA's DJAKITJ page](https://djakitj.com.au/) identifies Girrkitj as a Dja Dja Wurrung native food, and [Djaara Balaki Wuka](https://imags.com.au/published/Djaara_Balaki_Wuka_Nyerna_Djay_Wurreka/40/) records the name, raw ripe-fruit use, taste and moderation warning on Djandak. DJAARA states that its knowledge remains Dja Dja Wurrung intellectual property and that the resource is not to be republished without consent. Its guide also says it draws on several sources, does not guarantee that every statement is accurate and has not tested the documented traditional uses. It is cultural-use evidence, not toxicology, dose evidence or approval of this page. This personal guide therefore gives a short attributed summary only: it does not copy site locations, a seasonal calendar or another use. **Entry:** SP-010; working only.

## Prickly currant-bush — Coprosma quadrifida

**Status — CONDITIONAL FOOD:** Use only after exact identification and lawful, uncontaminated collection; similar fruit on another plant is not enough.

Museums Victoria's Bunjilaka guide records **Morr** as the Woi wurrung name. This is a bounded museum-recorded attribution, not direct Traditional Owner review or approval of this page. It applies to the named source and Country; it is not a pan-Victorian name.

> **WARNING — SIMILAR COPROSMA FRUIT IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY FOOD**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the Victorian botanical description and documented ripe-fruit use; Moderate for this preparation summary; Insufficient for novice identification from the pictures.** Closely related plants and hybrids can carry similar orange-red fruit. The final plate has not had botanical or food-safety review.
>
> **Wording confidence: 80% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### Features to check together

- An upright, tangled-looking shrub, commonly **2–5 m** high.
- Slender young branches that are hairy and often end in sharp points.
- Small opposite leaves, commonly **5–15 mm long** and **2–5 mm wide**. They are thin and dull rather than thick and mirror-glossy.
- A clear network of veins on the leaf underside, not merely one strong vein.
- Tiny male and female flowers usually on separate plants. Male flowers show hanging anthers; female flowers show two long pale styles.
- Glossy orange to red oval or rounded fruit, **5–8 mm long**, with the remains of the flower forming a small crown at the tip.

**Habitat and Victoria:** widespread and often locally common in moist open forest, streamside scrub, fern gullies and rainforest. VicFlora records flowers from August to January. Museums Victoria records small red fruit in summer; an older ANBG compilation says fruit can be abundant in late summer to autumn. Check the plant, not a calendar promise.

**Botanical check:** [VicFlora species account](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/1b8f3911-2ea5-499d-88af-ffe7fc4dbdb4) and [VicFlora genus account](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/19bf8b14-adce-49ce-b84d-31cca4199f1e).

### Conditional ripe-fruit use

For a lawfully available plant already known with certainty, first confirm that the exact patch's pesticide and herbicide history is known. If it is unknown, stop; appearance, smell and rinsing cannot clear the plant.

1. Trace the fruit to a sharp-ended branch with the matching tiny, thin leaves. Check the vein network underneath a leaf.
2. Select only intact, glossy red fruit at a ripe stage learned beforehand. Leave green, orange, mouldy, damaged, fallen, flood-exposed or visibly fouled fruit.
3. Rinse with drinking-quality water.
4. Eat the soft fruit and spit out its one or two hard seeds. This entry gives no use for the leaf, branch, root or unripe fruit.

**Expected taste:** older sources describe ripe fruit as **sweet and juicy**. No controlled Victorian taste study was found; variation is expected. Taste never confirms the species.

**Food value:** no defensible species-specific fresh energy value was found in the current Australian database or the reproduced 1993 bush-food tables. The fruit is small and the seed takes part of its mass. Do not borrow a value for European currants, gooseberries, another *Coprosma* or “generic berries”. Treat it as refreshment only. ### Lookalikes, hybrids and wildlife

- *Coprosma hirtella* has much larger leaves, commonly 15–50 mm long, and clustered flowers.
- *Coprosma nitida* has stiff, thick, glossy leaves with one main vein rather than the clear underside network described above.
- Introduced karamu, *Coprosma robusta*, has hybridised with *C. quadrifida* on the Mornington Peninsula. Do not transfer this food entry to a hybrid or an uncertain plant.

### Features to check together

- A tree up to about **20 m** in Victoria's native range.
- Red-brown bark, smooth to slightly flaky, with lengthwise cracks on older stems.
- Young twigs often square when cut across.

For a lawfully available tree already known with certainty, first confirm that the exact patch's pesticide and herbicide history is known. If it is unknown, stop; appearance, smell and rinsing cannot clear the tree.

## European blackberry complex — Rubus fruticosus aggregate

**Status — CONDITIONAL FOOD:** Private-site-only boundary. Use only on private land with the owner's current permission and treatment history. Do not eat public-land or roadside fruit.

This is an introduced noxious weed complex, not one reliably identifiable species. Agriculture Victoria says the management name covers at least nine species in Victoria plus hybrids. The three photographs below are source-labelled *Rubus anglocandicans*, apparently the state's most widespread member, but they must not be used to name every blackberry thicket.

> **WARNING — DO NOT EAT ROADSIDE, RESERVE OR OTHER PUBLIC-LAND BLACKBERRIES**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High that the *Rubus fruticosus* aggregate has edible fruit; High that Victorian councils document public-land spraying and advise against eating fruit there; Moderate for using the photographs to recognise the broad complex; Insufficient for proving the exact member or treatment history of a patch.** Council-documented spray programs can overlap fruiting, and signs may be absent, removed or moved. Do not assume washing or cooking makes treated or unknown fruit safe.
>
> **Wording confidence: 89% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### Recognising the broad complex

- A perennial, prickly, scrambling shrub forming dense thickets several metres high.
- Arching or trailing canes that can reach **7 m**, with straight or curved prickles.
- Leaves arranged one after another on the cane and divided into **three or five** oval, toothed leaflets. Some species have a whitish hairy underside.
- White or pink flowers, **2–3 cm** across, with five petals and many stamens.
- Fruit made of many small juicy beads, **1–3 cm** across, changing green to red to black. Victorian fruit develops mainly from late December to April.
- A ripe European blackberry remains attached to its pale central core when picked. Victoria's native brambles have red or orange ripe fruit that comes away from that core.

**Habitat and Victoria:** mainly higher-rainfall parts of Victoria, especially bushland, streams, plantations and grazing land. Exact members can be hard to identify. Use [Agriculture Victoria's aggregate account](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/weeds/weeds-information/blackberry) and the [VicFlora *R. anglocandicans* account](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/390a7b22-b707-45b2-8ac5-6750bfaf4b12) for the limits of these photographs.

### The only conditional use retained

Do not use public-land fruit. Only consider fruit on private land when the owner gives permission **and the person responsible for treatment records confirms that the exact patch has not been chemically treated**. Unknown history means stop. Absence of a sign, chemical smell or damaged leaves is not confirmation.

If those conditions and the whole-plant check are met:

1. Use only sound, fully black fruit still attached to the checked cane. Do not enter a dense thicket or risk an eye injury for food.
2. Reject red, green, mouldy, fallen, animal-fouled, flood-exposed or polluted-site fruit.
3. Wash the fruit with drinking-quality water and eat it raw. Do not use cooking as a way to make a sprayed or uncertain fruit safe.
4. Do not carry fruit or seed to another site, plant it or discard seed elsewhere. Agriculture Victoria states that transporting a declared noxious weed, its seed or any part capable of growing requires a permit.

**Expected taste:** no controlled species-level taste result was found for this difficult aggregate. A familiar ripe blackberry may seem sweet, tart or both, but that general expectation cannot name the member, show that it is ripe or reveal herbicide exposure.

**Food value:** FSANZ gives **192 kJ per 100 g including fibre**, about **46 kcal**, for raw commercial blackberry. Most of that record comes from composite retail samples; their exact taxa or cultivars are not named. It is the best Australian scale comparison found, not a measurement of a Victorian wild thicket. At that proxy value, a weighed 250 g would be about 480 kJ or 114 kcal—around 5.5% of the 8700 kJ food-label reference. Field yield and safety are not promised.

**Food, spray and legal checks:** [NSW Government WeedWise](https://weeds.dpi.nsw.gov.au/Weed/Blackberry) explicitly records edible fruit for the *Rubus fruticosus* species aggregate. [Macedon Ranges Shire](https://www.mrsc.vic.gov.au/Live-Work/Environment/Weeds-and-pest-animals/Common-Weeds) says local control usually occurs December–March, overlapping fruiting, and tells people to avoid roadside, reserve and other public-land fruit because warning signs may be missing. An undated [Yarra Ranges Council notice](https://www.yarraranges.vic.gov.au/EE-Newsletter/Blackberry-Spraying-Underway) gives the same local no-consumption precaution. These are documented council-level warnings, not a current statewide survey. [Agriculture Victoria's weed-permit guidance](https://agriculture.vic.gov.au/biosecurity/protecting-victoria/legislation-policy-and-permits/permits-for-noxious-weeds-in-victoria) states when a permit is required. Nutrition: [FSANZ F001267](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/science-data/food-nutrient-databases/afcd/search/food/F001267).

All members of this European complex are introduced. No Victorian Traditional Owner food attribution was found, so no Aboriginal language name or pre-colonial use is assigned. This says nothing about contemporary Aboriginal experience; it only prevents the book from inventing an attribution. **Entry:** SP-013; public-land use rejected, private-site use conditional.

**Status — CULTURAL-NON-HARVEST:** Learn the plant and its separately attributed cultural records; this edition gives no field-eating instruction.

Older books and image files usually call this plant *Astroloma humifusum*. VicFlora treats that name as a synonym of the accepted name *Styphelia humifusa*. It is not the cultivated cranberry sold as food.

> **WARNING — RECOGNITION AND CULTURAL CONTEXT ONLY**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted species, Victorian features and documented use of the small pulp; Moderate for the source-labelled photographs; Insufficient for a novice to identify and eat a specimen.** A ripe fruit may remain green, related heaths can need flower details to separate, and no modern species-specific calorie, toxicology or safe-serving evidence was located. ### Features to learn together

- A low mat, spreading shrub or occasional upright shrub, up to about **70 cm** high. Its form varies markedly, including within the Grampians and Little Desert.
- Fine hairs on young branchlets.
- Crowded narrow leaves, **5–18 mm** long and **0.6–3 mm** wide. Each has a sharp little tip; the edges have sparse fine teeth or hairs.
- Red tube-shaped flowers, usually single and partly hidden among the leaves. The five spreading lobes are densely bearded on their inner face. That close flower detail matters in the *Styphelia* key.
- Round to oval fruit, only **5–10 mm** long. VicFlora says it is green when ripe but can be tinged with purple or become dark purple.

**Habitat and Victoria:** common and widespread from near-coastal country to inland Victoria, especially in heathy or grassy woodland and open forest. VicFlora records flowers mainly January–July. Other regional sources give broader flowering and fruit periods. The Dja Dja Wurrung seasonal-calendar page mentions ripe fruit in both a spring and an autumn passage. Those records describe local observation; they do not create one statewide picking month.

## River mint — Mentha australis

**Status — CULTURAL-NON-HARVEST:** This page records bounded food and cultural context but supplies no wild tea, medicine, oil or food dose.

River mint is a culturally important Victorian plant and a contemporary culinary herb. This page deliberately separates that record from an untested survival recipe.

> **WARNING — DO NOT IMPROVISE A TEA, MEDICINE, OIL OR FOOD DOSE**

### Recognition features—not an eating key

- A perennial herb that can form a mat, sprawl or stand to about **80 cm**.
- Four-angled stems, sometimes purple-tinged, ranging from almost hairless to bristly.
- Leaves in opposite pairs, **6–60 mm** long and **4–20 mm** wide, with pointed tips. Their edges may be whole or toothed and their hairiness varies.
- White, pink or purple tube-shaped flowers in compact clusters where the leaves meet the stem.
- A strong mint smell when a leaf is rubbed. Smell is common to several mints and must not decide the identity.
- Wet habitat such as stream and swamp edges or clay depressions. An introduced lookalike, pennyroyal, can occupy seasonally waterlogged ground in Victoria too.

### Why this edition withholds food and medicine instructions

FSANZ records river mint as a traditional Australian food. DJAARA's DJAKITJ enterprise separately describes it as a contemporary culinary herb. No suitable fresh-leaf calorie value was located; in ordinary culinary use it is flavouring, not meaningful survival energy.

A 2026 peer-reviewed comparison found that *M. australis* oil yield was consistently below **0.2% of plant mass**, but the relative share of pulegone within the measured volatile fraction varied. One plant cultivated from a Clifton Hill source had a reported relative peak of about **70% pulegone**. Those are not percentages of the leaf and cannot be turned into a tea strength or safe serving. The result is a reason to pause, not a claim that a normal sprig causes poisoning.

## Sweet quandong — Santalum acuminatum

**Status — CONDITIONAL FOOD:** Only the ripe outer flesh is considered, after exact identification and lawful, uncontaminated collection. Never eat the kernel using this guide.

This is a northern and north-western Victorian plant, not a general statewide fallback. It is widespread in that part of Victoria but VicFlora describes it as generally uncommon.

> **WARNING — RIPE OUTER FLESH ONLY; DO NOT EAT THE KERNEL**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted species, edible ripe flesh and exact FSANZ food record; Moderate for this personal-use preparation summary and the source-labelled images; Insufficient for identifying your specimen without a botanist-reviewed comparison.** VicFlora calls the kernel edible, but a separate bush-food safety review found unusual kernel chemistry and no defensible field dose. This guide therefore excludes every quandong kernel. Red fruit alone is not an identification.
>
> **Wording confidence: 81% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### Features to check together

- An upright shrub or small tree to about **6 m**, often producing new stems from its roots.
- Hairless, spreading to drooping young branches with visible lengthwise angles or ridges.
- Opposite narrow leaves, usually **3–9 cm** long and **3–15 mm** wide, often curved like a sickle. Both surfaces are about the same yellowish-green colour.
- Many small green-to-orange four-part flowers in branched clusters at the ends of stems.
- Round ripe fruit **15–25 mm** across, normally bright, shiny red and rarely yellow.
- A large, very hard stone with deep pits. The edible flesh around it may be only **3–5 mm** thick.

**Habitat and season:** woodland in northern and north-western Victoria. VicFlora records flowering mainly October–May but does not give a single Victorian fruiting period. Rain, locality and individual trees vary. Do not leave a known position on the assumption that fruit will be present.

### Bitter quandong: a named Victorian comparison

Victoria also has bitter quandong, *Santalum murrayanum*. VicFlora says its fruit flesh is exceedingly bitter and inedible. Its leaves are usually much narrower and shorter—mostly **1.5–3.5 cm** by **1.5–4 mm**—and can occur in whorls of three. Authoritative descriptions disagree about fruit colour: VicFlora says green to brownish, while a Lucid fact sheet says red to brownish red. More useful specialist features include the narrower leaves and the flower parts falling away to leave a circular scar at the top of the fruit; in sweet quandong those parts persist. The bitter-quandong stone is described as wrinkled or only slightly pitted rather than deeply pitted. These differences require the whole plant and expert review; do not use taste or fruit colour to settle them.

### Conditional ripe-flesh use

Only after the tree is already known with certainty, collection is lawful, the site is uncontaminated and the fruit can be traced to the checked branch:

1. Select intact, sound, bright red ripe fruit. This edition excludes the rare yellow form because the lookalike gate is incomplete.
2. Reject mouldy, damaged, animal-fouled, flood-exposed, fallen or chemically treated fruit. Unknown pesticide or herbicide history means stop.
3. Rinse with drinking-quality water. Split the fruit with clean fingers or a clean knife.
4. Remove the entire hard stone. Eat only the surrounding soft outer fruit tissue. Do not crack, roast, grind or eat the kernel inside the stone.

**Expected taste:** published descriptions range from sweet to tart, tangy, somewhat astringent and not especially sweet. FSANZ records **8.1 g total sugars per 100 g** in its analysed commercial flesh. Tree, ripeness and processing matter. Strong bitterness is a stop signal after an already identified fruit has been opened; it is never a taste test for identity.

**Food value:** current FSANZ data give **213 kJ per 100 g including fibre**, about **51 kcal**, for commercially supplied frozen fruit flesh with kernels removed. The database labels the source as analysed; its energy figure is calculated from measured nutrient components. It is not a measurement of whole fruit, stones or a Victorian wild tree. At that exact value, 250 g of weighed edible flesh would be about **533 kJ or 127 kcal**—about **6.1%** of the 8700 kJ food-label reference. That is arithmetic, not a serving recommendation. Gathering 250 g of edible tissue may require many fruits because most of each fruit is stone.

An older Australian table gives **335 kJ per 100 g** as an average of four raw fruit samples. The sample origin is not stated, and the result differs materially from the current commercial frozen record. The two values are kept separate rather than averaged. No safe serving, allergy-free claim, wild yield or personal energy requirement has been established.

**Why the kernel is excluded:** a 2001 safety review says kernels can be rich in oil and protein but reports that **32–46% of their fatty acids** in five *Santalum* species was the unusual santalbic acid. Rats fed an extreme diet in which kernels supplied half of absorbed energy showed liver biochemical and metabolic effects. The review also found a strongly variable unpleasant compound that roasting did not remove. This does not prove that one kernel poisons a person. It does mean this guide has no evidence-based field dose or processing method and will not turn a calorie-dense kernel into advice.

**Cultural attribution gap:** sweet quandong is culturally important in many parts of Australia, but no Victoria-specific Traditional Owner account suitable for reuse was located in this pass. The guide therefore assigns no Victorian Aboriginal name and does not import a method from another Country. This is an explicit research gap, not a denial of cultural knowledge.

## Kangaroo apples — recognise and avoid

**Status — DO NOT EAT:** Do not taste kangaroo-apple fruit at any stage using this guide.

Victoria has two widespread native plants commonly called kangaroo apple: *Solanum aviculare* and *Solanum laciniatum*. Historical food records exist, but this personal guide does not convert them into emergency eating advice.

> **WARNING — DO NOT EAT OR TASTE KANGAROO APPLE**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted Victorian species, their described mature fruit colours, the presence of solasodine-related compounds and their listing in the Food Standards Code; Moderate for the source-labelled photographs; Insufficient for a safe wild serving, preparation, novice identification or harmless taste test.** Even ripe fruit has been reported to retain some solasodine. No evidence located for this edition shows what amount, if any, would be safe for a stranded person.
>
> **Wording confidence: 94% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

The Food Standards Code lists *S. aviculare* and *S. laciniatum* in Schedule 23 as prohibited plants for food that is sold, processed or handled for sale, or imported. That commercial rule is not by itself a diagnosis of poisoning from one fruit. Together with unresolved alkaloid dose, lookalikes and identification difficulty, however, it is a strong reason not to issue a personal field-eating recipe.

**Status — DO NOT EAT:** Use this page for education only; the guide cannot reliably separate every specimen from the endangered relative.

This slender climber is common through much of Victoria. Its fruit has a well-documented food history, but this page is deliberately a recognition lesson rather than an eating instruction.

### Features to learn together

- A slender twining or scrambling plant that uses other vegetation for support. It may climb to about **2 m**, but the pictures below do not show a complete whole-plant habit.

### Why the endangered relative stops an eating instruction

The current narrow species *Billardiera scandens* is listed Endangered in Victoria. Compared with *B. mutabilis*, it is usually more shrub-like, has more strongly rolled-under leaf edges, shorter and stouter flower or fruit stalks, and fruit covered in dense hairs. Those are useful study points, not a novice decision key. VicFlora says separation can still be difficult and records intermediate plants around Beechworth, Lake Hume and the Snowy River. Do not collect either species when there is doubt. [VicFlora *B. scandens*](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/6c325a2f-c22e-4773-83ae-6c5a2a810385); [Victorian conservation assessment](https://bio-prd-naturekit-public-data.s3.ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/species_assessments/Billardiera_scandens_504290.pdf).

### Food, expected taste and energy limits

VicFlora describes *B. mutabilis* fruit as edible. A Surf Coast page says the ripe fruit becomes translucent and very sticky. The same page attributes a stewed-apple taste description to information supplied by the then-named Wathaurung Aboriginal Corporation. **That expected taste is context only after expert identification. It is not an identification test.** Do not taste an unknown berry.

No reliable species-specific result was found for fresh kilojoules, calories, edible-pulp weight, allergens, toxicology or a safe amount. The fruit is small and contains many seeds. Calling it a useful calorie source would be guesswork. Older instructions filed under the broad name *B. scandens* may predate the modern species split and are not transferred here.

## Kangaroo grass — Themeda triandra

**Status — CULTURAL-NON-HARVEST:** The page records botanical and cultural context, not a seed-harvest or flour-making procedure.

Kangaroo grass is one of Victoria's most widespread native grasses. Its seed-food history matters, but a stranded person should not mistake cultural importance for an easy handful of emergency grain.

> **WARNING — EDUCATION ONLY; DO NOT HARVEST, TASTE OR GRIND WILD SEED FROM THIS PAGE**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted species, the cited Victorian features and the documented history of grinding seed into flour; Moderate for the source-labelled photographs; Insufficient for novice grass identification, a safe raw or cooked serving, a field calorie return or sustainable wild harvest.** Only small amounts ripen together, ripe seed falls quickly, and no specialist has approved an emergency preparation. This page gives no harvest or recipe.
>
> **Wording confidence: 91% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### Features to learn together

- A tufted perennial grass, often **50–100 cm** tall. Green leaves and stems may turn reddish, copper or purple as flowering develops or conditions change.
- Narrow leaf blades to about **50 cm long** and **5 mm wide**.
- A drooping or one-sided, many-branched flowering head about **10–25 cm long**.
- Clusters containing small spikelets. The fertile spikelet has silky brown hairs and a strong purple-brown awn—the long bristle—about **4–8 cm** long and bent twice when mature.
- Colour alone proves nothing. Age, fire, rain, grazing and locality all change the plant's appearance.

**Habitat and season:** widespread in Victorian grassland and woodland, but uncommon in the Otways and subalps and generally absent from permanently wet, very dry, salty or heavily shaded ground. VicFlora records flowering through much of the year, mainly October–March.

### Food work, taste and calorie return

Historical and contemporary sources record kangaroo-grass seed being ground into flour and baked. That does not make it a quick survival snack. The Australian National Botanic Gardens notes that only small amounts ripen at once and seed falls rapidly. Modern agronomy also reports uneven ripening, seed shattering, variable seed quality, low yield and difficult harvesting and processing.

No defensible wild-serving kilojoule or calorie value is available. A CSIRO summary of a Wadawurrung-led research partnership says analysed kangaroo-grass grain had promising protein and carbohydrate results compared with whole wheat, but it does not publish the numerical energy result. A 2023 study deliberately hid the mapping between native species and its nutrition table; its kangaroo-grass material was a composite because there was too little seed. Those numbers cannot be reverse-engineered and assigned to this plant. Livestock-feed values are not human grain nutrition.

**Expected taste:** no reliable, species-specific, simply prepared wild-grain taste benchmark was found. This guide will not invent one. Raw-snacking advice is withheld because the food record centres on processed seed and no field-safety study establishes a raw amount. Even if a future cultivated-grain value proves useful, the energy spent searching, collecting, separating, grinding and cooking still matters.

## Coast beard-heath — Leucopogon parviflorus

**Status — DO NOT EAT:** This education page supplies no eating or harvesting instruction and cannot exclude every similar white-fruited heath.

This coastal shrub bears tiny white fruit with a documented food history. The fruit is too small and the evidence too limited to promise meaningful emergency energy.

> **WARNING — EDUCATION ONLY; PROTECTED-FLORA AND LOOKALIKE CHECKS ARE INCOMPLETE**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted species, Victorian coastal range, cited features and documented ripe-flesh food use; Moderate for the source-labelled photographs; Insufficient for novice identification, a safe serving, a reliable fresh-calorie value or public-land collection.** Victoria has several small white-fruited *Leucopogon* species. Native Ericaceae are declared restricted-use protected flora. No eating or harvesting instruction is given.
>
> **Wording confidence: 92% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### Features to learn together

- An upright shrub or small tree, usually **1–5 m** high. On exposed dunes it may be much lower and wind-shaped.
- Narrow, smooth leaves about **15–35 mm long** and **3–7 mm wide**, often paler or bluish-green underneath. The thickened tip is channelled above.
- Short flower spikes near the ends of branches, each holding about **6–13 small white flowers**.
- Dense white hairs inside the flower tube and on the lobes—the “beard”.
- Round fleshy fruit about **4.5–5 mm** across, white when ripe, with a large hard stone.

**Habitat and season:** common on dunes and sandstone or limestone slopes along almost the whole Victorian coast, usually close to the sea. VicFlora records flowering mainly September–November. A South Australian source gives fruit September–February; this is not presented as a verified statewide Victorian calendar.

### Food, expected taste and the missing calorie answer

Several sources document people eating the correctly identified ripe white flesh raw. Published descriptions include sweet, juicy, mildly acidic and grape-like. Those words describe an expected result **after** expert identification; they are not a taste test. This edition gives no eating step and makes no claim about the hard stone.

A peer-reviewed Victorian study collected about **300 g** of berries from **one plant** near Warrnambool in February 2017 and could not obtain enough fruit for another collection in 2019. The paper does not provide a direct fresh edible-flesh energy result, and it is unclear how the hard stone affected the figures. The fruit's small size, large stone and the research team's difficulty collecting it mean it should not be treated as dependable calories. A 100 g whole-fruit pile would not equal 100 g edible flesh. No modern human allergen, toxicology or safe-serving study was located.

Cumbungi is conspicuous in many Victorian wetlands, but a familiar outline is not a species identification. Victoria has two native species, *Typha orientalis* and *T. domingensis*, and the naturalised *T. latifolia*.

### What the photographs can teach

- Cumbungi is a robust perennial wetland plant with upright shoots from branching underground stems.
- Long, flat, parallel-veined leaves rise mainly from the base.
- The flowerhead has a slender male section above a thicker female section.

## Beaded glasswort or samphire — Salicornia quinqueflora

**Status — CULTURAL-NON-HARVEST:** Learn the plant and the Krauatungalung Country context; this page cannot clear identity, contamination, harvest or eating.

The word **samphire** is used for several different succulent saltmarsh plants. This page concerns *Salicornia quinqueflora*, formerly called *Sarcocornia quinqueflora*. VicFlora accepts two subspecies in Victoria. The widespread one is subsp. *quinqueflora*; the other is not covered by this page.

### What the pictures can show

VicFlora describes a native perennial usually no more than about **30 cm high**. It may spread along the ground or form short upright tufts. The green branchlets are fleshy and divided into obvious joints. Individual fleshy sections are usually about **5–20 mm long**. The terminal flower or fruit spikes are about **1–5 cm long**. The actual flowers are tiny and partly hidden in the fleshy spike.

It occurs in many Victorian districts, especially in saline or brackish coastal and estuarine ground, and sometimes around inland salt lakes. Habitat supports an identification; it does not prove one.

### Why a salty-looking shoot is not enough

Victoria also has *Salicornia blackiana* and numerous *Tecticornia* species. The plants can be difficult to separate without mature flowers, seeds and a botanical key. Seven Victorian *Tecticornia* taxa are listed Endangered under the current Flora and Fauna Guarantee threatened list. Suitable coastal habitat can also form part of the federally listed **Subtropical and Temperate Coastal Saltmarsh** ecological community, and saltmarsh is important wildlife habitat.

Stop if any of these are true:

- you know only the common name “samphire”;
- the patch is near a drain, road, mine, industrial land, sewage influence, treated reserve or polluted sediment;
- you cannot separate the plant from every similar Victorian species;
- you would have to damage a saltmarsh patch or collect where plants are protected;
- your reason for continuing is that a restaurant, shop or online post sells “samphire”.

Commercial food does not establish the identity or contaminant level of a wild plant.

Passing this stop list would still not identify a plant or permit harvesting or eating.

Lower-level culinary sources describe prepared young samphire shoots as salty and sometimes slightly peppery. That is a report about food already selected for cooking. **Do not taste a wild plant to check its identity.** A salty taste would not separate the species or reveal metals and other contaminants.

### Features an expert would check together

## Common nardoo — Marsilea drummondii

**Status — DO NOT EAT:** Do not try to detoxify or prepare nardoo using this guide.

Nardoo can look abundant after floodwater recedes. That abundance is dangerous if it suggests an easy flour. *Marsilea drummondii* contains **thiaminase**, an enzyme that destroys vitamin B1. Poorly processed nardoo can cause serious deficiency. This guide will not shorten skilled knowledge into an improvised “detox” recipe.

> **DANGER — DO NOT EAT OR TRY TO DETOX NARDOO FROM THIS GUIDE**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted species, thiaminase hazard, four Victorian *Marsilea* species and the separately attributed cultural records; Moderate for the source-labelled photographs; Insufficient for identifying your specimen or for any novice processing method.** Grinding, roasting, boiling or copying one historical step is not assumed to make it safe. Use this page to recognise and avoid a dangerous shortcut.
>
> **Wording confidence: 96% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### What it looks like—and why leaves are not enough

Common nardoo is a native aquatic fern, not a true clover. Each frond ends in four broadly wedge-shaped leaflets arranged as two opposite pairs. The stalk is usually about **3–30 cm long**. On drying mud, hard brown, hairy spore cases called **sporocarps** may appear. VicFlora describes them as about **6–9 mm** across, with their unbranched stalks usually much longer than the sporocarp.

Leaf size, scalloping and hairiness vary with growth conditions. Victoria has four *Marsilea* species, and sterile plants may not be identifiable with confidence. The shape, teeth and stalk of the mature sporocarp are important expert features.

**Status — DO NOT EAT:** Recognition only; the field identity, safe serving and full African-boxthorn comparison are incomplete.

### Features to compare together

VicFlora describes a rigid, tangled, sometimes spine-ended shrub up to about **2 m high**. Young branchlets and both sides of the leaves are covered with close-lying hairs. ### Food record, taste and energy

The Australian National Botanic Gardens records white berries eaten raw and describes them as juicy, pleasantly flavoured and a little sour. Museums Victoria's Bunjilaka guide separately says the cream-coloured berries can be eaten raw. ### Features an expert would check

VicFlora describes a grey-green shrub up to about **3 m high**. Male and female flowers occur on separate plants. ### Features an expert would check together

VicFlora describes a tufted plant up to about **1.2 m high**. The small tubers can be up to about **2 cm** long and sit **3–5 cm** out from the rootstock. The plant base has persistent old leaf fibres. Leaves are narrow and flat or slightly channelled, usually **10–60 cm** long and **1–10 mm** wide.

## Pale vanilla-lily — Arthropodium milleflorum

**Status — CULTURAL-NON-HARVEST:** This page preserves bounded food history and named cultural context; it gives no wild digging or eating method.

Pale vanilla-lily is a tall, pale-flowered relative of chocolate lily. Its roots have a documented food history, but an old low-energy figure, destructive digging and difficult relatives make it a poor emergency target.

> **WARNING — DO NOT DIG OR EAT WILD PALE VANILLA-LILY FROM THIS PAGE**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for the accepted species, Victorian features, broad distribution and the existence of threatened or unresolved Victorian relatives; Moderate for the three source-labelled pictures and broad historical food record; Low for applying the old raw-root energy row to a found Victorian plant; Insufficient for a novice identity, safe preparation, serving, expected taste, lawful sustainable harvest or underground-part comparison.** “Vanilla” describes the flower scent, not the taste or safety of the root.
>
> **Wording confidence: 94% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

### Features an expert would check together

VicFlora describes a tufted perennial up to about **1.3 m** high. Tubers are usually **2–3 cm** long and sit **2–8 cm** from the rootstock. Basal leaves are flat or spreading, sometimes blue-grey, and can reach about **40 cm** long and **25 mm** wide.

The flowering stem branches and carries groups of **2–9** flowers at a node. Flowers may be white, pale mauve or pale pink. The fine hairs on the pollen-bearing stalks are white near their base and purple closer to the purple anther. These tiny features matter. Flowering is mainly **November to February**.

In Victoria it is chiefly a montane and subalpine plant along the Great Divide, with scattered records in cool lowlands. Habitat is supportive, not diagnostic.

Victoria also has an Endangered unnamed entity called ***Arthropodium* sp. 1** and another unresolved entity called ***Arthropodium* sp. 2**. Flower size, arrangement, anthers and other fine characters help separate them. A pale many-flowered lily is not enough.

### Food value, taste and practical return

An older Australian food table reports **124 kJ, about 30 kcal, per 100 g raw root** under “Vanilla Lily”. The reproduced table does not establish the specimen's Country, current taxon, sample count, season, trimming, storage or safe preparation. The number is historical scale only.

The old value indicates a low-energy food, not a serving or likely field yield. Finding, correctly identifying and digging a useful mass would destroy plants, consume time and energy, and risk threatened lookalikes for a small return.

No reliable expected-taste benchmark suitable for this guide was found. Do not assume the root tastes like vanilla, and do not taste it to check the name.

## Murnong: important knowledge, not a wild emergency pantry

**Status — CULTURAL-NON-HARVEST:** This page preserves separately governed records and the taxonomic problem; it gives no wild identification, digging or preparation method.

Current Royal Botanic Gardens Victoria work applies the name **Microseris walteri** to the plump-tubered murnong most securely associated with food use. Similar *Microseris* species can require tubers or seeds to separate. **Microseris scapigera is critically endangered in Victoria.** An image search found photographs whose “walteri” titles conflicted with their database records for “lanceolata”, so no identification photograph is shown here.

> **WARNING — DO NOT DIG WILD MURNONG FROM THIS PAGE**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High for murnong's documented importance on separately named Countries and for the modern taxonomic problem; Insufficient for a novice wild identification, lawful harvest, energy value or preparation instruction.** Digging removes the edible tuber and usually the plant. No wild-harvest procedure is given.
>
> **Wording confidence: 95% (subjective).** This rates this warning against the cited sources—not identity, safety, technique success or survival.

The record is not one generic “Aboriginal use”:

- **Wadawurrung Country:** Wadawurrung Traditional Owners Aboriginal Corporation's 2020 *Paleert Tjaara Dja* Country Plan records murnong as once plentiful on the volcanic grasslands and places its renewal within Wadawurrung-led care for Country.
- **Gunditjmara Country:** GMTOAC's 2013 Budj Bim fact sheet identifies Yam Daisy as *Microseris* species and records **Muurang** in Dhauwurd Wurrung. The source's broader taxonomy is preserved rather than silently replaced with one modern species.
- **Taungurung Country:** the 2016 *Taungurung buk dadbagi* Country Plan describes murnong as a staple and important carbohydrate and records its severe decline after introduced grazing animals.
- **Dja Dja Wurrung Country / Djandak:** DJAARA's 2024–25 Annual Report describes the Women's Knowledge Group's “Right Plant, Right Way” work to return murna or yam daisies through seed rescue and research partnership.
- **Wurundjeri Woiwurrung Country:** the Wurundjeri Woi Wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation's 2021 *biik wurrdha* overview records likely murnong cultivation areas in its Jacksons Creek study area and their depletion after grazing began.

These are separately governed records, not interchangeable proof of a statewide technique. They show why the simple survival-book idea “find a daisy and dig a tuber” is both unsafe and disrespectful. Murnong was part of active knowledge, seasonal practice and managed Country. Its former role as a staple does not prove that present wild patches can sustain one stranded person.

No secure modern species-specific field energy figure was found for *M. walteri*. Older calorie claims use broad or changed taxonomy. Do not borrow the measured value for the critically endangered *M. scapigera*.

## Why there is no generic wattleseed recipe

**Status — DO NOT EAT:** This guide supplies no wild wattle identification, harvest or processing method. Use commercially prepared food.

“Wattle” means a large genus, not one food. VicFlora records many Victorian *Acacia* taxa. A seed being dry, hard or carried in a pod does not make its species edible or its preparation safe.

The calorie attraction is real but easy to misuse. One study of precisely identified **Acacia longifolia subsp. sophorae** seed found about **1485 kJ (355 kcal) per 100 g** in raw, low-moisture ground seed. VicFlora lists the study's name *Acacia sophorae* as a synonym of that accepted Victorian name. The number is calculated dry-seed energy, not a safe ready-to-eat field value. Later research found substances that can interfere with digestion; one measured effect was strong inhibition of trypsin, an enzyme that helps digest protein. Processing changed those substances, and commercial roasting conditions varied. A copied “roast for a few minutes” rule would not be a validated safety method.

Traditional Owner publications may refer to wattle seed on a named Country without naming a botanical species. That cultural record does not authorise this book to guess the species or detach a preparation method. This edition therefore gives no wild wattle identification, harvesting or cooking steps. Use commercially prepared food. Any other use requires exact botanical identification and validated processing advice. If the advice comes from cultural knowledge, it also requires consent from the relevant Traditional Owner authority.

**Evidence:** [VicFlora's exact coast-wattle account and synonyms](https://vicflora.rbg.vic.gov.au/flora/taxon/032751af-9771-46f8-99f0-dd6da352096f), [Shelat and colleagues' 2019 species-specific nutrition study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6836056/) and [Adiamo and colleagues' food-safety study](https://doi.org/10.1111/ijfs.16572). **Entry:** SP-009; rejected as novice field-food instruction.

## Traditional Owner knowledge, Country and permissions

The Federation of Victorian Traditional Owner Corporations' 2021 *Victorian Traditional Owner Native Foods and Botanicals Strategy* states that Traditional Owners hold rights and interests in biocultural species—plants and animals whose biology, care, use and cultural knowledge are connected—and in the associated knowledge and practices. It says the native-food sector's cultural and intellectual property belongs to Traditional Owners. Access and use should be led by Traditional Owners, based on culturally appropriate consent and fair benefit.

Traditional Owners are the authority for their own cultural and ecological knowledge and its relationship to Country. That authority deserves attention, respect and precise attribution. It does not turn one community's knowledge into a generic Victorian rule, and it does not replace the separate botanical, medical, legal or food-safety evidence needed for a survival instruction.

That principle changes how this guide is written:

- knowledge is named by Country and source rather than labelled simply “Aboriginal”;
- public access to a Country Plan is treated as permission to read and cite it, not permission to copy, adapt, illustrate or turn it into a generic recipe;
- historical practice, living practice and contemporary restoration are kept separate;
- Traditional Owner collection rights and cultural authority are not transferred to the reader;
- botanical verification and, for food, toxicology or food-safety review are still required, because cultural authority, plant taxonomy and individual medical safety answer different questions;
- actionable medicine, cultural fire, water extraction, traps and detailed harvesting are excluded unless the relevant Traditional Owner organisation actively co-designs and approves the exact use and the applicable legal, ecological and medical or safety reviews are also complete. A brief, source-bound historical or contemporary context may be noted without reproducing a method, dose or recipe.
