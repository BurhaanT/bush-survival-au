# How to use this guide

## Read the action first

Each practical section begins with the situation it addresses. A warning appears before advice whose limits matter to your decision. Numbered steps explain the order. A short check tells you whether the action appears to be helping and when to seek more help.

You do not need to read the whole book in order. Use the contents or the problem table at the front. If someone is critically ill, go straight to [First aid](06-first-aid-and-evacuation.md).

## What the warning boxes mean

> **WARNING — SPECIALIST REVIEW OUTSTANDING**
>
> **Evidence confidence: High / Moderate / Low / Insufficient**, followed by the reason.
>
> **What remains uncertain:** the specific wording, technique, equipment or situation that has not been settled.
>
> **Practical limit:** what not to assume, and what to do instead where a supported alternative exists.

**High** means the stated core advice agrees with current authoritative guidance and corroborating sources. It does not mean that every part is backed by strong clinical trials, that our wording has been independently reviewed, or that the technique will succeed.

**Moderate** means the general principle is supported but its application here involves a stated judgement or an unresolved limitation. Read that limitation before acting.

**Low** means the evidence is indirect, old, incomplete or poorly transferable. It may explain why an idea was considered, but it is not enough to support a consequential action.

**Insufficient** means the evidence cannot support a dependable instruction for an untrained reader. The book explains the problem and the supported alternative; it does not print a speculative procedure beneath a warning.

These labels describe the evidence for the words, not the chance of surviving. For consequential working material that still needs authority review, this personal edition also gives the founder's requested **editorial percentage estimate of wording accuracy** when the source comparison provides a reasonable basis. That number is subjective and uncalibrated. It is never the chance that an action is safe, that a treatment works, that a plant is identified correctly or that someone survives. When a real-world outcome cannot be estimated, the guide says it is unknown rather than inventing a success rate.

The working scale is deliberately conservative: **95%** is reserved for wording that closely follows current primary guidance and has a meaningful corroborating check; **80–90%** means the main limits agree across sources but this guide still adapts or combines them; **70–79%** means the parts are supported but the exact sequence or stressed-reader use is untested. Below that, the guide should normally withhold the action and estimate confidence in the warning or prohibition instead. These bands organise editorial review; they are not measurements.

## Waiting for review is not the same as evidence of error

A section may closely follow established first aid yet still need a clinician to check the exact text. Another may contain a genuine disagreement between sources. The boxes distinguish these cases. They should not leave you guessing whether a routine emergency call is doubtful or a proposed bushcraft method is unproven.

Conversely, finding several websites that repeat one source is not several independent checks. Source notes identify the main guidance and link to the underlying research. They are there for review before a trip, not as required internet reading during an emergency.

## The order changes with the threat

The book uses this organising question: **What can cause serious harm before I can deal with the other problems?**

A person who is not breathing normally needs immediate first aid. A person standing in the path of rising water needs to escape that immediate exposure. A wet group facing a cold night needs protection before food gathering. Once immediate threats are being managed, communication, staying findable, water and ongoing care continue together.

This is an editorial framework for using the linked chapters, not a fixed timetable or a “rule of threes”. There is no countdown that makes it safe to postpone care.

## With and without a first-aid kit

Every medical section separates shared actions from equipment choices.

- **With a first-aid kit** names what is actually needed.
- **Without a first-aid kit** covers a missing kit or missing supplies.

The two paths are not equivalent. Clean cloth can help apply pressure to a bleeding wound; it cannot replace adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Ordinary materials are included only where the treatment principle supports them.

## Follow live directions

When emergency services can communicate with you, tell them your actual conditions and follow their directions. Repeat an important instruction back if it is unclear. Report any change in the person's condition, your location or the danger around you.

The book's later sections use the same shared first-aid and stay-or-move pages, so a short reference does not create a second, contradictory procedure.
